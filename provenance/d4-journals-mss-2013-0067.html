The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite set of ballot types \(\mathcal T\), a rational population vector \(\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal T)\), a positional scoring rule with the paper’s tie-breaking and manipulation assumptions, and the induced region \(\mathcal W\subseteq\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\) of manipulation-winning coalition masses, compute \(\theta=\inf\{u\in[0,1]:u\mu\in\mathcal W\}\). This is the limiting fraction of the population that must be queried, with \(1-\theta\) giving the continuous limit of the query-based measure.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m\geq2\) candidates. A society is given by its support: a list \(\mathcal T\) of \(\tau\) strict complete rankings with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number \(m!\) of possible rankings. The input also includes a rational positional score vector \(1=s_1\geq\cdots\geq s_m=0\); \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Scores are aggregated by mass, and the outcome lottery is uniform over all candidates attaining the maximum score. Let \(p\) be the sincere outcome lottery.
A manipulating subcoalition has real masses \(c_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t c_t>0\). Each participating type may divide its mass arbitrarily among any strict complete reported rankings, including its sincere ranking; all remaining mass votes sincerely.
Its resulting lottery \(p'\) must satisfy, for every \(t\) with \(c_t>0\) and every \(k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\), \(\sum_{a\in\operatorname{Top}_k(t)}p'(a)\geq\sum_{a\in\operatorname{Top}_k(t)}p(a)\), with a strict inequality for at least one \(k\) for each such \(t\).
Define \(\mathcal W\subseteq\prod_{t\in\mathcal T}[0,\mu_t]\) by declaring \(x\in\mathcal W\) exactly when some manipulating subcoalition satisfies \(c_t\leq x_t\) for every \(t\). Define \(\theta=\inf\{u\in[0,1]:u\mu\in\mathcal W\}\), taking \(\theta=1\) when this set is empty; the associated continuous query measure is \(1-\theta\).

Can \(\theta\) be computed exactly in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this computation NP-hard?

For finite monotone simple games, the rescaled query index satisfies \(\mathbb E[F(n,Q)]=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{F(n,k)-F(n,k+1)}{\binom nk}|W_k|\): \(Q\) is the first winning query count, or \(n+1\) if none exists, \(W_k\) comprises winning coalitions of size \(k\), and \(F(n,\cdot)\) decreases from \(F(n,0)=1\) to \(F(n,n+1)=0\). This identity asserts no computational complexity bound. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1202.1882v2)

Status and new results

The unrestricted complexity question remains unresolved in this write-up. I prove three additional results:

  • If the sincere winner is unique, $\theta$ is exactly computable in polynomial time for every positional score vector.
  • For tied sincere outcomes, let $b$ be the number of sincere winners and $\kappa$ the number of score entries equal to $1$. Either a readily checked condition gives $\theta=0$, or every manipulation has at most $b\kappa$ final winners. Consequently, exact computation is polynomial-time for fixed $b$ and $\kappa$.
  • If $s_1>s_2$ and there are exactly two sincere winners, then $\theta\in\{0,1\}$, with an explicit polynomial-time criterion.

The main new ingredient is a perturbation that replaces a manipulation of a unique sincere winner, at any budget below $1$, by a nearby manipulation with a single final winner.

1. Definitions and an exact formulation for a fixed winner set

Let $\mathcal A$ be the candidate set. For each type $t$, let $r_t(a)$ denote the position of candidate $a$ in its sincere ranking. Define sincere scores by
$$ A_a=\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t s_{r_t(a)}. $$
Let
$$ H=\max_{a\in\mathcal A}A_a, \qquad \mathcal B=\{a:A_a=H\}, \qquad b=|\mathcal B|. $$
Thus the sincere lottery is uniform on $\mathcal B$.

A manipulation has *budget at most $u$* when its participating masses satisfy $c_t\leq u\mu_t$ for every type. This is exactly the condition used in the definition of $\theta$.

1.1 Eligibility for a proposed outcome

Fix a nonempty set $W\neq\mathcal B$ and write $w=|W|$. Define
$$ E_W= \left\{ t: b\,|W\cap\operatorname{Top}_k(t)| \geq w\,|\mathcal B\cap\operatorname{Top}_k(t)| \text{ for every }k \right\}. $$
These inequalities express stochastic dominance of the uniform lottery on $W$ over the uniform lottery on $\mathcal B$.

Every type in $E_W$ also has at least one strict inequality. Indeed, equality at every prefix would, by subtracting consecutive equalities, imply equality of the probability assigned to every candidate. The two uniform lotteries would then have the same support, contradicting $W\neq\mathcal B$.

Consequently, a manipulation producing exactly $W$ may involve precisely types from $E_W$, subject to the score constraints.

1.2 Aggregate reports without enumerating rankings

Put
$$ C=\sum_t c_t. $$
Introduce variables $Y_{aj}$ for the participating mass reporting candidate $a$ in position $j$. Their constraints are
$$ Y_{aj}\geq0, \qquad \sum_{j=1}^mY_{aj}=C\quad(a\in\mathcal A), \qquad \sum_{a\in\mathcal A}Y_{aj}=C\quad(1\leq j\leq m). $$

These constraints characterize exactly the aggregate reports allowed by arbitrary splitting.

Necessity follows because every reported ranking uses every candidate and every position once. For sufficiency, suppose $C>0$. Consider the bipartite graph of positive entries of $Y$. If $I$ is a set of candidate rows and $N(I)$ its neighboring position columns, then
$$ |I|C = \sum_{a\in I}\sum_jY_{aj} \leq \sum_{j\in N(I)}\sum_aY_{aj} = |N(I)|C. $$
Thus Hall’s condition holds, and the graph has a perfect matching.

Subtract the smallest entry on that matching from every matched entry. The residual matrix remains nonnegative, with equal row and column sums, and at least one positive entry disappears. Repeating this operation terminates after at most $m^2$ steps and gives
$$ Y=\sum_\ell\alpha_\ell P_\ell, \qquad \alpha_\ell>0, \qquad \sum_\ell\alpha_\ell=C, $$
where each $P_\ell$ is the permutation matrix of a strict complete ranking.

Each participating type can report ranking $P_\ell$ on fraction $\alpha_\ell/C$ of its participating mass. Summing these reports realizes $Y$. If $C=0$, the constraints force $Y=0$.

The resulting candidate scores are therefore
$$ q_a = A_a-\sum_t c_t s_{r_t(a)} +\sum_{j=1}^m s_jY_{aj}. $$

1.3 Strict feasibility and the infimum

Let $P_W$ be the following closed linear program, with objective to minimize $u$:
$$ 0\leq u\leq1, \qquad 0\leq c_t\leq u\mu_t, \qquad c_t=0\quad(t\notin E_W), $$
together with the aggregate-report constraints and
$$ 0\leq M\leq1, \qquad q_a=M\quad(a\in W), \qquad q_a\leq M\quad(a\notin W). $$

To test whether a genuine manipulation produces exactly $W$, add a variable $0\leq\varepsilon\leq1$, impose
$$ C\geq\varepsilon, \qquad M-q_a\geq\varepsilon\quad(a\notin W), $$
and maximize $\varepsilon$.

Call $W$ *admissible* if this program is feasible and its optimum is positive. This is equivalent to the existence of a genuine manipulation producing $W$: positive $\varepsilon$ supplies positive participating mass and all required strict score gaps; conversely, a genuine manipulation supplies a positive minimum of these finitely many quantities.

For admissible $W$, let
$$ \alpha_W=\min\{u:(u,c,Y,M)\in P_W\}. $$
Then $\alpha_W$ is the infimum of budgets of genuine manipulations producing $W$.

To prove this assertion, let $z_0$ minimize $u$ in $P_W$, and let $z_1$ be a genuine strict-feasibility witness. For $0<\lambda\leq1$, the point
$$ z_\lambda=(1-\lambda)z_0+\lambda z_1 $$
satisfies the closed constraints, has positive participating mass, and has every exclusion gap positive. Its budget tends to $\alpha_W$ as $\lambda$ tends to zero. Conversely, every genuine manipulation producing $W$ is feasible in $P_W$.

It follows that
$$ \theta = \min\left( \{1\}\cup \{\alpha_W:W\neq\mathcal B,\ W\text{ admissible}\} \right). $$

Each program has $O(m^2+\tau)$ variables and constraints, with rational coefficients of polynomial encoding length. All feasible regions are bounded: $C\leq1$, $Y_{aj}\leq C$, and the remaining variables are explicitly bounded.

I use the standard rational linear programming theorem: feasibility and exact optimization over a nonempty bounded rational polyhedron can be performed in time polynomial in the encoded program size. The rationality, size, and boundedness hypotheses have all been verified here. See [Khachiyan’s polynomial-time linear programming theorem](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319).

In particular, enumerating all $W$ gives an exact algorithm using $O(2^m)$ polynomial-size linear programs. Below, smaller families of winner sets suffice in additional cases.

2. A unique sincere winner: the perturbation lemma

Assume throughout this section that
$$ \mathcal B=\{b_0\}. $$
Thus
$$ A_{b_0}>A_a\quad(a\neq b_0). $$

First observe a necessary preference condition. If a participating type $t$ accepts the uniform lottery on $W$, then the dominance inequality at the prefix ending at $b_0$ gives
$$ \frac{|W\cap\operatorname{Top}_{r_t(b_0)}(t)|}{|W|} \geq1. $$
Hence every candidate in $W\setminus\{b_0\}$ is ranked above $b_0$ by every participant. Moreover, $W\setminus\{b_0\}$ is nonempty, since otherwise the outcome would not change.

2.1 Statement

Lemma. Suppose the sincere winner is unique and a manipulation produces $W$ at budget $u<1$. For every $\eta>0$, there is a manipulation at budget less than $\min\{1,u+\eta\}$ whose outcome is a singleton $\{a\}$, where $a\neq b_0$.

If $W$ is already a singleton, the assertion follows by retaining the manipulation. The proof below handles nonsingleton $W$.

2.2 The two-candidate case

When $m=2$, no manipulation exists under a unique sincere winner.

Indeed, write $a$ for the other candidate. Every participant would have to rank $a$ above $b_0$, so sincerely contributes score $1$ to $a$ and score $0$ to $b_0$. Changing its report cannot increase $a$’s score or decrease $b_0$’s score. Thus
$$ q_a\leq A_a<A_{b_0}\leq q_{b_0}, $$
which prevents $a$ from being a winner.

It remains to prove the lemma for $m\geq3$.

2.3 Choosing the candidate to promote

Take a manipulation at budget $u<1$. Let its masses be $c_t$, let $C=\sum_t c_t>0$, and define the withdrawn sincere contribution
$$ S_z=\sum_t c_t s_{r_t(z)} \quad(z\in\mathcal A). $$
Let $R_z$ be its aggregate reported contribution. Then
$$ q_z=A_z-S_z+R_z, \qquad 0\leq R_z\leq C. $$

Choose
$$ a\in W\setminus\{b_0\} $$
minimizing $S_a$ over $W\setminus\{b_0\}$. Therefore
$$ S_a\leq S_z \quad (z\in W\setminus\{a,b_0\}). $$
Every original participant ranks $a$ above $b_0$, so every such participant strictly prefers the singleton outcome $\{a\}$.

2.4 A feasible additional report distribution

Define
$$ \beta= \min\left\{ \frac{s_2}{2}, \frac{A_{b_0}-A_a}{2C} \right\}, \qquad \rho= \frac{\sum_{j=1}^m s_j-1-\beta}{m-2}. $$
We construct a report distribution with average scores
$$ v_a=1, \qquad v_{b_0}=\beta, \qquad v_z=\rho\quad(z\notin\{a,b_0\}). $$

If $s_2=0$, put $a$ first, $b_0$ last, and distribute the other candidates uniformly over the remaining positions. This realizes the desired averages, with $\beta=\rho=0$.

If $s_2>0$, mix the following two distributions:

  • put $a$ first and $b_0$ last;
  • put $a$ first and $b_0$ second.

Within each distribution, assign the other candidates uniformly to the remaining positions. Give the second distribution probability $\beta/s_2$. Since $0\leq\beta\leq s_2/2$, this is a valid mixture. The average score of $b_0$ is $\beta$, and symmetry and conservation of total score give average $\rho$ to each remaining candidate.

Crucially,
$$ \rho<1. $$
For $s_2=0$, this was already established. Otherwise $\beta>0$, and
$$ \sum_{j=1}^m s_j\leq m-1 $$
because $s_m=0$ and all other entries are at most $1$. Consequently,
$$ \rho\leq1-\frac{\beta}{m-2}<1. $$
This small positive value of $\beta$ handles veto as well as score vectors whose total score is strictly below $m-1$.

2.5 Adding a small participating mass

For each original participant, add mass $\delta c_t$ using the new report distribution, where $\delta>0$ will be sufficiently small. The new participating mass of type $t$ is $(1+\delta)c_t$, so its budget is at most $(1+\delta)u$.

The resulting score is
$$ q_z^{(\delta)}=q_z+\delta D_z, \qquad D_z=Cv_z-S_z. $$

For $z\in W\setminus\{a,b_0\}$, the original scores tie, and
$$ D_a-D_z = C(1-\rho)+S_z-S_a > 0. $$
Thus $a$ strictly outranks every such $z$ after the perturbation.

If $b_0\in W$, its original score also ties with $a$. The equality
$$ A_a-S_a+R_a = A_{b_0}-S_{b_0}+R_{b_0} $$
implies
$$ C-S_a+S_{b_0} = A_{b_0}-A_a+C-R_a+R_{b_0} \geq A_{b_0}-A_a. $$
Therefore
$$ D_a-D_{b_0} = C-S_a+S_{b_0}-C\beta \geq \frac{A_{b_0}-A_a}{2} > 0. $$
So $a$ also strictly outranks $b_0$.

Finally, every candidate outside $W$ originally has score strictly below $q_a$. Since there are finitely many candidates and scores depend continuously on $\delta$, these strict gaps remain positive for all sufficiently small $\delta>0$.

Choose such a $\delta$ also satisfying
$$ (1+\delta)u<1, \qquad \delta u<\eta. $$
This is possible because $0<u<1$. Candidate $a$ is then the unique winner at budget less than $\min\{1,u+\eta\}$.

The participants remain exactly the original participating types, and each strictly prefers $\{a\}$ to $\{b_0\}$. This proves the lemma.

3. Exact polynomial-time computation with a unique sincere winner

Theorem. If the sincere winner is unique, $\theta$ can be computed exactly in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$, for an arbitrary rational positional score vector.

Proof. For each $a\neq b_0$, apply the fixed-set formulation to $W=\{a\}$. Its eligible types are exactly
$$ E_{\{a\}}=\{t:r_t(a)<r_t(b_0)\}. $$
Test admissibility and, when admissible, compute $\alpha_{\{a\}}$. Define
$$ \widehat\theta = \min\left( \{1\}\cup \{\alpha_{\{a\}}:a\neq b_0,\ \{a\}\text{ admissible}\} \right). $$

Singleton manipulations are particular manipulations, so
$$ \theta\leq\widehat\theta. $$

If $\theta<1$, there are manipulation budgets $u$ arbitrarily close to $\theta$ with $u<1$. By the perturbation lemma, for every such $u$ there are singleton manipulation budgets arbitrarily close to $u$ from above. Hence
$$ \widehat\theta\leq u. $$
Taking manipulation budgets tending to $\theta$ gives $\widehat\theta\leq\theta$.

If $\theta=1$, the inequalities $\theta\leq\widehat\theta\leq1$ give equality directly. Thus in every case
$$ \theta=\widehat\theta. $$

There are $m-1$ proposed singleton sets, each requiring at most two polynomial-size rational linear programs. The running time is polynomial in $m,\tau,L$. $\square$

The use of an infimum matters here. The perturbation adds participating mass, so the lemma applies to budgets below $1$. It does not assert that every manipulation at budget exactly $1$ can be replaced by a singleton manipulation. The definition’s default value $\theta=1$ makes this endpoint unnecessary for computing the threshold.

4. Tied sincere winners: a bound on final support size

Assume now that $b\geq2$. For each type $t$, let $h_t$ be its highest-ranked candidate in $\mathcal B$. Define
$$ \kappa=\max\{j:s_j=1\}, $$
the size of the initial plateau of maximal positional scores.

4.1 An immediate zero-threshold condition

Proposition. If some type $t$ satisfies
$$ s_{r_t(h_t)}<1, $$
then $\theta=0$.

Proof. The type’s first candidate lies outside $\mathcal B$: otherwise it would be $h_t$ and would receive score $1$.

On an arbitrarily small positive mass of this type, swap $h_t$ with its first candidate. The score of $h_t$ increases by
$$ c_t\bigl(1-s_{r_t(h_t)}\bigr)>0. $$
The swapped outsider’s score decreases; all other candidate scores remain unchanged. Thus $h_t$ becomes the unique winner.

Because $h_t$ is this type’s favorite sincere winner and $b\geq2$, its singleton lottery strictly stochastically dominates the uniform lottery on $\mathcal B$. Arbitrarily small proportional budgets suffice. $\square$

4.2 A cardinality bound otherwise

Proposition. Suppose
$$ s_{r_t(h_t)}=1 \quad\text{for every type }t. $$
Every genuine manipulation has a final winner set $W$ satisfying
$$ |W|\leq b\kappa. $$

Proof. Take any participating type $t$. Its prefix ending at $h_t$ contains exactly one sincere winner. Stochastic dominance therefore requires
$$ \frac{|W\cap\operatorname{Top}_{r_t(h_t)}(t)|}{|W|} \geq\frac1b. $$
Consequently,
$$ |W| \leq b\,|W\cap\operatorname{Top}_{r_t(h_t)}(t)| \leq b\,r_t(h_t) \leq b\kappa. $$
The last inequality follows from $s_{r_t(h_t)}=1$. $\square$

Combining these propositions with the fixed-set formulation gives an exact algorithm:

  • If the zero-threshold condition holds, return $0$.
  • Otherwise, test only nonempty sets of size at most $\min\{m,b\kappa\}$.

Its running time is
$$ O\left( \left( \sum_{j=1}^{\min\{m,b\kappa\}}\binom mj \right) \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L) \right). $$

In particular, exact computation is polynomial-time for fixed $b$ and $\kappa$. If $s_1>s_2$, then $\kappa=1$, so it is polynomial-time for every fixed number of sincere winners.

5. Exactly two sincere winners and a strict top score

Assume
$$ \mathcal B=\{a_0,b_0\}, \qquad s_1>s_2. $$
For type $t$, let $h_t$ and $\ell_t$ be respectively its higher- and lower-ranked members of $\mathcal B$.

Theorem. The exact threshold is
$$ \theta= \begin{cases} 0, & \text{if some }t\text{ has } s_{r_t(h_t)}<1 \text{ or } s_{r_t(\ell_t)}>0, \\ 1, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} $$
In the second case, no manipulation exists.

Proof.

If $s_{r_t(h_t)}<1$ for some type, Section 4.1 proves $\theta=0$.

Suppose instead that $s_{r_t(\ell_t)}>0$ for some type. On an arbitrarily small positive mass, move $\ell_t$ to last position, preserving the order of all other candidates. Its score strictly decreases, while the score of $h_t$ remains unchanged. Every outsider initially has score strictly below $H$, so for sufficiently small mass all outsiders remain below $H$. Hence $h_t$ becomes the unique winner. This type strictly prefers $\{h_t\}$ to the uniform lottery on the two sincere winners, proving $\theta=0$.

It remains to consider the case
$$ s_{r_t(h_t)}=1, \qquad s_{r_t(\ell_t)}=0 \quad\text{for every }t. $$
Because $s_1>s_2$, every type’s sincere first candidate is $h_t\in\mathcal B$.

Suppose a manipulation produces $W$, with $w=|W|$. For every participant, dominance at its first prefix requires
$$ \frac{\mathbf 1_{\{h_t\in W\}}}{w}\geq\frac12. $$
Thus
$$ h_t\in W, \qquad w\leq2. $$

Strict improvement requires $W\neq\mathcal B$. Since $W$ contains a participant’s first candidate from $\mathcal B$ and has at most two members, it must contain exactly one sincere winner, say $a$, and exclude the other, say $d$.

Every participant then sincerely places $a$ first and gives $d$ score $0$. Its reports cannot increase its contribution to $a$ above its sincere contribution, and cannot decrease its contribution to $d$ below its sincere contribution. Therefore
$$ q_a\leq A_a=H, \qquad q_d\geq A_d=H. $$
This contradicts the requirement that $a$ be a winner while $d$ is strictly below the winning score.

No manipulation exists, so $\theta=1$. $\square$

The criterion requires only the rankings and their positional scores, and is polynomial-time computable.

6. What remains open

The unrestricted problem still requires either a polynomial-time algorithm or a valid NP-hardness reduction.

The new results eliminate the unique-sincere-winner case as a source of hardness. They also give polynomial algorithms when both the number of sincere winners and the maximal-score plateau size are fixed. For a strict top score and exactly two sincere winners, they give the stronger explicit classification above.

What remains unresolved is the optimization over final winner sets in unrestricted tied sincere outcomes. No argument here proves that this remaining optimization is polynomial-time, and no hardness reduction is established.

2. Unique sincere winner: perturbation lemma

Necessary preference condition

With sincere outcome $\{b_0\}$, the prefix ending at $b_0$ has sincere probability $1$. Dominance requires the entire final lottery to lie within that prefix. Consequently, every final winner other than $b_0$ must be ranked above $b_0$ by every participant.

Strict improvement excludes $W=\{b_0\}$. Thus the candidate selection from $W\setminus\{b_0\}$ is legitimate.

Two-candidate case

The normalization forces the score vector to be $(1,0)$. Every possible participant ranks the other candidate first, so changing reports cannot increase that candidate’s score or decrease $b_0$’s score.

The displayed inequalities prevent both a replacement winner and a tie involving the other candidate. The impossibility claim is correct.

Additional report distribution

For $m\geq3$, the quantities defining $\beta$ and $\rho$ are well-defined because $C>0$ and $m-2>0$.

When $s_2=0$, all scores after first position are zero, so the proposed distribution realizes $\beta=\rho=0$.

When $s_2>0$, the mixture probability $\beta/s_2$ lies in $[0,1/2]$. The two constituent distributions produce the stated average score for $b_0$. Symmetry and conservation of positional score produce the stated average for every remaining candidate.

The strict inequality $\rho<1$ is correctly justified. In this case $\beta>0$, and
$$ \sum_j s_j\leq m-1 $$
uses exactly the hypotheses $s_j\leq1$ and $s_m=0$.

Perturbed score comparisons

For another final winner $z\neq a,b_0$, the minimizing choice of $S_a$ gives $S_z-S_a\geq0$. Hence
$$ D_a-D_z=C(1-\rho)+S_z-S_a>0. $$

When $b_0\in W$, rearranging the original score tie gives the stated identity. The estimate uses the valid bounds $R_a\leq C$ and $R_{b_0}\geq0$. Together with
$$ C\beta\leq\frac{A_{b_0}-A_a}{2}, $$
it proves the claimed positive derivative gap.

Candidates outside $W$ have strictly positive original exclusion gaps. Since there are finitely many candidates and each perturbed score is affine in $\delta$, all these gaps persist for sufficiently small positive $\delta$.

Budget and participation
