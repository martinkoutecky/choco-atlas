The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), additive valuations \(v_t\), an integral bundle catalogue \(2^G\), and per-capita supplies \(b_g\), find masses \(x_{t,B}\geq 0\) satisfying \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}=b_g\), such that for every subpopulation \(H\) with \(0\leq h_{t,B}\leq x_{t,B}\), every type \(t\) and bundle \(B\) with \(h_{t,B}>0\) satisfy \(v_t(B)\geq \frac12\MMS_t^\infty(H)\), where \(\MMS_t^\infty(H)\) is the maximum minimum \(v_t\)-value attainable by redistributing \(H\)'s pooled goods among its mass while each individual receives an integral bundle.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(T\) be a finite set of types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and let \(G\) be a finite set of goods. Each type has a nonnegative additive valuation \(v_t\); each individual receives an integral bundle \(B\subseteq G\). Rational \(b_g\) is the mass of individuals who must receive good \(g\).
An allocation consists of masses \(x_{t,B}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}=b_g\). A subpopulation has masses \(0\leq h_{t,B}\leq x_{t,B}\). Define \(\operatorname{MMS}^{\infty}_t(H)\) as the greatest \(q\) for which its pooled goods can be redistributed into integral bundles, preserving its total mass and each good’s mass, while every bundle assigned positive mass has \(v_t\)-value at least \(q\).
Is finding an allocation satisfying \(v_t(B)\geq \tfrac12\operatorname{MMS}^{\infty}_t(H)\) for every subpopulation \(H\) and every \((t,B)\) with \(h_{t,B}>0\) solvable in polynomial time in the binary input length, or is it NP-hard?
For finite agent lists, a \(\tfrac12\)-approximate groupwise maximin share allocation always exists and is computable in polynomial time. A later discrete result improves the polynomial-time guarantee to \((\sqrt5-1)/2\). Neither result addresses this exact mass model.

Prover output (gpt-6-astra / medium, Bolzano prover prompt)

I do not settle the polynomial-time versus hardness question. The following results give a finite support characterization, polynomial-size witnesses, and an obstruction to directly transferring a familiar discrete argument.

1. Definitions and assumptions

For the complexity statements, assume that the item values \(v_t(g)\) are rational and binary encoded. Write \(m=|G|\) and \(n=|T|\). Assume \(\mu_t\geq 0\) and \(0\leq b_g\leq 1\); these conditions are necessary for feasibility.

For a nonzero subpopulation \(h\), put

\(M(h)=\sum_{t,B}h_{t,B}\), and \(c_g(h)=\sum_{t,B:g\in B}h_{t,B}\).

A redistribution is a nonnegative vector \(y=(y_C)_{C\subseteq G}\) satisfying

\(\displaystyle \sum_C y_C=M(h),\qquad \sum_{C:g\in C}y_C=c_g(h)\quad(g\in G).\)

Only bundles receiving positive mass enter its minimum value. Consequently, the benchmark is attained: every possible minimum belongs to the finite set \(\{v_t(C):C\subseteq G\}\), and at least one redistribution exists.

Scaling a subpopulation and its redistributions gives

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{MMS}^{\infty}_t(\lambda h)=\operatorname{MMS}^{\infty}_t(h)\qquad(\lambda>0).\)

Call an allocation *fair* if it satisfies the requested factor \(\tfrac12\) guarantee.

2. Proven: fairness depends only on support

Define \(\operatorname{supp}(x)=\{(t,B):x_{t,B}>0\}\).

Proposition. If \(x\) is fair and \(x'\) is feasible with \(\operatorname{supp}(x')\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(x)\), then \(x'\) is fair.

Proof. Suppose \(h'\leq x'\) violates fairness at \((t,B)\), so that

\(\displaystyle h'_{t,B}>0,\qquad \operatorname{MMS}^{\infty}_t(h')>2v_t(B).\)

All positive coordinates of \(h'\) correspond to positive coordinates of \(x\). Since there are finitely many coordinates, a sufficiently small \(\lambda>0\) satisfies \(\lambda h'\leq x\). Homogeneity then gives the same violation within \(x\), a contradiction. \(\square\)

In particular, two feasible allocations with identical supports are either both fair or both unfair.

Corollary. If a fair allocation exists, one exists with at most \(n+m\) positive coordinates and with polynomial-length rational masses.

Proof. Fix a fair allocation and restrict the allocation equations to its support. Their matrix has \(n+m\) rows and entries in \(\{0,1\}\): one row per type and one per good.

This restricted feasible polytope is nonempty and bounded, because all allocation masses sum to one. Choose a vertex \(z\). Its positive columns must be linearly independent. Otherwise, a nonzero dependence supported on the positive coordinates would allow sufficiently small perturbations in both directions, contradicting extremality. Hence \(z\) has at most \(n+m\) positive coordinates.

Its support is contained in the original fair support, so the proposition makes \(z\) fair.

For the bit bound, let \(r\leq n+m\) be its support size, and select an invertible \(r\times r\) submatrix determining its positive coordinates. Let \(D\) be the product of the input denominators of the masses. Its binary length is at most their total input length. Cramer’s rule and Hadamard’s determinant bound give numerators and denominators of bit length

\(\displaystyle O\bigl(\log D+r\log(r+1)\bigr).\)

Thus the resulting support list and masses have polynomial encoding length. \(\square\)

This rules out a possible representation obstruction: whenever a solution exists, exponentially many output bundles or exponentially long mass encodings are unnecessary.

3. Proven: violations have polynomial-size certificates

Fix a feasible allocation \(x\), its support \(S\), and a distinguished pair \(i=(t,B)\in S\). Define

\(\displaystyle \mathcal Q_i=\{C\subseteq G:v_t(C)>2v_t(B)\}.\)

Consider nonnegative variables \(a_j\) for \(j\in S\), and \(y_C\) for \(C\in\mathcal Q_i\), satisfying

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j\in S}a_j=\sum_{C\in\mathcal Q_i}y_C,\)

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j=(s,D)\in S:g\in D}a_j=\sum_{C\in\mathcal Q_i:g\in C}y_C\quad(g\in G),\)

and \(a_i=1\).

Proposition. Fairness fails at \(i\) if and only if this system is feasible.

Proof. A violating subpopulation and a redistribution attaining its benchmark use only bundles in \(\mathcal Q_i\). Divide all their masses by the positive distinguished mass to obtain the displayed system.

Conversely, suppose the system is feasible. Choose \(\lambda>0\) small enough that \(\lambda a_j\leq x_j\) for every positive \(a_j\). Then \(\lambda a\) is a subpopulation containing \(i\), and \(\lambda y\) is its redistribution. Every positive redistribution bundle has value strictly greater than \(2v_t(B)\). There are finitely many such bundles, so their minimum also strictly exceeds \(2v_t(B)\). This is a violation. \(\square\)

The system has \(m+2\) equations, coefficients in \(\{-1,0,1\}\), and a right-hand side with a single nonzero entry, equal to one.

If it is feasible, it has a feasible solution with linearly independent positive columns: starting from any feasible solution, a dependence among its positive columns permits a feasible movement until a positive coordinate becomes zero; repeat. Thus at most \(m+2\) variables need be positive.

Selecting a nonsingular square submatrix and applying Cramer’s rule shows that their rational values have bit length \(O((m+2)\log(m+2))\). Each selected bundle needs only \(m\) bits to describe.

Therefore a violation has a polynomial-size certificate consisting of the distinguished pair, the selected original and redistribution bundles, and their normalized masses. Its equations and strict valuation inequalities can all be checked in polynomial time.

Two consequences follow:

  • Checking fairness of an explicitly listed rational allocation belongs to \(\mathrm{coNP}\).
  • The decision problem asking whether a fair allocation exists belongs to \(\Sigma_2^p\): existentially guess the polynomial-size allocation furnished by Section 2, and universally exclude the polynomial-size violation certificates above.

These are upper bounds, not hardness results or a polynomial-time construction.

4. Proven: a sufficient condition, and why it cannot always be imposed

The discrete EFL approach underlying the original finite-agent result motivates a useful sufficient condition. The finite-agent result is established by [Barman, Biswas, Krishnamurthy, and Narahari](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07621). The implication below is proved directly for the mass model.

Suppose that, for every two supported pairs \((t,B)\) and \((s,C)\), at least one of the following holds:

  • \(C\) contains at most one good positively valued by \(t\);
  • \(v_t(C)\leq 2v_t(B)\).

Proposition. Every allocation satisfying this condition is fair.

Proof. Fix a subpopulation \(h\) containing \((t,B)\), and put \(a=v_t(B)\). Let

\(\displaystyle L=\{g\in G:v_t(g)>2a\}.\)

Every original supported bundle containing a good in \(L\) has exactly one positively valued good. Indeed, its value exceeds \(2a\), so the first alternative must hold. Let \(s\) be the mass of such bundles within \(h\), and put \(r=M(h)-s\).

The distinguished bundle \(B\) contains no good in \(L\), since \(v_t(B)=a\). Hence \(r>0\).

Bundles containing \(L\) contribute no value from goods outside \(L\). Every remaining original bundle has value at most \(2a\): this follows either from the second alternative or from having at most one positive good, whose value is at most \(2a\). Thus the total pooled value from goods outside \(L\) is at most \(2ar\).

The total mass of goods in \(L\) is exactly \(s\). In any redistribution, the mass of individuals receiving at least one such good is therefore at most \(s\). At least \(r\) individuals receive only goods outside \(L\).

If every redistribution bundle had value at least some \(q>2a\), those individuals alone would require value at least \(qr>2ar\), a contradiction. Thus the benchmark is at most \(2a\). \(\square\)

However, this condition is not always achievable in the mass model.

Take one type and two goods with

\(\displaystyle v(g)=3,\qquad v(h)=1,\qquad b_g=\tfrac12,\qquad b_h=1.\)

Every individual must receive \(h\). Consequently, the unique allocation is

\(\displaystyle x_{\{h\}}=\tfrac12,\qquad x_{\{g,h\}}=\tfrac12.\)

It fails the sufficient condition: the second bundle contains two positive goods and has value \(4>2v(\{h\})\).

Nevertheless, it satisfies exact groupwise fairness. If a subpopulation contains positive mass of \(\{h\}\), its mass of \(g\) is smaller than its population mass. Every redistribution must give \(h\) to everyone, and some positive mass must remain without \(g\). Its benchmark is therefore exactly one. A subpopulation containing only \(\{g,h\}\) has benchmark four.

Thus even a universally present good can obstruct an EFL-style support requirement without obstructing the desired fairness guarantee.

5. Proven: exact existence for one type, but exact computation is hard

For one type, an allocation satisfying the stronger factor-one guarantee always exists.

Proof. Let \(a_1<\cdots<a_k\) be the distinct bundle values. For a feasible allocation \(x\), define

\(\displaystyle z_j(x)=\sum_{B:v(B)=a_j}x_B.\)

Lexicographically minimize \((z_1(x),\ldots,z_k(x))\), starting with the lowest value. A minimizer exists: minimize each coordinate successively over the compact feasible set left by the previous minimizations.

Suppose a subpopulation \(h\) containing \(B\) admits a redistribution \(y\) in which every positive bundle has value at least \(q>v(B)\). Let \(a_j\) be the smallest value of a bundle appearing positively in \(h\). Then \(a_j\leq v(B)<q\).

The allocation \(x'=x-h+y\) is feasible. Its coordinates \(z_\ell\) with \(\ell<j\) are unchanged, while \(z_j\) strictly decreases: positive mass at value \(a_j\) is removed and none is added. This contradicts lexicographic minimality. \(\square\)

This is an existence proof, not a polynomial-time algorithm. In fact, computing an exact fair allocation is already \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard for one type under explicit support-list output.

To prove this, take a PARTITION instance with positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\) summing to \(2A\). Introduce one good per integer, with

\(\displaystyle v(g_j)=a_j,\qquad b_{g_j}=\tfrac12.\)

For the grand population, a redistribution attaining minimum value at least \(A\) exists if and only if the integers have an equal partition:

  • If \(P\subseteq G\) has value \(A\), assign mass \(\tfrac12\) to \(P\) and mass \(\tfrac12\) to \(G\setminus P\).
  • Conversely, every feasible redistribution has average value

\(\sum_j b_{g_j}v(g_j)=A\).
If all positive bundles have value at least \(A\), they must all have value exactly \(A\). Any positive bundle yields a partition.

In a yes-instance, exact groupwise fairness forces every positive output bundle to have value at least \(A\), by applying the guarantee to the grand population. In a no-instance, no feasible allocation can have that property. Therefore a polynomial-time exact-fairness algorithm would decide PARTITION by inspecting its output.

The same reduction proves hardness of evaluating the exact grand-population benchmark. It does not prove hardness for the requested factor \(\tfrac12\).

6. Remaining question

The target classification remains unsettled by these arguments. The support characterization eliminates irrationality and output-size concerns, and provides small certificates of failure. What is still missing is either a polynomial-time method for constructing a fair support in the general heterogeneous instance or a hardness reduction for finding such a support at the specified approximation factor. General existence in this precise mass model is also not established here.
