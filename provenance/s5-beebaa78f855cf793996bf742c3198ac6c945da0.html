The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and directed balanced FEN relations \(q_{ts}\in\{-1,0,+1\}\), decide whether there exists a partition into finitely many coalition blocks \(K\), represented by masses \(x_{tK}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_K x_{tK}=\mu_t\), such that no move transfers \(0<\varepsilon<x_{tK}\) of type \(t\) from \(K\) to another block or a new block and yields a positive signed mass vote for every sufficiently small \(\varepsilon\). Each resident votes according to the sign of their balanced-utility change, and moved mass compares its utility in the destination with its utility in the source.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is atomless and given by its support: a list of \(\tau=|T|\geq1\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Support size means listed positive-mass types, not the number of possible types. Let \(m=\tau\) count relation vertices and \(L\) be the total input bit length.
The input includes directed relations \(q_{ts}\in\{-1,0,+1\}\), denoting enemies, neutrals, and friends. Symmetry is not required; \(q_{tt}\) describes relations between distinct individuals of type \(t\) and may take any of these values.
A partition consists of finitely many positive-total-mass blocks with real allocations \(x_{tK}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_Kx_{tK}=\mu_t\). The number of blocks is unrestricted. Balanced utility is \(u_t(K)=\sum_s q_{ts}x_{sK}\); higher utility is preferred and equal utilities give indifference.
A move transfers \(0<\varepsilon<x_{tK}\) of type \(t\) from \(K\) to a different existing block \(H\), or to a new block initialized by \(x_{sH}=0\). Remaining source residents of type \(s\) change utility by \(-\varepsilon q_{st}\), destination residents by \(\varepsilon q_{st}\), and moved individuals by \(u_t(H)+\varepsilon q_{tt}-u_t(K)\).
Each individual votes by the sign of their utility change, with indifference contributing zero; votes are weighted by mass. Thus the signed vote is \(V_{t,K,H}(\varepsilon)=\sum_s[-(x_{sK}-\varepsilon\mathbf{1}_{s=t})q_{st}+x_{sH}q_{st}]+\varepsilon\operatorname{sgn}(u_t(H)+\varepsilon q_{tt}-u_t(K))\).
A partition is locally popular if there are no \(t,K,H\) and \(\delta>0\) for which \(V_{t,K,H}(\varepsilon)>0\) for every \(0<\varepsilon<\min\{\delta,x_{tK}\}\). Moves are considered regardless of whether moved individuals benefit.

Is deciding whether such a locally popular partition exists solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite agents, existence of a locally popular partition with directed balanced friends–enemies–neutrals preferences is NP-complete; with symmetric relations, such partitions always exist and can be found in polynomial time. [Source](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/54dd78de6883c365bd17a930941e48f3-Paper-Conference.pdf)
No result settling this exact atomless, small-mass-transfer problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted the distribution as an atomless society and balanced utility as the unnormalized signed sum of coalition masses; an individual's own mass is zero.
  • Allowed every diagonal relation to describe interactions between distinct individuals of the same type; excluding self-interaction does not require neutrality between distinct same-type individuals.
  • Allowed real coalition allocations, required every represented block to have positive total mass, and imposed no bound on the finite number of blocks.
  • Specified that moved mass compares destination utility after the transfer with source utility before the transfer, including interactions within the moved mass.
  • Made indifference contribute zero and weighted each vote by the individual's mass, including moved mass.
  • Formalized 'every sufficiently small transfer' using a positive threshold for each fixed source, destination, and moved type.
  • There is no separate alternatives dimension in this model, so set m equal to the number of type-relation vertices, namely the support size.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-hard}\). In fact, its restriction to instances with \(q_{tt}=+1\) for every type is \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-complete}\), even when all masses are dyadic rationals.

The proof below directly uses the stated small-transfer voting rule.

1. The exact condition when \(q_{tt}=+1\)

For any block \(K\), define its incoming and outgoing scores for type \(t\) by

\(a_t(K)=\sum_s q_{st}x_{sK}\), and \(b_t(K)=\sum_s q_{ts}x_{sK}=u_t(K)\).

For the new-block destination, both scores are zero.

Fix a source \(K\) with \(x_{tK}>0\), a destination \(H\), and suppose \(q_{tt}=+1\). Set

\(A=a_t(H)-a_t(K)\), and \(D=b_t(H)-b_t(K)\).

The given vote formula becomes

\(V_{t,K,H}(\varepsilon)=A+\varepsilon\bigl(1+\operatorname{sgn}(D+\varepsilon)\bigr)\).

If \(A>0\), the vote is positive for every sufficiently small positive transfer. If \(A<0\), it is negative for sufficiently small transfers, since the second term lies between zero and \(2\varepsilon\).

If \(A=0\), there are two cases:

  • When \(D<0\), every sufficiently small positive transfer satisfies \(D+\varepsilon<0\), and its vote is zero.
  • When \(D\geq0\), every positive transfer has vote \(2\varepsilon>0\).

Consequently, this move is prevented from winning for all sufficiently small transfers exactly when

\(a_t(K)>a_t(H)\),

or

\(a_t(K)=a_t(H)\quad\text{and}\quad b_t(K)>b_t(H)\). \[\tag{1}\]

This criterion includes moves to a new block.

Lemma. If \(q_{tt}=+1\), then type \(t\) occupies exactly one block in every locally popular partition.

Proof. Suppose \(t\) occupies two distinct blocks \(K,H\). Applying (1) in both directions first forces \(a_t(K)=a_t(H)\), and then requires both \(b_t(K)>b_t(H)\) and \(b_t(H)>b_t(K)\), a contradiction. Since \(\mu_t>0\), type \(t\) must therefore occupy exactly one block. \(\square\)

Thus, when all diagonal entries are positive, every locally popular partition assigns each type’s entire mass to one block. In particular, it has at most \(\tau\) blocks.

2. A restricted partition problem

We reduce from the following restriction of \(\mathrm{PARTITION}\):

Given positive integers \(w_1,\ldots,w_n,B\), with

\(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i=2B\), \(0<w_i<B\), and \(B\) a power of two,

decide whether some subset has sum \(B\).

This restriction is \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-hard}\). Here is a reduction from the classical positive-integer \(\mathrm{PARTITION}\) problem, whose \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-completeness}\) is established in [Karp’s paper](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Let the original integers have total \(S\).

If \(S\) is odd, output the fixed no-instance \(w=(3,3,2)\), \(B=4\).

Otherwise write \(S=2C\). Choose \(B\) to be the smallest power of two strictly greater than \(2C\), and append two integers, each equal to \(B-C\).

The resulting total is \(2B\). Every original integer is at most \(2C<B\), and both appended integers lie strictly between \(B/2\) and \(B\). Hence all required inequalities hold.

In any equal partition of the resulting instance, the appended integers must lie on opposite sides: together they exceed \(B\). Each side must consequently contain original integers totaling \(C\). Conversely, an equal partition of the original integers extends to one of the resulting integers by placing one appended integer on each side.

Finally, \(B\leq4C\), so this transformation has polynomial bit complexity.

3. Constructing the society

Take a restricted instance \(w_1,\ldots,w_n,B\).

Introduce two anchor types \(a,b\) and one item type \(i\) for each integer \(w_i\). Write \(\alpha=1/4\), and assign masses

\(\mu_a=\mu_b=\alpha\), and \(\mu_i=w_i/(4B)\).

These masses are positive and sum to one:

\(2\alpha+\sum_i\mu_i=1/2+2B/(4B)=1\).

Moreover, \(\mu_i<\alpha\) for every item. Since \(B\) is a power of two, every mass is dyadic.

Specify all relations as follows:

  • Every diagonal entry is \(+1\).
  • The anchors are mutual enemies: \(q_{ab}=q_{ba}=-1\).
  • Each anchor regards every item as a friend: \(q_{ai}=q_{bi}=+1\).
  • Each item regards both anchors as enemies: \(q_{ia}=q_{ib}=-1\).
  • Distinct items are neutral to one another: \(q_{ij}=0\) for \(i\neq j\).

There are exactly \(n+2\) positive-mass types. The relation matrix and mass encodings have polynomial size in the source input.

4. A locally popular partition yields an equal split

Suppose the constructed society has a locally popular partition. By the lemma, every type occupies one block.

The anchors occupy distinct blocks

Suppose both anchors occupy a block \(K\), and let \(M\) be its total item mass. For anchor \(a\),

\(a_a(K)=\alpha-\alpha-M=-M\),

and

\(b_a(K)=\alpha-\alpha+M=M\).

If \(M>0\), its incoming score is negative, whereas a new block has incoming score zero. This violates (1).

If \(M=0\), both its scores are zero. A new block also has both scores zero, again violating the strict tie condition in (1).

Thus the anchors occupy distinct blocks, denoted \(K_a,K_b\).

Every item occupies an anchor block

Suppose item \(i\) occupies a block \(K\) containing neither anchor. Since distinct items are neutral,

\(a_i(K)=\mu_i\).

At \(K_a\), its incoming score is \(\alpha\): the anchor contributes \(\alpha\), other items contribute zero, and item \(i\) itself is absent.

Because \(\mu_i<\alpha\), moving toward \(K_a\) violates (1). Therefore every item occupies either \(K_a\) or \(K_b\).

Both blocks have exactly the required item mass

Let \(M_a,M_b\) be their respective item masses. Their sum is

\(M_a+M_b=\sum_i\mu_i=2\alpha\).

Anchor \(a\)’s incoming score in its block is

\(a_a(K_a)=\alpha-M_a\).

Comparison with a new block, whose incoming score is zero, requires \(M_a\leq\alpha\). Likewise, \(M_b\leq\alpha\). Together with their sum, these inequalities force

\(M_a=M_b=\alpha\).

The items assigned to \(K_a\) therefore satisfy

\(\sum_{i\in K_a}w_i=4B M_a=B\).

Hence the source partition instance is a yes-instance.

5. An equal split yields a locally popular partition

Conversely, suppose the items can be divided into two groups, each having integer weight \(B\). Form two blocks: anchor \(a\) with the first group, and anchor \(b\) with the second.

Each block has item mass \(\alpha\).

For any item \(i\), its incoming score in its own block is \(\alpha+\mu_i\). Its incoming score in the other block is \(\alpha\), and in a new block it is zero. Thus every possible destination has strictly smaller incoming score, satisfying (1).

For either anchor, its scores in its own block are

\(a_t(K)=\alpha-\alpha=0\), and \(b_t(K)=\alpha+\alpha=2\alpha>0\).

Its incoming score in the other block is \(-\alpha-\alpha=-2\alpha<0\). Its incoming score in a new block is zero, but its outgoing score there is zero, strictly below \(2\alpha\). Both destinations therefore satisfy (1).

These comparisons exhaust every possible source type and destination. The partition is locally popular.

6. Complexity conclusion

We have proved the equivalence

\(\text{the integers admit an equal partition}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{the constructed society admits a locally popular partition}\).

The construction is polynomial in input bit length and uses \(m=\tau=n+2\). Therefore the decision problem in the question is \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-hard}\).

For the restriction where every \(q_{tt}=+1\), membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) also follows: a certificate assigns each type to one of at most \(\tau\) blocks. Its allocations are exactly the given rational masses, and all comparisons in (1) can be checked using polynomial-time exact rational arithmetic. Hence that restriction is \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-complete}\).

A polynomial-time algorithm for the unrestricted problem in \(m,\tau,L\) would therefore imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
