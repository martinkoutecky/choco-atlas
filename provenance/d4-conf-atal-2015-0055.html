The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(M\) resource categories and a finite set of types \(\theta=(\succ_\theta,e_\theta)\), each with rational mass \(\mu_\theta>0\) summing to \(1\), let category supplies be \(\beta_r=\sum_{\theta:e_\theta=r}\mu_\theta\) and initial allocation be \(x^0_{\theta,e_\theta}=\mu_\theta\). A move chooses types \(\theta,\theta'\), categories \(r,s\), and \(\delta>0\) with \(x_{\theta,r},x_{\theta',s}\ge\delta\), \(s\succ_\theta r\), and \(r\succ_{\theta'}s\), then exchanges mass \(\delta\) between those cells. Given a rational threshold \(q\), does a finite sequence of such moves reach an allocation \(x\) with \(\sum_{\theta,r}x_{\theta,r}(M+1-\operatorname{rank}_\theta(r))\ge q\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(R=\{1,\ldots,m\}\), with \(m\ge1\). A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types \(\theta=(\succ_\theta,e_\theta)\), where \(\succ_\theta\) is a strict total order on \(R\), \(e_\theta\in R\), and each mass \(\mu_\theta>0\) is a binary rational, with \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Set \(\beta_r=\sum_{\theta:e_\theta=r}\mu_\theta\). An allocation has real entries \(x_{\theta,r}\ge0\), row sums \(\mu_\theta\), and column sums \(\beta_r\); initially \(x^0_{\theta,e_\theta}=\mu_\theta\) and all other entries are zero.
A move chooses distinct types \(\theta,\theta'\), distinct categories \(r,s\), and real \(0<\delta\le\min\{x_{\theta,r},x_{\theta',s}\}\), with \(s\succ_\theta r\) and \(r\succ_{\theta'}s\). It subtracts \(\delta\) from those two cells and adds \(\delta\) to \(x_{\theta,s}\) and \(x_{\theta',r}\), leaving other entries unchanged.
Given a binary rational \(q\), the decision problem asks whether a finite sequence of moves, possibly empty, reaches \(x\) with \(\sum_{\theta,r}x_{\theta,r}(m+1-\operatorname{rank}_\theta(r))\ge q\), where rank one is best. Only allocations reached after finitely many moves count.
Is this decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, where \(L\) is the total input bit length?
For discrete societies with one distinct resource per agent, the corresponding utilitarian threshold reachability problem under strictly improving bilateral swaps is NP-complete ([Damamme et al., 2015](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2015/aamas/p625.pdf)). No result settling the exact mass problem above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Preferences are strict total orders over all categories, and rank one is best, following the discrete definitions.
  • Allocation entries and move amounts are real; only input masses and the threshold require binary rational encoding.
  • Allocations preserve each type's mass and each category's supply; all unspecified initial cells are zero.
  • The finite sequence may be empty, and limiting allocations of infinite sequences do not count.
  • Zero-supply categories are allowed, as the graded question imposes positivity only on supported type masses.

Result

The decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The proof reduces the exact threshold question to finite reachability in a continuous Petri net.

1. The known reachability theorem

A continuous Petri net has finitely many places and transitions, with nonnegative integer input and output matrices \(\mathrm{Pre}\) and \(\mathrm{Post}\). A marking \(a\) assigns a nonnegative real number to each place. Transition \(t\) can fire by a real amount \(\delta>0\) when \(a_p\ge\delta\,\mathrm{Pre}_{p,t}\) for every place \(p\); its resulting marking is \(a+\delta(\mathrm{Post}_{\cdot,t}-\mathrm{Pre}_{\cdot,t})\).

Known theorem (Fraca–Haddad). Given such a net and nonnegative rational initial and target markings, deciding whether a finite sequence of real-valued firings reaches the target is polynomial-time in the binary encoding size. This is the reachability result in Proposition 27, using Algorithm 2; Definitions 4–5 specify precisely the firing and finite-reachability semantics above. [Fraca and Haddad, *Complexity Analysis of Continuous Petri Nets*](https://lsv.ens-paris-saclay.fr/Publis/PAPERS/PDF/FH-fundi15.pdf).

We apply this theorem to an explicitly constructed net.

2. Construction

Write \(w_{\theta,r}=m+1-\operatorname{rank}_\theta(r)\) and \(W(x)=\sum_{\theta,r}w_{\theta,r}x_{\theta,r}\). Each weight is an integer satisfying \(1\le w_{\theta,r}\le m\).

Compute \(W(x^0)\). If \(q\le W(x^0)\), return yes, witnessed by the empty sequence. Henceforth assume \(q>W(x^0)\), which implies \(q>0\).

Construct a continuous Petri net with one place \(p_{\theta,r}\) for every allocation cell and one additional utility place \(z\). Its transitions are as follows.

  • Swap transitions. For every pair of distinct types \(\theta,\theta'\) and distinct categories \(r,s\) satisfying \(s\succ_\theta r\) and \(r\succ_{\theta'}s\), include

\(p_{\theta,r}+p_{\theta',s}\longrightarrow p_{\theta,s}+p_{\theta',r}\).
All four arc weights are one. Firing this transition by \(\delta\) is exactly the corresponding society move.

  • Collection transitions. For each cell, include

\(p_{\theta,r}\longrightarrow w_{\theta,r}z\).
Firing by \(\delta\) consumes \(\delta\) from that cell and produces \(w_{\theta,r}\delta\) in \(z\).

  • Disposal transition. Include a transition consuming one unit from \(z\) and producing nothing. Firing by \(\delta\) removes \(\delta\) utility units.

The initial marking \(a^0\) has \(a^0_{p_{\theta,r}}=x^0_{\theta,r}\) and \(a^0_z=0\). The fixed target marking \(b\) has \(b_{p_{\theta,r}}=0\) for every cell and \(b_z=q\).

Claim. The society instance is a yes-instance if and only if \(b\) is finitely reachable from \(a^0\).

3. Proof of the equivalence

A successful society sequence reaches the auxiliary target

Suppose finitely many society moves reach an allocation \(x\) satisfying \(W(x)\ge q\).

Execute the corresponding swap transitions in the net. Next, for each positive cell \(x_{\theta,r}\), fire its collection transition by exactly \(x_{\theta,r}\). Every such firing is enabled, since it consumes only that cell.

After these finitely many collection firings, all cell places are zero and the utility place contains exactly \(W(x)\). If \(W(x)>q\), fire the disposal transition by \(W(x)-q\). If \(W(x)=q\), no disposal is needed.

Thus the target \(b\) is reached after finitely many firings.

A successful auxiliary sequence yields a successful society sequence

Conversely, suppose a finite auxiliary firing sequence reaches \(b\). Remove all collection and disposal firings, retaining the swap firings in their original order and with their original amounts.

We first prove that this retained sequence is executable in the society.

Process the auxiliary sequence from its beginning. Let \(a\) be its current vector of cell markings, and let \(X\) be the allocation obtained by executing only the swaps encountered so far. Initially, \(X=a=x^0\). Maintain the coordinatewise inequality \(X\ge a\).

  • A collection firing decreases one coordinate of \(a\) and leaves \(X\) unchanged, preserving the inequality.
  • A disposal firing changes neither vector.
  • For a swap firing of amount \(\delta\), its two input cells in \(a\) contain at least \(\delta\). The corresponding cells in \(X\) therefore also contain at least \(\delta\), so the society move is legal. Updating both vectors by the same swap preserves \(X\ge a\).

This induction proves that every retained swap is legal. Consequently, the retained finite sequence reaches a valid society allocation \(X\): its row and column sums remain those of \(x^0\).

It remains to compute its utility. Let \(d_{\theta,r}\) be the total amount consumed by collection transitions from cell \(p_{\theta,r}\), and let \(h\ge0\) be the total amount removed from \(z\) by disposal firings.

Throughout the preceding induction, the difference \(X-a\) equals the vector of amounts collected so far: swaps preserve this difference, and collection increases the corresponding coordinate by its firing amount. At the auxiliary target, every cell marking is zero. Therefore \(X_{\theta,r}=d_{\theta,r}\) for every cell.

Only collection produces utility tokens, and only disposal consumes them. Since the initial utility marking is zero and the final one is \(q\), we obtain
\(q=\sum_{\theta,r}w_{\theta,r}d_{\theta,r}-h\).
Hence
\(W(X)=\sum_{\theta,r}w_{\theta,r}d_{\theta,r}=q+h\ge q\).

The retained society sequence therefore witnesses a yes-instance. This proves the claim.

4. Polynomial running time and finite reachability

The net has \(\tau m+1\) places, at most \(\tau^2m^2\) swap transitions, \(\tau m\) collection transitions, and one disposal transition. Every arc weight is a nonnegative integer at most \(m\). Its initial and target markings contain only the input masses, \(q\), and zeros.

Thus the net and both markings have binary encoding size polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and they can be constructed within that bound. All hypotheses of the finite-reachability theorem hold. Applying its polynomial-time algorithm decides the society problem.

The equivalence preserves finite sequences in both directions and includes threshold equality exactly. No passage to a limit or assumption that a welfare supremum is attained is used.
