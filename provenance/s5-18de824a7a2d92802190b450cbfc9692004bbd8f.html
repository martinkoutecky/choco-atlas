The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(d\), a finite type set \(T\), masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), a resolution \(N\) such that \(N\mu_t\) is integral, and each type's ballot probabilities \(p_{t,j}\) and inspection cost \(c_t\), choose adaptively which uninspected ballot unit to inspect. Each unit of type \(t\) independently votes for \(j\) with probability \(p_{t,j}\), and inspection costs \(c_t/N\). Minimize expected total cost until the strict absolute-majority winner, or the absence of one, is certified.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m=d\geq2\) candidates. Input includes a binary-encoded integer resolution \(N\geq1\) with \(N\mu_t\in\mathbb Z\), binary-rational probabilities \(0<p_{t,j}<1\) with \(\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{t,j}=1\), and binary-rational costs \(c_t\geq0\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Type \(t\) supplies \(N\mu_t\) ballot units, each of mass \(1/N\). All units independently choose a candidate according to their type's probabilities. An inspection reveals one previously uninspected unit's vote and costs \(c_t/N\); subsequent choices may depend on observed votes.
If \(a_j\) inspected units voted for \(j\) and \(s=\sum_j a_j\), certify winner \(j\) when \(a_j\geq\lfloor N/2\rfloor+1\), or certify no winner when \(s-a_j\geq\lceil N/2\rceil\) for every \(j\). Certification must be exact for every realization; ties yield no winner.
Let \(\operatorname{OPT}\) be the minimum expected total inspection cost among all adaptive strategies that certify the outcome.

Does this support-encoded inspection problem admit an adaptive strategy with expected cost at most \(4\operatorname{OPT}\), whose preprocessing and every next-inspection decision take time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\log(N+1)\), uniformly over all valid resolutions \(N\)?

For explicitly listed voters, a polynomial-time adaptive \(4\)-approximation is known, with polynomial time understood per next-inspection decision ([Hellerstein, Liu, and Schewior](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10476)).
No result explicitly treating this support encoding with binary resolution and the stated running-time bound was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified binary encoding for the resolution and rational encoding for probabilities and costs; defined L as total input bit length.
  • Restored strictly positive ballot probabilities summing to one and nonnegative inspection costs, as in the discrete model; required at least two candidates and a positive resolution.
  • Made certification exact for every realization and supplied the discrete winner and no-winner certificate thresholds, with the voter count replaced by N.
  • Interpreted polynomial time per inspection decision, with polynomial preprocessing, measured against the support encoding and log(N+1), rather than requiring polynomial total time for up to N physical inspections.
  • Defined the approximation benchmark over unrestricted adaptive certification strategies.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

Yes. The explicit-voter algorithm can be implemented exactly using type multiplicities, with preprocessing and each next-inspection decision polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\log(N+1)\).

The essential step is to implement its threshold-testing subroutine without expanding the \(N\) ballot units. Zero costs require a separate limiting argument, given below.

1. Known results used

We use two published results.

Absolute-majority approximation theorem. For explicitly listed independent categorical votes with positive inspection costs, Algorithm 1 of Hellerstein–Liu–Schewior has expected cost at most \(4\) times optimum. Its first phase inspects in nondecreasing cost order until at most two candidates remain possible winners; its second phase uses SBB threshold evaluation to check those candidates. [Hellerstein–Liu–Schewior, Theorem 1 and Algorithm 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10476#S3).

SBB threshold theorem. Consider independent Bernoulli variables with probabilities \(q_i\in(0,1)\), positive costs \(w_i\), and the task of determining whether at least \(k\) of the \(r\) variables equal \(1\). For \(1\leq k\leq r\), sort the remaining variables by \(w_i/q_i\) and separately by \(w_i/(1-q_i)\). An optimal adaptive strategy may test any variable belonging both to the first \(k\) positions of the first order and to the first \(r-k+1\) positions of the second, and then recurse. Both orders may break ties arbitrarily. [Gkenosis–Grammel–Hellerstein–Kletenik, Sections 3 and 7](https://arxiv.org/html/2111.08793#S3).

The intersection exists because these two prefixes have sizes summing to \(r+1\), whereas there are only \(r\) remaining variables.

We apply these results to a conceptual expansion of the input. We never construct that expansion.

2. Conceptual expansion and compressed state

Set \(n_t=N\mu_t\). By the input promises, these are positive integers and \(\sum_{t\in T}n_t=N\).

Conceptually label the units of type \(t\) by \((t,1),\ldots,(t,n_t)\). Each has the given categorical distribution and inspection cost \(c_t/N\). This is exactly the original inspection problem: changing its encoding does not change its realizations, available inspections, certificates, or optimum.

Our implementation always inspects units of a chosen type in increasing local-label order. It stores:

  • \(r_t\), the number of uninspected units of type \(t\);
  • \(a_j\), the number of inspected votes for candidate \(j\);
  • the current phase and, in the second phase, the candidate being checked.

Write \(s=\sum_j a_j\) and \(r=\sum_t r_t=N-s\). All these counts are at most \(N\).

Conditional on any reached inspection history, the uninspected votes remain independent with their original type distributions. Indeed, the choices already made are deterministic functions of previously observed votes; conditioning on a reached history fixes only those revealed coordinates.

Define \(H=\lfloor N/2\rfloor+1\) and \(Q=\lceil N/2\rceil\). Candidate \(j\) remains a possible winner precisely when \(a_j+r\geq H\), equivalently \(s-a_j<Q\), because \(N-H+1=Q\).

These possible-winner sets only shrink: inspecting a vote increases \(s-a_j\) by either zero or one.

For a fixed candidate \(j\), deciding whether it wins is therefore the residual threshold problem with \(r\) variables and threshold \(k=H-a_j\). Its variables have success probabilities \(p_{t,j}\). The terminal cases are:

  • \(k\leq0\): candidate \(j\) is certified;
  • \(k>r\): candidate \(j\) is ruled out.

The latter condition is exactly \(s-a_j\geq Q\).

3. Implementing an SBB decision from multiplicities

First suppose all costs are positive. For each candidate \(j\), preprocess two permutations of the types:

  • \(\sigma_j^+\), ordered by \(c_t/p_{t,j}\);
  • \(\sigma_j^-\), ordered by \(c_t/(1-p_{t,j})\).

Break equal ratios by a fixed type index. Within each type, use increasing local-label order. The common cost factor \(1/N\) does not affect either ordering.

Deleting inspected units leaves each type as a contiguous block of \(r_t\) remaining units in each order. Thus these fixed type permutations describe the current expanded orders exactly.

Consider a nonterminal threshold state \(1\leq k\leq r\). Let \(A\) be the first \(k\) remaining units in the positive order and \(B\) the first \(r-k+1\) remaining units in the negative order.

For each type, compute the block lengths preceding it:

\(P_t^+=\sum_{u\text{ preceding }t\text{ in }\sigma_j^+}r_u\), and \(P_t^-=\sum_{u\text{ preceding }t\text{ in }\sigma_j^-}r_u\).

The numbers of type-\(t\) units in the two prefixes are exactly

\(\ell_t^+=\min\{r_t,\max\{0,k-P_t^+\}\}\),

\(\ell_t^-=\min\{r_t,\max\{0,r-k+1-P_t^-\}\}\).

These formulas follow by locating where each prefix ends relative to the type block.

Crucially, both prefixes take initial segments of the same remaining local-label order within each type. Consequently,

\(\lvert A\cap B\cap\{\text{type-}t\text{ units}\}\rvert=\min\{\ell_t^+,\ell_t^-\}\).

Since \(\lvert A\rvert+\lvert B\rvert=r+1\), some type has both \(\ell_t^+>0\) and \(\ell_t^->0\). Choose the smallest-index such type and inspect its first remaining unit, whose label is \((t,n_t-r_t+1)\).

That unit belongs to \(A\cap B\), so this is a valid SBB decision. After its observation, decrement \(r_t\), update the candidate counts, and repeat. A vote for the checked candidate decreases both \(r\) and \(k\); another vote decreases only \(r\), exactly as required by SBB recursion.

Computing the two prefix-count arrays and finding the selected type takes two scans and one additional scan of the support. There is no operation proportional to a prefix’s number of units.

4. Exact simulation of the majority algorithm

Preprocess one further type permutation in nondecreasing cost order, again breaking ties by type index.

While more than two candidates remain possible winners, choose the first nonempty type in that permutation and inspect its first remaining unit. This exactly implements the first phase on the conceptual expansion.

At the phase transition, select the surviving candidates in decreasing order of \(a_j\), breaking ties by candidate index. Check the first using the compressed SBB procedure. If it does not win, check the second if it remains possible. Stop whenever one of the prescribed global certificates holds.

Every selected inspection is therefore an inspection allowed by the explicit algorithm, with fixed valid tie choices. The compressed strategy is an exact simulation of that algorithm on the conceptual expansion.

All hypotheses of the approximation theorem hold: votes are independent, every categorical probability is positive, the outcome is strict absolute majority, and the inspection costs in this section are positive. The conceptual expansion has the same optimum as the support instance. Hence the compressed strategy has expected cost at most \(4\operatorname{OPT}\).

For \(m=2\), the first phase is empty. The threshold subroutine handles every \(N\geq1\).

5. Zero inspection costs

We now extend the guarantee to the permitted costs \(c_t\geq0\).

If every cost is zero, inspect units in any fixed type order until certification. Its cost and \(\operatorname{OPT}\) are both zero.

Otherwise, define

\(c_{\min}=\min\{c_t:c_t>0\}\),

\(\rho=\min_{t,j}\{p_{t,j},1-p_{t,j}\}\),

\(\delta_0=c_{\min}\rho/2\).

Both \(c_{\min}\) and \(\rho\) are positive. For \(0<\delta<\delta_0\), replace each zero type cost by \(\delta\), keeping positive type costs unchanged. Denote these positive type costs by \(c_t^{(\delta)}\); a unit then costs \(c_t^{(\delta)}/N\).

With our fixed tie rules, all ordering permutations are independent of \(\delta\) throughout this interval:

  • In cost order, the formerly zero-cost types precede all positive-cost types.
  • For either threshold denominator \(q=p_{t,j}\) or \(q=1-p_{t,j}\), a formerly zero-cost type has ratio \(\delta/q\leq\delta/\rho<c_{\min}/2\). A positive-cost type has ratio \(c_t/q\geq c_{\min}\). Thus the two groups are strictly separated.
  • Within the formerly zero-cost group, comparing \(\delta/q\) ratios is independent of the common positive factor \(\delta\). Within the positive-cost group, nothing changes.

The compressed strategy depends on costs only through these permutations. Its decisions, on every realization, are therefore independent of \(\delta\) in this interval. Call this fixed strategy \(\mathcal A\). It can be implemented using the single rational proxy \(\delta_*=\delta_0/2\).

Let \(C_\delta(\mathcal A)\) be its total cost under the perturbed unit costs, and let \(\operatorname{OPT}_\delta\) be the corresponding optimum. The positive-cost theorem gives

\(\mathbb E[C_\delta(\mathcal A)]\leq4\operatorname{OPT}_\delta\).

An optimal original strategy inspects at most \(N\) units. Perturbation adds at most \(\delta/N\) per inspection, so using that strategy in the perturbed instance proves

\(\operatorname{OPT}_\delta\leq\operatorname{OPT}+\delta\).

Also \(C_0(\mathcal A)\leq C_\delta(\mathcal A)\) on every realization. Consequently, for every \(0<\delta<\delta_0\),

\(\mathbb E[C_0(\mathcal A)]\leq4\operatorname{OPT}+4\delta\).

The strategy on the left is fixed, so taking \(\delta\) arbitrarily close to zero yields \(\mathbb E[C_0(\mathcal A)]\leq4\operatorname{OPT}\). This includes instances with \(\operatorname{OPT}=0\).

6. Bit complexity and exact certification

Preprocessing computes the multiplicities and sorts \(2m+1\) lists, each of length \(\tau\). This uses \(O(m\tau\log(\tau+1))\) comparisons.

All comparisons are exact rational comparisons. For example, comparing \(c_t/p_{t,j}\) with \(c_u/p_{u,j}\) reduces to comparing \(c_t p_{u,j}\) with \(c_u p_{t,j}\), followed by integer cross multiplication. The proxy \(\delta_*=c_{\min}\rho/4\) has bit length polynomial in \(L\).

All counters have \(O(\log(N+1))\) bits. Each decision uses \(O(m+\tau)\) arithmetic operations: checking certificates and possible winners, scanning a cost order or the two threshold orders, and updating state. Every operand has bit length polynomial in \(L+\log(N+1)\). Thus preprocessing and each next-inspection decision satisfy the requested uniform polynomial bound. The stored state also has polynomial size.

Finally, every inspection removes one remaining unit. After at most \(N\) inspections, either some \(a_j\geq H\), or every \(a_j\leq H-1\), in which case \(N-a_j\geq N-H+1=Q\) for every \(j\). Thus certification is exact for every realization, including ties.

The number of inspections may be \(N\); the claimed bound concerns preprocessing and computation per inspection, as requested.

\[ a_j+r\geq H \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s-a_j<Q. \]

\[ \frac{\delta}{q}\leq\frac{\delta}{\rho} <\frac{c_{\min}}2, \qquad \frac{c_t}{q}\geq c_{\min} \]
