The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), \(\alpha > 1\), a finite set of complete voter types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu\), and either full type-to-candidate distances or ordinal rankings together with compatible pseudometric distances, choose a committee \(R \subseteq C\) of size \(k\) such that for every integer \(j\) with \(j\alpha/k \le 1\) and every subdistribution \(\sigma \le \mu\) of mass at least \(j\alpha/k\), the weighted cost of the best \(j\) members of \(R\) for \(\sigma\) is at most \(\gamma(\alpha) = 1 + (7 + \sqrt{41})\alpha/(2(\alpha-1))\) times the weighted cost of the best \(j\) candidates in \(C\) for \(\sigma\); determine whether such an \(R\) exists, construct one, and study the optimum achievable factor.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\) candidates, let \(1\le k<m\), and let \(\alpha>1\) be a binary rational.
A society is given by its support: the list \(T\) of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau=|T|\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
In the cardinal model, all nonnegative rational distances \(d(t,c)\) are given and promised to extend to a pseudometric on \(T\cup C\). In the ordinal model, only a strict complete ranking \(\succ_t\) for each type is given; a compatible pseudometric satisfies \(c\succ_t c'\Rightarrow d(t,c)\le d(t,c')\), allowing distance ties.
A coalition is any real subdistribution \(0\le\sigma_t\le\mu_t\), with mass \(|\sigma|=\sum_t\sigma_t\). For \(R\subseteq C\) of size \(k\), define \(A_j(R,\sigma;d)=\sum_t\sigma_t\min_{Q\subseteq R,\,|Q|=j}\sum_{c\in Q}d(t,c)\) and \(B_j(\sigma;d)=\min_{Q\subseteq C,\,|Q|=j}\sum_t\sigma_t\sum_{c\in Q}d(t,c)\).
Call \(R\) feasible if \(A_j(R,\sigma;d)\le\gamma(\alpha)B_j(\sigma;d)\), where \(\gamma(\alpha)=1+\frac{(7+\sqrt{41})\alpha}{2(\alpha-1)}\), for every integer \(1\le j\le k\) and coalition satisfying \(|\sigma|\ge j\alpha/k\). In the ordinal model, the same \(R\) must satisfy these inequalities for every compatible pseudometric. The inequality applies also when \(B_j=0\).

For each information model, is deciding whether a feasible committee exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), with construction in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\) whenever one exists, or is the decision problem \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For explicitly listed finite voters, Expanding Approvals constructs a committee with the stated factor using only rankings in \(\widetilde{O}(mn)\) time, with coalition thresholds \(j\alpha\lceil n/k\rceil\). [Verified full version](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.10369v2).
No result settling exactly the support-encoded mass problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total mass to one and used the unrounded mass quota 1/k, as required by the graded coalition threshold.
  • Required 1 ≤ k < m and binary rational α > 1; retained the permitted vacuous regime α > k.
  • Interpreted committee cost as each type's individually best j members, while the comparison uses one common set of j candidates for the coalition.
  • Allowed arbitrary real coalition masses within each type's available mass.
  • Specified cardinal distances as nonnegative binary rationals promised to admit a pseudometric extension.
  • Interpreted ordinal input as rankings alone, with one committee required to work for every compatible pseudometric; strict rankings may resolve equal distances arbitrarily.
  • Used the multiplicative inequality directly when the comparison cost is zero.

1. Result

Both information models are in \(\mathrm{P}\). More strongly, every promised instance has a feasible committee, and one can construct such a committee in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\).

The proof uses an exact compressed simulation of Expanding Approvals. Replication removes the ceiling in the finite-voter threshold, while invariance under further replication extends the guarantee to all real coalitions.

2. The finite-voter theorem used

We use the supplied result precisely as follows. On an electorate of \(n\) explicitly listed voters, Expanding Approvals selects a committee \(R\) of size \(k\). For every compatible pseudometric, every \(\alpha>1\), every \(1\le j\le k\), and every voter subset \(S\) satisfying

\[ |S|\ge j\alpha\lceil n/k\rceil, \]

the committee satisfies

\[ \sum_{v\in S}\min_{\substack{Q\subseteq R\\|Q|=j}} \sum_{c\in Q}d(v,c) \le \gamma(\alpha) \min_{\substack{Q\subseteq C\\|Q|=j}} \sum_{v\in S}\sum_{c\in Q}d(v,c). \]

We use Algorithm 1, with a fixed voter order and fixed arbitrary completion rule. Its sequential implementation elects a candidate when its neighborhood reaches exactly \(\lceil n/k\rceil\), and removes that neighborhood. The theorem allows pseudometrics, distance ties compatible with strict rankings, and the displayed inequality without division by its right-hand side. These are the conventions of [Algorithm 1, Theorem 4.1, and Section 3 of the supplied full version](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.10369v2).

3. A polynomial compressed implementation

First consider the ordinal model. Fix orders on the types and candidates. Write each mass as \(\mu_t=a_t/b_t\), where \(a_t,b_t\) are positive integers, and define

\[ D=k\prod_{t\in T}b_t,\qquad p=D/k. \]

Thus \(D\mu_t\) and \(p\) are integers. Conceptually, replace type \(t\) by \(D\mu_t\) identical voters and place these voters consecutively in the fixed type order. This electorate has exactly \(D\) voters, and its finite-voter quota is

\[ \lceil D/k\rceil=D/k=p. \]

We will simulate this electorate without listing its voters.

Maintain:

  • An integer \(u_t\), initially \(D\mu_t\), counting the uncovered voters of type \(t\).
  • A Boolean flag \(F_{t,c}\), initially false, for each type and candidate.
  • The selected committee \(R\), initially empty.

At the boundaries between type blocks, the meaning of \(F_{t,c}\), for an unselected candidate \(c\), is that all currently uncovered voters of type \(t\) belong to \(c\)'s neighborhood. A false flag means that none belong. Flags for types with \(u_t=0\) are immaterial.

For each rank \(q=1,\ldots,m\), process the types in their fixed order. For the current type \(t\):

  • If \(u_t=0\), skip the block.
  • Let \(c\) be the candidate ranked \(q\)-th by type \(t\). If \(c\in R\), skip the block.
  • Compute

\[ s=\sum_{\substack{b\in T\\F_{b,c}\text{ is true}}}u_b. \]

  • If \(s+u_t<p\), set \(F_{t,c}\) to true.
  • Otherwise, put \(x=p-s\), add \(c\) to \(R\), set \(u_b=0\) for every type \(b\) with \(F_{b,c}\) true, and replace \(u_t\) by \(u_t-x\).

Leave the flags for other candidates unchanged. At termination, complete \(R\) to size \(k\), if necessary, using the fixed candidate order.

We now prove that these operations are well-defined and simulate the finite algorithm exactly.

At every type-block boundary, each unselected neighborhood has size strictly less than \(p\): additions elect its candidate immediately upon reaching \(p\), and removals only decrease other neighborhoods. Consequently, \(s<p\).

Furthermore, \(F_{t,c}\) is false when step 3 is reached. Indeed, type \(t\) has not previously processed candidate \(c\): that candidate occurs at exactly rank \(q\), and the current block is its first insertion opportunity.

Suppose first that \(s+u_t<p\). Processing the uncovered clones of \(t\) one at a time inserts all of them into \(c\)'s neighborhood without an election. Setting \(F_{t,c}\) to true therefore gives exactly the finite algorithm's state.

Otherwise,

\[ 1\le x=p-s\le u_t. \]

The first \(x\) uncovered clones of \(t\) bring \(c\)'s neighborhood to quota. At that moment, its neighborhood consists precisely of these \(x\) clones and all uncovered clones of the types already flagged for \(c\). Thus the finite algorithm removes exactly the voters specified in step 5. All remaining clones of \(t\) skip their insertion because \(c\) is now selected.

For every other unselected candidate, removing these voters preserves the all-or-none membership property within each type: a current type loses a prefix of its uncovered clones, and another participating type loses all its uncovered clones. Hence its unchanged flag, together with the updated \(u_t\), still describes its neighborhood correctly.

Induction over the rank and type blocks proves exact simulation. Each election removes exactly \(p\) voters, so there are at most \(D/p=k\) elections. The completion step consequently produces a committee of size \(k\), exactly as in the finite algorithm.

4. The output is invariant under replication

Replace \(D\) by \(rD\), for any positive integer \(r\). The resulting quota and initial uncovered counts are \(rp\) and \(rD\mu_t\).

Inductively, the two compressed runs have identical flags and committees, while their uncovered counts differ by the factor \(r\). Their computed neighborhood sizes therefore differ by that factor as well. In particular,

\[ s+u_t<p \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad rs+ru_t<rp. \]

When an election occurs, the amount removed from the current type becomes

\[ rp-rs=r(p-s)=rx. \]

The updates preserve the induction hypothesis. The fixed completion rule also agrees between the runs.

Therefore, every replication size \(rD\) produces the same committee \(R\). This property will let us represent any rational coalition without changing the output.

5. Guarantee for every rational coalition and compatible pseudometric

Fix an arbitrary compatible pseudometric \(d\) on \(T\cup C\), an integer \(1\le j\le k\), and a rational subdistribution \(\sigma\) satisfying

\[ 0\le\sigma_t\le\mu_t, \qquad |\sigma|\ge j\alpha/k. \]

Choose a positive integer \(r\) such that \(n=rD\) makes every \(n\sigma_t\) integral. Create \(n\mu_t\) clones of each type and let \(S\) contain exactly \(n\sigma_t\) clones of type \(t\). Such a subset exists because \(0\le n\sigma_t\le n\mu_t\).

To define distances, map every clone of type \(t\) to \(t\), and map every candidate to itself. Define the distance between any two replicated points to be the \(d\)-distance between their images. This is a pseudometric: symmetry, nonnegativity, the zero diagonal, and the triangle inequality follow directly from the corresponding properties of \(d\). It is compatible with every clone's ranking.

Because \(k\) divides \(n\),

\[ |S|=n|\sigma| \ge j\alpha n/k =j\alpha\lceil n/k\rceil. \]

By Sections 3 and 4, the finite algorithm on this electorate returns our fixed committee \(R\). Its theorem therefore applies to \(S\).

Grouping the left-hand sum by type gives exactly \(nA_j(R,\sigma;d)\). For each fixed alternative \(Q\), grouping its sum gives

\[ \sum_{v\in S}\sum_{c\in Q}d(v,c) = n\sum_{t\in T}\sigma_t\sum_{c\in Q}d(t,c). \]

Taking the minimum over \(Q\) consequently gives \(nB_j(\sigma;d)\). Dividing the finite inequality by \(n>0\) proves

\[ A_j(R,\sigma;d)\le\gamma(\alpha)B_j(\sigma;d). \]

The committee is independent of both \(\sigma\) and \(d\). Since the compatible pseudometric was arbitrary, this establishes the required ordinal guarantee for every rational coalition, simultaneously for all compatible pseudometrics.

6. Extension to all real coalitions

Fix any real subdistribution \(\sigma\) meeting the threshold. For each positive integer \(\ell\), define the rational subdistribution

\[ \sigma_t^{(\ell)} = \min\left\{\mu_t,\frac{\lceil\ell\sigma_t\rceil}{\ell}\right\}. \]

Both entries in this minimum are at least \(\sigma_t\). Hence

\[ \sigma_t\le\sigma_t^{(\ell)}\le\mu_t, \qquad 0\le\sigma_t^{(\ell)}-\sigma_t\le1/\ell. \]

In particular,

\[ |\sigma^{(\ell)}|\ge|\sigma|\ge j\alpha/k, \]

so Section 5 applies to every \(\sigma^{(\ell)}\).

For fixed \(R,j,d\), the function \(A_j(R,\sigma;d)\) is linear in \(\sigma\): its coefficient for type \(t\) is the fixed sum of that type's \(j\) smallest distances within \(R\). The function \(B_j(\sigma;d)\) is continuous, being the minimum of finitely many linear functions of \(\sigma\).

Passing to the limit in the rational-coalition inequalities gives

\[ A_j(R,\sigma;d)\le\gamma(\alpha)B_j(\sigma;d). \]

This also covers \(B_j(\sigma;d)=0\). No division by \(B_j\) was used; the inequality then forces \(A_j(R,\sigma;d)=0\).

Thus \(R\) is feasible in the ordinal model.

7. Bit complexity and the cardinal model

Although \(D\) may be numerically large, its bit length satisfies

\[ \log_2D=\log_2k+\sum_{t\in T}\log_2b_t=O(L+\log k). \]

All uncovered counts, quotas, neighborhood sizes, and removal amounts are integers between zero and \(D\). Their arithmetic therefore has polynomial bit complexity.

There are \(m\tau\) type blocks. Computing a neighborhood size by scanning the types uses at most \(\tau\) additions per block; an election requires at most \(\tau\) updates. Thus the simulation uses

\[ O(m\tau^2+k\tau) \]

integer operations on numbers of polynomial bit length. Computing the initial counts and storing the rankings and flags also take polynomial time and space. Crucially, the algorithm never lists the replicated voters.

In the cardinal model, sort each type's candidates by the given rational distances, breaking equal-distance ties by a fixed candidate order. These strict rankings are compatible with every promised pseudometric extension of the given distances. Exact rational comparisons and sorting take polynomial time. Apply the ordinal construction to these rankings.

The resulting committee satisfies the desired inequalities for the given distances, since all quantities \(A_j\) and \(B_j\) depend only on the supplied type-to-candidate distances.

Consequently, both models admit polynomial-time construction on every promised instance, and their existence decision problems always answer yes.
