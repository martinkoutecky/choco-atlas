The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational mass vector \(\mu\), population scale \(N\), explicit piecewise-uniform valuation densities \(\nu_t\) on a cake interval \([0,N]\), and threshold \(B\), decide whether there are assignment measures \(\xi_t\) over connected intervals \(I\) such that \(\xi_t(\mathcal I)=N\mu_t\), each mass unit receives one interval, \(\sum_t\int \mathbf{1}_{x\in I}\,d\xi_t(I)\leq 1\) almost everywhere, and \(v_t(I)=\int_I\nu_t(x)\,dx\geq B\) for \(\xi_t\)-almost every assigned interval.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The input also gives a positive binary rational population scale \(N\), a binary rational threshold \(B\), and explicit densities \(\nu_t\) on \([0,N]\). Each density is a step function taking only values \(0\) and \(c_t>0\), with rational breakpoints and heights, and satisfies \(\int_0^N\nu_t(x)\,dx=1\).
Let \(m\) be the number of cells in the common breakpoint partition and \(L\) the total input bit length. State running times in \(m,\tau,L\), including any dependence on the numerical value of \(N\).
Identify the space \(\mathcal I\) of connected intervals with \(\{(a,b):0\leq a\leq b\leq N\}\), equipped with its Borel sets; degenerate intervals are allowed. Write \(v_t(I)=\int_I\nu_t(x)\,dx\).
An allocation consists of finite nonnegative Borel measures \(\xi_t\) on \(\mathcal I\), with \(\xi_t(\mathcal I)=N\mu_t\): every unit of population mass receives one interval. Require \(\sum_{t\in T}\int_{\mathcal I}\mathbf1_{\{x\in I\}}\,d\xi_t(I)\leq1\) for Lebesgue-almost every \(x\), and \(v_t(I)\geq B\) for \(\xi_t\)-almost every \(I\). Unused cake is permitted.

Is this assignment-measure feasibility problem in \(\mathrm P\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For a finite list of agents, connected egalitarian threshold feasibility is strongly \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even with explicit piecewise-uniform valuations; the ordered indivisible-item variant is also strongly \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. [Aumann–Dombb–Hassidim](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1205.3982)
No complexity classification for exactly the assignment-measure formulation above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive type masses to sum to one, so total population mass is N.
  • Specified N as a positive binary rational scale and B as an arbitrary binary rational threshold; type population masses need not be integers.
  • Carried over nonnegative densities and unit total valuation for every type; piecewise-uniform means a type-specific positive height on its desired intervals and zero elsewhere.
  • Specified rational breakpoints and heights, defined m through their common partition, and defined L as total input bit length.
  • Specified assignment measures as finite nonnegative Borel measures on the endpoint triangle, allowing degenerate intervals as the finite-agent definition does.
  • Retained the graded question's capacity inequality and permission to leave cake unused.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

1. Proven classification. The assignment-measure feasibility problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions.

This holds even when every density is uniform on a single connected support interval, \(m=\tau\), and \(\tau\leq N<2\tau\). The masses and threshold in the construction are dyadic rationals. Consequently, an algorithm running in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L,N)\) time would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

The proof below addresses arbitrary finite Borel assignment measures directly.

2. A lemma extracting an exact tiling from fractional coverage. Let \(H>0\), and let \(d_1,\ldots,d_r>0\). For each \(j\), let \(\eta_j\) be a finite nonnegative Borel measure supported on intervals \([a,a+d_j]\subseteq[0,H]\). Suppose their aggregate coverage is exactly one almost everywhere:

\(\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^r\int \mathbf 1_{\{x\in I\}}\,d\eta_j(I)=1\quad\text{for almost every }x\in(0,H).\)

Then there is a finite sequence of lengths from \(\{d_1,\ldots,d_r\}\), allowing repetitions, whose sum is \(H\).

To prove this, let \(S\) and \(E\) be the aggregate start and end measures. Extend the coverage function \(g\) by zero outside \([0,H]\). For every smooth compactly supported function \(\varphi\), Fubini’s theorem and the fundamental theorem of calculus give

\(\displaystyle \int_{\mathbb R}g(x)\varphi'(x)\,dx =\sum_j\int\bigl(\varphi(a+d_j)-\varphi(a)\bigr)\,d\eta_j =\int\varphi\,d(E-S).\)

On the other hand, the assumed coverage gives

\(\displaystyle \int_{\mathbb R}g(x)\varphi'(x)\,dx=\varphi(H)-\varphi(0).\)

These identities imply the equality of finite signed measures

\(\displaystyle S-E=\delta_0-\delta_H.\)

Indeed, smooth test functions determine finite measures on a compact interval, by their uniform density in the continuous functions.

All interval lengths are positive, so \(E(\{0\})=0\); hence \(S(\{0\})=1\). Some type therefore has a positive start atom at \(0\), producing a positive end atom at its right endpoint \(x_1\).

Whenever a positive end atom occurs at \(x<H\), the measure identity gives \(S(\{x\})=E(\{x\})>0\). Thus an interval with positive atomic mass starts there. Repeatedly following such intervals produces strictly increasing endpoints, each increment at least \(\min_j d_j>0\), and every endpoint lies in \([0,H]\). The process must therefore reach \(H\) after finitely many steps. Those increments give the asserted exact tiling.

This lemma requires neither finite support nor an assumed decomposition into integral allocations.

3. Arithmetic encoding of PARTITION. We reduce from PARTITION with positive integers \(w_1,\ldots,w_n\), where \(n\geq2\) and \(\sum_i w_i=2W\). The question is whether some subset sums to \(W\). PARTITION is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete; odd totals and lists with fewer than two entries can be handled by elementary preprocessing, so this restriction preserves hardness. See the [MIT lecture notes on NP-completeness](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-046j-design-and-analysis-of-algorithms-spring-2015/f2829e57caade7e7f21d765b8e7b9058_MIT6_046JS15_lec16.pdf).

Define

\(\displaystyle R=n+1,\qquad D=2nW+1,\qquad e_i=R^{i-1},\qquad E_*=\sum_{i=1}^n e_i.\)

For each \(i\), introduce two numbers

\(\displaystyle a_i^0=De_i,\qquad a_i^1=De_i+w_i.\)

Set

\(\displaystyle A=\max_{i,\epsilon}a_i^\epsilon,\qquad H_*=nA+DE_*+W+1,\)

and define the interval-length integers and target

\(\displaystyle p_i^\epsilon=H_*+a_i^\epsilon,\qquad C=nH_*+DE_*+W.\)

They satisfy

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^n(p_i^0+p_i^1)=2C.\)

We claim that every nonnegative integer solution of

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i,\epsilon}q_i^\epsilon p_i^\epsilon=C\)

chooses exactly one number from each pair and identifies a PARTITION solution.

Let \(q=\sum_{i,\epsilon}q_i^\epsilon\). If \(q\leq n-1\), then

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i,\epsilon}q_i^\epsilon p_i^\epsilon \leq(n-1)(H_*+A)<nH_*<C,\)

because \(H_*>nA\). If \(q\geq n+1\), then

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i,\epsilon}q_i^\epsilon p_i^\epsilon \geq(n+1)H_*>C,\)

because \(H_*>DE_*+W\). Thus \(q=n\).

Subtracting \(nH_*\) gives

\(\displaystyle D\sum_i(q_i^0+q_i^1)e_i+\sum_iq_i^1w_i=DE_*+W.\)

The quantity \(z=\sum_iq_i^1w_i\) satisfies \(0\leq z\leq2nW<D\), and \(0<W<D\). Reduction modulo \(D\) therefore gives \(z=W\), followed by

\(\displaystyle \sum_i(q_i^0+q_i^1)R^{i-1}=\sum_iR^{i-1}.\)

Every coefficient \(q_i^0+q_i^1\) lies between \(0\) and \(n=R-1\). Uniqueness of base-\(R\) representation gives

\(\displaystyle q_i^0+q_i^1=1\quad\text{for every }i.\)

Consequently, \(\{i:q_i^1=1\}\) sums to \(W\).

Conversely, a subset summing to \(W\) selects one number from each pair with total \(C\). The complementary choices also total \(C\).

4. Construction of the society. Let \(P_*=\max_{i,\epsilon}p_i^\epsilon\), and let \(k\) be the smallest power of two satisfying \(k\geq n+1\). Define

\(\displaystyle N=2k,\qquad B=\frac1k,\qquad d_i^\epsilon=\frac{p_i^\epsilon}{P_*},\qquad \ell=\frac C{P_*}.\)

Since \(C<(n+1)H_*\leq k p_i^\epsilon\), we have

\(\displaystyle 0<\ell<k d_i^\epsilon\leq k<N.\)

There are \(2n+2\) types:

  • For every \(i,\epsilon\), an item type with mass \(\mu_i^\epsilon=1/(4k)\) and density \(\nu_i^\epsilon(x)=1/(k d_i^\epsilon)\) on \([0,kd_i^\epsilon]\), and zero elsewhere.
  • A blocker type with mass \(\mu_b=1/2\) and density \(1/(k-\ell)\) on \([\ell,k]\), and zero elsewhere.
  • A dummy type with mass \(\mu_d=(k-n)/(2k)\) and density \(1/k\) on \([k,2k]\), and zero elsewhere.

Each density integrates to one. All masses are positive, and their sum is

\(\displaystyle \frac{n}{2k}+\frac12+\frac{k-n}{2k}=1.\)

The required assignment masses are therefore

\(\displaystyle N\mu_i^\epsilon=\frac12,\qquad N\mu_b=k,\qquad N\mu_d=k-n.\)

Every item type values all of \([0,\ell]\) uniformly. An interval contained there meets its threshold precisely when its length is at least \(d_i^\epsilon\).

5. Equivalence of the two instances. First suppose the PARTITION instance is a YES-instance.

Its selected and complementary choices each have total length \(C\) in the integer encoding. After division by \(P_*\), each choice therefore tiles \([0,\ell]\). Construct both tilings, and give every item interval in each tiling assignment mass \(1/2\).

Every item type appears in exactly one tiling, so it receives its required mass \(1/2\). Its interval has length \(d_i^\epsilon\), giving value exactly \(B\). The aggregate item coverage is one almost everywhere on \([0,\ell]\).

For the blocker, partition \([\ell,k]\) into \(k\) equal intervals and place assignment mass one on each. Each has value \(1/k=B\). For the dummy, use \(k-n\) disjoint unit intervals inside \([k,2k]\), each with assignment mass one and value \(1/k=B\).

These measures satisfy all demands and the capacity constraint. The finitely many shared endpoints do not affect the almost-everywhere requirement.

Now suppose the constructed society admits arbitrary feasible assignment measures. Write \(g_t\) for each type’s coverage function.

The blocker’s threshold and required mass imply, by Tonelli’s theorem,

\(\displaystyle 1=kB\leq\int v_b(I)\,d\xi_b(I) =\frac1{k-\ell}\int_\ell^k g_b(x)\,dx\leq1.\)

The final inequality follows from the capacity constraint. Equality throughout implies \(g_b(x)=1\) almost everywhere on \((\ell,k)\). All other types consequently have zero coverage there almost everywhere.

In particular, for each item type,

\(\displaystyle \int |I\cap(\ell,k)|\,d\xi_i^\epsilon(I)=0.\)

Thus almost every item interval has zero-length intersection with the blocker region. Its value is positive, and its valued support is a prefix ending at or before \(k\); consequently, it must be contained in \([0,\ell]\). Indeed, any positive-valued interval extending beyond \(\ell\) would intersect \((\ell,k)\) in positive length.

For such intervals, the threshold requires \(|I|\geq d_i^\epsilon\). Capacity on \([0,\ell]\) now gives

\(\displaystyle \ell \geq\sum_{i,\epsilon}\int |I|\,d\xi_i^\epsilon(I) \geq\frac12\sum_{i,\epsilon}d_i^\epsilon =\frac C{P_*} =\ell.\)

Both inequalities are therefore equalities. Because all excess lengths are nonnegative, item intervals have length exactly \(d_i^\epsilon\) almost surely. Their aggregate coverage is also exactly one almost everywhere on \([0,\ell]\): it is bounded by one and its integral equals \(\ell\).

Apply the tiling lemma from Section 2. It supplies a finite sequence of item lengths summing exactly to \(\ell\). Multiplying by \(P_*\) supplies nonnegative integer counts satisfying

\(\displaystyle \sum_{i,\epsilon}q_i^\epsilon p_i^\epsilon=C.\)

Section 3 proves that these counts select exactly one number from every pair and yield a subset of the original weights summing to \(W\). The PARTITION instance is therefore a YES-instance.

This establishes both directions, including the direction involving arbitrary Borel measures.

6. Encoding size and running-time consequence. Let

\(\displaystyle h=O\bigl(n\log(n+1)+\log(W+1)\bigr).\)

All integers in the arithmetic encoding have \(O(h)\) bits. All constructed rational parameters also have \(O(h)\) bits; for example, item support endpoints and heights are

\(\displaystyle \frac{k p_i^\epsilon}{P_*}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{P_*}{k p_i^\epsilon}.\)

The reduction runs in \(n\operatorname{poly}(h)\) bit time and produces \(L=O(nh)\) input bits using the explicit step-function descriptions.

The \(2n\) item support endpoints are distinct: within each pair this follows from \(w_i>0\), and between consecutive pairs it follows from \(D(e_{i+1}-e_i)>w_i\). All lie strictly above \(\ell\), and the largest is \(k\). Including \(0,\ell,N\), the common partition therefore has

\(\displaystyle m=2n+2=\tau.\)

Moreover, \(n+1\leq k\leq2n\), so

\(\displaystyle \tau\leq N=2k<2\tau.\)

Hence the hardness persists when the numerical value of \(N\) is linear in both \(m\) and \(\tau\). The established classification is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness; the reduction uses binary rational precision and does not establish strong \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.
