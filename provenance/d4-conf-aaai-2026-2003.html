The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) candidates, a committee bound \(k\), and \(\tau\) voter types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\) and rational ballots \(u_t:C\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), candidates arrive in a uniformly random order. When \(c\) arrives, a policy observes \(u_t(c)\) for each type and irrevocably accepts or rejects \(c\), selecting at most \(k\) candidates. For \(W\subseteq C\), define \(\mathrm{NW}_{\mu}(W)=\sum_t\mu_t\log(1+\sum_{c\in W}u_t(c))\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}=\max_{|W|\le k}\mathrm{NW}_{\mu}(W)\). Does there exist a policy running in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length, such that \(\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{NW}_{\mu}(W)]\ge ((1-1/e)/7)\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\) candidates and let \(k\) be an integer with \(2\le k<m\). A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\ge1\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has an additive ballot \(u_t:C\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), encoded in binary. Let \(L\) be the total bit length of the instance, including all ballots.
The instance is fixed before candidates arrive in a uniformly random permutation. Initially a policy knows \(m,k,\tau\) and the masses; ballot entries for a candidate are revealed only upon its arrival. Each candidate must immediately be accepted or rejected irrevocably, with at most \(k\) acceptances. Internal randomization is allowed.
For \(W\subseteq C\), define \(\mathrm{NW}_{\mu}(W)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\log(1+\sum_{c\in W}u_t(c))\), using the natural logarithm, and \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}=\max_{W\subseteq C,\,|W|\le k}\mathrm{NW}_{\mu}(W)\).
Computation uses the ordinary bit model; exact real arithmetic and exact logarithmic comparison oracles are not supplied. A policy may resolve ties by any fixed or randomized rule.

Does there exist an online policy with total running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\) that, for every such instance, satisfies \(\mathbb E[\mathrm{NW}_{\mu}(W)]\ge\frac{1-1/e}{7}\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}\), where expectation includes arrival order and internal randomness?

For finitely many equally weighted voters, Online Nash Rule is stated to run in polynomial time and achieve the same expected approximation ratio. [Papasotiropoulos and Pishbin](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.23097v1)
For monotone nonnegative submodular objectives, the same ratio is known in the secretary model with value-oracle access restricted to arrived candidates; this is an oracle result, rather than a bit-complexity result for rational type masses. [Bateni, Hajiaghayi, and Zadimoghaddam](https://mhbateni.com/academic/pubs/secretary-10.pdf)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restored the discrete domain restriction that the committee bound is an integer satisfying 2 ≤ k < m.
  • Removed zero-mass entries so that the listed types are exactly the positive-mass support.
  • Made the information restriction explicit: masses and population parameters are known initially, but future ballot entries are hidden until arrival.
  • Interpreted polynomial running time in the ordinary bit model, with L counting the complete encoded instance and without exact logarithmic comparison oracles.
  • Allowed internal randomization and arbitrary policy tie resolution; included internal randomness in the expectation.
  • Specified natural logarithms and a guarantee for every fixed instance, with no additional divisibility restriction on m.

Result

Yes. There is a deterministic online policy, using polynomial-time rational arithmetic, such that
\( \mathbb E[\mathrm{NW}_{\mu}(W)]\ge \frac{99}{998}\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}. \)
This is stronger than the requested guarantee.

Consecutive-block selection underlies the cited [Online Nash Rule](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.23097v1) and [submodular secretary algorithm](https://mhbateni.com/academic/pubs/secretary-10.pdf). The proof below establishes the guarantee directly, including the bit complexity of approximate marginal comparisons.

1. The objective and its marginal gains

Write \(f(W)=\mathrm{NW}_{\mu}(W)\), and define
\(f(c\mid A)=f(A\cup\{c\})-f(A)\).

For \(c\notin A\), additivity gives
\( \displaystyle f(c\mid A)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\log\!\left(1+\frac{u_t(c)}{1+\sum_{d\in A}u_t(d)}\right). \)

Every summand is nonnegative. Thus \(f(\varnothing)=0\), and \(f\) is nonnegative and monotone.

If \(A\subseteq B\) and \(c\notin B\), then
\( \displaystyle \frac{u_t(c)}{1+\sum_{d\in A}u_t(d)} \ge \frac{u_t(c)}{1+\sum_{d\in B}u_t(d)}. \)
Since logarithm is increasing and the masses are positive, \(f(c\mid A)\ge f(c\mid B)\). This is the diminishing-marginals definition of submodularity.

2. A rational approximation to logarithm

We construct a rational-valued function \(h\), computable on rational inputs \(y\ge1\), satisfying
\( \displaystyle \rho\log y\le h(y)\le\log y,\qquad \rho=\frac{99}{100}. \)

First let \(1\le q\le2\), set \(z=(q-1)/(q+1)\), and define
\( \displaystyle H(q)=2z+\frac{2z^3}{3}. \)

Here \(0\le z\le1/3\). Integrating the uniformly convergent geometric series on this interval gives
\( \displaystyle \log q=2\int_0^z\frac{dv}{1-v^2} =2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2j+1}}{2j+1}. \)

Consequently,
\( \displaystyle 0\le\log q-H(q)\le\frac{2z^5}{5(1-z^2)}. \)
For \(z>0\), using \(\log q\ge2z\), we obtain
\( \displaystyle \frac{\log q-H(q)}{\log q} \le\frac{z^4}{5(1-z^2)}\le\frac1{360}. \)
For \(z=0\), both values are zero. Hence
\( \displaystyle \frac{359}{360}\log q\le H(q)\le\log q. \)

For arbitrary rational \(y\ge1\), find the integer \(a\ge0\) satisfying \(2^a\le y<2^{a+1}\), and put \(q=y/2^a\). Define
\( \displaystyle h(y)=aH(2)+H(q). \)

Because \(\log y=a\log2+\log q\) and both terms are nonnegative, the preceding bounds imply
\( \displaystyle \frac{359}{360}\log y\le h(y)\le\log y, \)
which proves the claimed bounds with \(\rho=99/100\).

This construction uses only rational arithmetic. The integer \(a\) can be found by comparisons with powers of two; computing a logarithm is unnecessary.

For an arrived candidate \(c\) and a previously accepted set \(A\), define its rational score
\( \displaystyle \widehat g_A(c)= \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t h\!\left(1+\frac{u_t(c)}{1+\sum_{d\in A}u_t(d)}\right). \)

Termwise approximation and nonnegative weights give
\[ \displaystyle \rho f(c\mid A)\le\widehat g_A(c)\le f(c\mid A). \tag{1} \]

3. The online policy

Partition the arrival positions into \(k\) consecutive blocks, with sizes differing by at most one. These boundaries depend only on \(m,k\). Let \(S_i\) denote the candidates arriving in block \(i\), and let \(T_i\) be the accepted set after that block, with \(T_0=\varnothing\).

Within block \(i\), scores are computed relative to the fixed set \(T_{i-1}\). Break score ties by any fixed, arrival-independent ordering of candidate identifiers.

For a block of size \(s\):

  • If \(s=1\), accept its candidate.
  • If \(s\ge2\), reject its first \(r=\lfloor s/2\rfloor\) candidates and record their highest score, including the tie-breaking priority.
  • Accept the first subsequent candidate ranked above that recorded candidate, if one exists.
  • Reject every other candidate in the block.

Thus the policy accepts at most one candidate per block, and therefore at most \(k\) candidates. Every score uses only the current candidate’s ballot and previously accepted candidates.

4. Expected gain within a block

Condition on all block memberships and on the complete history preceding block \(i\). The order within the current block remains uniform. Its scores are fixed under this conditioning, because \(T_{i-1}\) is fixed.

For \(s\ge2\), consider the event that the highest-ranked candidate lies after the sample and the second-highest-ranked candidate lies in the sample. On this event, the policy selects the highest-ranked candidate. Its probability is
\( \displaystyle \frac{s-r}{s}\frac{r}{s-1} =\frac{r(s-r)}{s(s-1)}\ge\frac14. \)

Indeed, \(r(s-r)=s^2/4\) when \(s\) is even, and \(r(s-r)=(s^2-1)/4\) when \(s\) is odd; either expression is at least \(s(s-1)/4\). For \(s=1\), selection succeeds with probability one.

Whenever the highest-score candidate is selected, (1) implies that its true marginal is at least
\( \displaystyle \max_{c\in S_i}\widehat g_{T_{i-1}}(c) \ge\rho\max_{c\in S_i}f(c\mid T_{i-1}). \)
On all other events, the gain is nonnegative.

Writing \(\Delta_i=f(T_i)-f(T_{i-1})\), we therefore have, for any designated candidate \(b\in S_i\) whose designation does not depend on the within-block order,
\[ \displaystyle \mathbb E[\Delta_i\mid\text{block memberships, preceding history, designation}] \ge\frac{\rho}{4}f(b\mid T_{i-1}). \tag{2} \]

5. A benchmark with one optimum candidate per block

By monotonicity, fix an optimal set \(O\) of exactly \(k\) candidates.

For analysis only, choose one candidate uniformly from \(O\cap S_i\) whenever this intersection is nonempty. Make these choices independently of the within-block orders. Let \(R\subseteq O\) be the resulting representative set.

We prove
\[ \displaystyle \mathbb E[f(R)]\ge\frac12f(O). \tag{3} \]

Put \(\ell=\lfloor m/k\rfloor\ge1\). Every block has size \(s\ge\ell\). If \(s>m-k\), its intersection with \(O\) is necessarily nonempty. Otherwise, its probability of being empty is
\( \displaystyle \prod_{j=0}^{s-1}\frac{m-k-j}{m-j} \le\left(1-\frac{k}{m}\right)^s \le\left(\frac{\ell}{\ell+1}\right)^\ell \le\frac12. \)

The middle bound uses \(m<k(\ell+1)\). The last bound follows from the binomial inequality
\( (1+1/\ell)^\ell\ge1+\ell(1/\ell)=2 \).

Therefore \(\mathbb E[|R|]\ge k/2\). By symmetry of the uniform permutation and the uniform representative choices, every candidate of \(O\) has the same inclusion probability \(p\). Thus \(p=\mathbb E[|R|]/k\ge1/2\).

To translate this into welfare, order \(O=\{o_1,\ldots,o_k\}\), put \(O_j=\{o_1,\ldots,o_j\}\), and define
\(d_j=f(O_j)-f(O_{j-1})\ge0\).
Telescoping over the members of any realization of \(R\), submodularity gives
\( \displaystyle f(R)\ge\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbf1_{\{o_j\in R\}}d_j. \)
The reason is that the preceding selected members form a subset of \(O_{j-1}\), so their marginal for \(o_j\) is at least \(d_j\).

Taking expectations yields
\( \displaystyle \mathbb E[f(R)]\ge p\sum_{j=1}^{k}d_j =p f(O)\ge\frac12f(O), \)
proving (3). No independence between inclusion events is required.

6. Charging the benchmark to the policy

For each nonempty \(O\cap S_i\), denote its representative by \(b_i\). Monotonicity and submodularity imply, for every realization,
\[ \displaystyle f(R)\le f(T_k\cup R) \le f(T_k)+\sum_i f(b_i\mid T_k) \le f(T_k)+\sum_i f(b_i\mid T_{i-1}), \tag{4} \]
where the sums range over blocks having representatives.

Conditional on block memberships, preceding history, and the representative choices, the current within-block order is uniform. Thus (2) applies to \(b_i\). Summing its expectations gives
\[ \displaystyle \mathbb E[f(T_k)]\ge\beta\, \mathbb E\!\left[\sum_i f(b_i\mid T_{i-1})\right], \qquad \beta=\frac{\rho}{4}=\frac{99}{400}. \tag{5} \]

Let \(F=\mathbb E[f(T_k)]\) and \(G=\mathbb E[f(R)]\). Equations (4)–(5) give \(G\le F+F/\beta\), so, using (3),
\( \displaystyle F\ge\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}G =\frac{99}{499}G \ge\frac{99}{998}\mathrm{OPT}_{\mu}. \)

Finally, \(e<3\), so
\( \displaystyle \frac{1-1/e}{7}<\frac{2}{21}<\frac{99}{998}. \)
The second inequality follows from \(2\cdot998=1996<2079=99\cdot21\). For completeness, \(e<3\) follows from its series and \(n!\ge2^{n-1}\) for \(n\ge2\), with strict inequality for \(n\ge3\).

7. Polynomial bit complexity

Maintain each type’s accepted utility sum exactly as a rational number. A sum of at most \(k\) input rationals has numerator and denominator lengths bounded by \(O(kL+\log k)\), using a product of their denominators.

Consequently, every rational argument of \(h\) has polynomial bit length. For an argument of bit length \(B\), its exponent \(a\) is at most \(B\). Finding \(a\), forming \(2^a\), and evaluating the fixed-degree rational expression \(H\) all take polynomial time and produce polynomial-length rational numbers.

Each candidate score sums \(\tau\) such rational expressions multiplied by the input masses. Rational addition and exact rational comparisons therefore remain polynomial in \(\tau,k,L\). There are at most \(m\tau\) logarithm-approximation evaluations and a polynomial number of other arithmetic operations.

Hence the policy’s total running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\). It does not need to know \(L\) before arrivals, replicate types according to their masses, or perform exact logarithmic comparisons.
