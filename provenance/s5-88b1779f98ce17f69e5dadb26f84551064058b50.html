Proposition 1 — class: other

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite sets of patient-pair types and non-directed-donor types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), directed type-pair compatibility arcs with weights \(w_{tu}\) and independent failure probabilities \(p_{tu}\), and cycle and chain limits \(K,L\), compute a maximum-expected-weight allocation of mass to feasible exchange templates. Each template uses distinct participants, even when a type appears more than once. If \(r_{tq}\) is the number of participants of type \(t\) used by template \(q\), and \(U(q)\) is its expected weight—\(U(c)=(\sum_{e\in c}w_e)\prod_{e\in c}(1-p_e)\) for a cycle and \(U(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}w_{e_i}\prod_{j=1}^{i}(1-p_{e_j})\) for a chain—optimize \(\sum_q x_q U(q)\) subject to \(\sum_q r_{tq}x_q\le\mu_t\) for every type and \(x_q\ge0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: disjoint finite sets \(P\) of patient–donor-pair types and \(N\) of non-directed-donor types, with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Write \(\tau=|P|+|N|\); this is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Compatibility is specified by \(E\subseteq(P\cup N)\times P\), with \(m=|E|\), positive binary-rational weights \(w_e\), and binary-rational failure probabilities \(p_e\in[0,1]\). Different transplant occurrences fail independently, including occurrences having the same type arc.
The input includes unary-encoded integer caps \(K\ge2\) and \(H\ge1\). Templates are all cycles with between \(2\) and \(K\) pair participants, and all chains starting with exactly one non-directed donor followed by between \(1\) and \(H\) pair participants; every required compatibility arc must exist. Length counts edges. Participants are distinct, but types may repeat, including consecutively when the corresponding type arc exists.
For a template \(q\), let \(r_{tq}\) count its participants of type \(t\). A cycle has utility \(U(c)=(\sum_{e\in c}w_e)\prod_{e\in c}(1-p_e)\); a chain with ordered edge occurrences \(e_1,\ldots,e_h\) has utility \(U(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{h}w_{e_i}\prod_{j=1}^{i}(1-p_{e_j})\). Products and sums count occurrences. Cycles execute only if every edge succeeds; chains execute their successful initial prefix.
An allocation assigns real masses \(x_q\ge0\) to templates, subject to \(\sum_q r_{tq}x_q\le\mu_t\) for every type, and maximizes \(\sum_q x_qU(q)\). These capacities reserve participants before failures; unused mass is allowed. Templates are implicit, not supplied as an enumerated input. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.

Is exact maximum-expected-weight mass allocation solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L,K,H\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite participants, an exact mixed-integer linear reformulation has \(O(H|E|+|C|)\) variables, where \(C\) is the enumerated set of permitted cycles; this is a formulation result, not a polynomial-time algorithm. [Bidkhori et al.](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v124/bidkhori20a/bidkhori20a.pdf)
No complexity classification for this precise continuous problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Renamed the chain cap H to distinguish it from the required input-bit-length notation L, and defined m as the number of compatibility arcs.
  • Specified positive binary-rational edge weights and binary-rational failure probabilities in [0,1], retaining the discrete model's probability endpoints.
  • Specified that arcs end only at patient-pair types, cycles contain at least two pair participants, and chains contain exactly one initial non-directed donor and at least one pair participant.
  • Measured both caps in edges and made them variable unary-encoded input integers, so polynomial dependence on the caps is polynomial in their encoding.
  • Specified that every compatible template within the caps is available implicitly, rather than treating an enumerated template list as input.
  • Allowed repeated types and within-type arcs between distinct participants, while excluding a participant's donation to its own paired patient.
  • Interpreted independence per transplant occurrence, including repeated occurrences of a type arc.
  • Specified that mass capacities reserve all planned participants before failures, with no recourse or reuse of failed-template participants.

Result

Exact maximum-expected-weight mass allocation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. Its threshold decision version is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

Hardness holds even when there are no non-directed donors, all type masses are equal dyadic rationals, all edge weights are positive integers, and every permitted cycle has the same length. The construction accounts for repeated types being allowed: its graph forces every permitted cycle to visit each layer exactly once.

1. A dyadic approximation lemma

We first justify the numerical computations used in the reduction.

Lemma. Given a rational \(z\in[0,2]\) and a positive integer \(b\), one can compute a dyadic rational \(q\in[0,1]\) satisfying \(\lvert q-\exp(-z)\rvert\le 2^{-b}\), in time polynomial in the encoding length of \(z\) and in \(b\).

Proof. Put \(r=b+8\) and compute the Taylor polynomial
\(\displaystyle T_r(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\frac{(-z)^j}{j!}\)
using exact rational arithmetic. Taylor’s theorem gives
\(\displaystyle \lvert T_r(z)-\exp(-z)\rvert\le\frac{2^{r+1}}{(r+1)!}\).

For any integer \(M\ge3\),
\(\displaystyle \frac{2^M}{M!}=\frac43\prod_{j=4}^{M}\frac2j\le\frac43\,2^{-(M-3)}\).
Taking \(M=r+1=b+9\) makes the Taylor error smaller than \(2^{-b-2}\).

Round \(T_r(z)\) to the nearest multiple of \(2^{-b}\), then project the result onto \([0,1]\). Rounding introduces error at most \(2^{-b-1}\), and projection cannot increase distance from \(\exp(-z)\in[0,1]\). The resulting error is therefore smaller than \(2^{-b}\).

If \(z=A/C\), all Taylor terms can be expressed over the common denominator \(C^r r!\). Its bit length is polynomial in \(b\) and the encoding length of \(z\), as are the numerators and the number of arithmetic operations. This proves the computational assertion. \(\square\)

All exponential expressions below are used only in the proof. The constructed society contains only dyadic probabilities.

2. Reduction from PARTITION

We use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem PARTITION: given positive binary-encoded integers, determine whether some subset has half their total sum. See, for example, [MIT’s lecture notes](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.890/fall14/lectures/L02.pdf).

We may restrict to instances with even total sum. Indeed, multiplying every integer in an arbitrary instance by two preserves its answer and makes its total even.

Thus let the instance be positive integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) with
\(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i=2B\),
where \(B\) is a positive integer. Define
\(k=2n+1\) and \(D=B+k\).

Compatibility graph and masses

Create pair types \(u_0,\ldots,u_n\) and \(v_i^0,v_i^1\) for each \(i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\). Include exactly these compatibility arcs:

  • \(u_{i-1}\to v_i^0\) and \(u_{i-1}\to v_i^1\);
  • \(v_i^0\to u_i\) and \(v_i^1\to u_i\);
  • \(u_n\to u_0\).

There are initially \(3n+1\) types and \(4n+1\) arcs.

Let \(\tau\) be the smallest power of two at least \(3n+1\). Add isolated pair types until the support has size \(\tau\), and give every type mass
\(\alpha=1/\tau\).
These masses are positive dyadic rationals summing to one.

Set \(N=\varnothing\), \(K=k\), and \(H=1\). Hence there are no chains.

The non-isolated graph has \(k\) cyclically ordered layers:
\(u_0,\{v_1^0,v_1^1\},u_1,\ldots,\{v_n^0,v_n^1\},u_n\).
Every arc advances exactly one layer, modulo \(k\). Consequently, every closed walk has length divisible by \(k\). Since the permitted cycle lengths lie between \(2\) and \(K=k\), every permitted cycle has length exactly \(k\).

Such a cycle visits every layer once, selecting precisely one of \(v_i^0,v_i^1\) for each item \(i\). Thus cycles correspond to subsets \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\), up to cyclic rotation. Every cycle uses \(u_0\) exactly once, and every participant type in that cycle occurs exactly once. Allowing repeated types introduces no additional permitted cycles in this graph.

Weights and success probabilities

Write \(s_e=1-p_e\) for edge success probability.

Give every arc weight \(1\), except that
\(w_{u_{i-1},v_i^1}=1+a_i\).

Choose a positive integer \(b\) such that
\(2^b\ge4096nD^2\), and put \(\eta=2^{-b}\).
Using the lemma, compute dyadic numbers \(\widehat s_i\in[0,1]\) with
\(\lvert\widehat s_i-\exp(-a_i/D)\rvert\le\eta\).
This is applicable because \(0<a_i/D<2\).

Assign success probability \(\widehat s_i\) to \(u_{i-1}\to v_i^1\), and success probability \(1\) to every other arc.

For the cycle corresponding to \(I\), put \(S_I=\sum_{i\in I}a_i\). Its utility is exactly
\(\displaystyle U_I=(k+S_I)\prod_{i\in I}\widehat s_i\).

This follows directly from the specified cycle utility: the total edge weight is \(k+S_I\), and the only non-unit success factors are those belonging to selected items.

3. A strict gap at the target subset sum

Consider the ideal function
\(\displaystyle f(S)=(k+S)\exp(-S/D)\)
on \([0,2B]\). Since \(D=B+k\),
\(\displaystyle f'(S)=\frac{B-S}{D}\exp(-S/D)\).

Therefore \(f\) has its unique maximum at \(S=B\).

More precisely, if \(S\in[0,2B]\), integrating the derivative between \(S\) and \(B\) gives
\(\displaystyle f(B)-f(S)\ge\frac{\exp(-2)}{2D}(S-B)^2\).
Indeed, throughout that interval the exponential factor is at least \(\exp(-2)\), because its argument lies between \(0\) and \(2B/D<2\).

The elementary estimate \(\exp(1)<3\) implies \(\exp(-2)>1/9\). Thus, for every integer \(S\ne B\),
\(\displaystyle f(B)-f(S)>\frac1{18D}\).

We next control the error caused by using dyadic success probabilities. For factors in \([0,1]\), telescoping the difference of two products yields
\(\displaystyle \left|\prod_j x_j-\prod_j y_j\right|\le\sum_j|x_j-y_j|\).
Consequently,
\(\displaystyle \left|\prod_{i\in I}\widehat s_i-\exp(-S_I/D)\right|\le n\eta\).

Since \(k+S_I\le k+2B\le2D\), every constructed cycle satisfies
\(\displaystyle |U_I-f(S_I)|\le2Dn\eta\le\frac1{2048D}\).
Denote this last error bound by \(\varepsilon=1/(2048D)\).

A dyadic threshold

Use the lemma again to compute a dyadic \(q_*\) satisfying
\(\lvert q_*-\exp(-B/D)\rvert\le\eta\), and put \(M=Dq_*\).
Because \(f(B)=D\exp(-B/D)\),
\(\displaystyle |M-f(B)|\le D\eta\le\frac1{4096D}\).
Write \(\varepsilon_0=1/(4096D)\).

Let \(c\) be the smallest integer satisfying \(2^c\ge32D\), and define
\(\lambda=2^{-c}\) and \(T=M-\lambda\).
These numbers are dyadic, and
\(\displaystyle \frac1{64D}<\lambda\le\frac1{32D}\).

If some subset has \(S_I=B\), then
\(\displaystyle U_I-T\ge\lambda-\varepsilon-\varepsilon_0>0\),
because \(\varepsilon+\varepsilon_0=3/(4096D)<1/(64D)\).

If no subset has sum \(B\), then every cycle satisfies
\(\displaystyle T-U_I>\frac1{18D}-\lambda-\varepsilon-\varepsilon_0>0\).
The final inequality follows from
\(\displaystyle \frac1{32}+\frac3{4096}<\frac1{18}\).

We have therefore proved the exact equivalence:
a subset sums to \(B\) if and only if \(\max_I U_I\ge T\).
Both directions have a strict gap.

4. Why fractional allocation preserves the reduction

Let \(V\) be the optimal allocation value for the constructed society.

Every permitted cycle consumes one unit of type \(u_0\) per unit of cycle mass. Its capacity constraint therefore gives
\(\displaystyle \sum_q x_q\le\alpha\).

It follows that every feasible allocation satisfies
\(\displaystyle \sum_q x_qU(q)\le\alpha\max_I U_I\).

Conversely, choose a cycle of maximum utility and allocate mass \(\alpha\) to it. Every type it uses occurs exactly once and has capacity \(\alpha\), so this allocation is feasible. Hence
\(\displaystyle V=\alpha\max_I U_I\).

Combining this identity with the preceding gap proves
\(\displaystyle V\ge\alpha T\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{the PARTITION instance is a yes-instance}\).

Thus the reduction establishes hardness of the continuous allocation problem itself, rather than merely hardness of selecting one cycle.

Encoding size

The construction has \(\tau<2(3n+1)\), \(m=4n+1\), \(K=2n+1\), and \(H=1\). The unary caps therefore have polynomial encoding length.

Furthermore, \(b\) has order \(\log n+\log D\), and \(D=B+2n+1\) has polynomial bit length in the PARTITION input. The approximation lemma constructs every probability in polynomial time with polynomial bit length. The weights, masses, and threshold \(\alpha T\) also have polynomial bit length.

This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction to the threshold decision problem. Exact optimization is consequently \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard.

5. The threshold decision problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\)

For completeness, consider an arbitrary input society together with a binary-rational threshold \(\theta\).

There are finitely many type-sequence templates because both length caps are finite. Templates differing only in participant identities have identical resource counts and utilities, so they may be aggregated by their type sequences. The resulting allocation problem is a finite linear program.

Its feasible region is nonempty, since the zero allocation is feasible. It is bounded: every template consumes at least one participant, so each allocation variable is bounded by a type capacity. Thus an optimum exists at an extreme point.

Put \(R=\max\{K,H+1\}\). At any extreme point, at most \(\tau\) template variables are positive. To verify this, restrict the tight capacity constraints to the positive variables. If their matrix had less than full column rank, a nonzero null vector would give sufficiently small feasible perturbations in both directions: positive variables would remain nonnegative, tight constraints would remain tight, and slack constraints would remain feasible. This would contradict extremality. Hence there are at most \(\tau\) positive variables.

For \(s\) positive variables, select \(s\) independent tight capacity equations. Their coefficient matrix is an invertible integer matrix with entries at most \(R\). Its determinant has absolute value at most \(s!R^s\). After clearing the denominators of the masses, Cramer’s rule therefore gives all positive allocation values polynomial bit length.

A certificate can consequently list at most \(\tau\) templates and their rational allocation masses. Each template has at most \(R\) participants and can be encoded with polynomially many bits because the caps are unary. A verifier checks its arcs, resource counts, capacities, and expected utility using exact rational arithmetic. Products and sums involve at most the unary length bounds, so their bit lengths and computation times are polynomial. The verifier then compares the allocation value with \(\theta\).

Every yes-instance has such a certificate, namely an optimal extreme-point allocation. The threshold problem is therefore in \(\mathrm{NP}\), and the reduction proves it is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

Conclusion

Exact maximum-expected-weight mass allocation is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, even under the restricted construction above. A polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L,K,H\) would therefore imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). The reduction establishes ordinary hardness; it does not establish strong hardness.
