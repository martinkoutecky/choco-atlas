The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given applicant types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), school types \(j\) with rational per-capita capacities \(q_j\), and a feasible proposed allocation \(x_{t,j}\), is the allocation popular against every feasible reassignment of the same population? Let \(z_{t,j,k}\) be the mass of type \(t\) reassigned from its current destination \(j\) to destination \(k\), require \(\sum_k z_{t,j,k}=x_{t,j}\) and \(\sum_{t,j}z_{t,j,k}\le q_k\) for each school type \(k\), and maximize \(\sum_{t,j,k} z_{t,j,k}(\mathbf{1}[k\succ_t j]-\mathbf{1}[j\succ_t k])\); the allocation is popular exactly when this maximum is at most \(0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(m\ge1\) schools. A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\ge1\) applicant types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible preference types.
Each type \(t\) has a nonempty acceptable set \(A_t\) of schools and a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) on \(D_t=A_t\cup\{0\}\), with the unmatched destination \(0\) ranked last. Every school is acceptable to some type. Schools have positive binary-rational capacities \(q_k\), measured relative to total applicant mass, and do not vote; destination \(0\) has no capacity constraint.
The input includes a binary-rational allocation \(x_{t,j}\ge0\), indexed by \(j\in D_t\), satisfying \(\sum_{j\in D_t}x_{t,j}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t:k\in A_t}x_{t,k}\le q_k\) for every school \(k\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including rankings, masses, capacities, and allocation entries.
A reassignment consists of real masses \(z_{t,j,k}\ge0\), for \(j,k\in D_t\), satisfying \(\sum_{k\in D_t}z_{t,j,k}=x_{t,j}\) and \(\sum_{t:k\in A_t}\sum_{j\in D_t}z_{t,j,k}\le q_k\) for every school \(k\). Mass may be split arbitrarily, and remaining at the same destination is allowed.
Its voting margin against \(x\) is \(V(z)=\sum_t\sum_{j,k\in D_t}z_{t,j,k}\bigl(\mathbf{1}[k\succ_t j]-\mathbf{1}[j\succ_t k]\bigr)\). The allocation \(x\) is popular if \(V(z)\le0\) for every feasible reassignment.

Is deciding whether the given allocation is popular solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For discrete house allocation with strict applicant preferences and private last resorts, checking a given matching for popularity is polynomial-time solvable: it is popular exactly when it maximizes the weight of an applicant-perfect matching, assigning weights \(2,1,0\) to destinations better than, equal to, or worse than the applicant’s current destination.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total applicant mass to one so capacities are per-capita quantities.
  • Specified strict rankings over nonempty acceptable sets, excluded unacceptable destinations, and retained the absence of isolated schools.
  • Represented private last resorts by a shared, unconstrained unmatched destination ranked below every acceptable school.
  • Specified positive binary-rational school capacities and binary-rational proposed allocation entries; defined L as total input bit length.
  • Required allocation rows to exhaust each type’s mass and allowed arbitrary nonnegative real reassignment masses, including moves to the unmatched destination and staying put.
  • Specified that only applicants vote.

Result

Deciding whether the given allocation is popular is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\). Arbitrary splitting of mass makes the best voting margin a linear-programming objective over a polynomial-size feasible region.

1. A linear program for the best reassignment

For each type \(t\) and destinations \(j,k\in D_t\), define

\(c_{t,j,k}=\mathbf{1}[k\succ_t j]-\mathbf{1}[j\succ_t k]\in\{-1,0,1\}.\)

Consider the linear program with variables \(z_{t,j,k}\), objective

\(\displaystyle M=\max_z\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\sum_{j,k\in D_t}c_{t,j,k}z_{t,j,k},\)

and constraints:

  • \(\displaystyle \sum_{k\in D_t}z_{t,j,k}=x_{t,j}\) for every \(t\) and \(j\in D_t\);
  • \(\displaystyle \sum_{t:k\in A_t}\sum_{j\in D_t}z_{t,j,k}\le q_k\) for every school \(k\);
  • \(z_{t,j,k}\ge0\) for every variable.

These are exactly the reassignment constraints in the question. In particular, there is no integrality restriction: the feasible points include every allowed reassignment with real, arbitrarily split masses. The objective is exactly its voting margin.

The feasible region is nonempty. Indeed, the reassignment defined by \(z_{t,j,j}=x_{t,j}\) and \(z_{t,j,k}=0\) when \(k\ne j\) leaves everyone at their current destination. It satisfies school capacities because \(x\) does, and its voting margin is zero.

Furthermore, nonnegativity and the row-sum constraints imply \(0\le z_{t,j,k}\le x_{t,j}\le1\). Thus the feasible region is closed and bounded, so the maximum exists. Consequently \(M\ge0\), and

\(x\text{ is popular}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad M=0.\)

If \(M>0\), an optimizer supplies a feasible reassignment defeating \(x\). If \(M=0\), every feasible reassignment has voting margin at most zero.

2. Polynomial size and exact computation

Write \(d_t=|D_t|\le m+1\). The linear program has

\(\displaystyle N=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}d_t^2\le\tau(m+1)^2\)

variables, \(\sum_t d_t\le\tau(m+1)\) row-sum equalities, \(m\) capacity inequalities, and \(N\) nonnegativity inequalities.

Every constraint coefficient belongs to \(\{-1,0,1\}\), and every objective coefficient belongs to \(\{-1,0,1\}\). The right-hand sides are allocation entries and capacities already present in the input. Ranking positions determine all objective coefficients in polynomial time. Therefore even a dense encoding of this linear program has bit length polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

We use the standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming: a rational linear program can be solved exactly, or classified as infeasible or unbounded, in time polynomial in its binary encoding length. The rational-data complexity theorem is recalled in [Adler and Beling, *Polynomial Algorithms for Linear Programming over the Algebraic Numbers*](https://adler.ieor.berkeley.edu/ilans_pubs/lp_algebraic_stoc_1992.pdf).

Its hypotheses hold here: all coefficients are rational, the encoding has polynomial size, and feasibility and boundedness were proved above. Thus \(M\) can be computed exactly and compared with zero in polynomial time. This exact comparison handles arbitrarily small positive voting margins without a numerical tolerance.

3. Connection to the discrete weighted-matching criterion

The weights in the question extend directly to the present model. Define \(w_{t,j,k}=1+c_{t,j,k}\). Strictness of preferences implies that these weights are \(2,1,0\) when the new destination is respectively better than, equal to, or worse than the current destination.

Every feasible reassignment has total mass

\(\displaystyle \sum_t\sum_{j,k\in D_t}z_{t,j,k}=\sum_t\sum_{j\in D_t}x_{t,j}=\sum_t\mu_t=1.\)

Hence its weighted objective satisfies

\(\displaystyle \sum_t\sum_{j,k\in D_t}w_{t,j,k}z_{t,j,k}=1+V(z).\)

The reassignment that leaves everyone in place has weight \(1\). Therefore \(x\) is popular exactly when this reassignment maximizes the weighted objective.

The relevant current-destination groups are the explicitly listed pairs \((t,j)\), of which there are at most \(\tau(m+1)\). No expansion into individual applicants or enumeration of unlisted preference types is necessary.
