The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given schools \(C\), a finite type set \(T\) encoding complete student preferences and school-priority orders, a rational mass vector \(\mu\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}^{\tau}\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a rational normalized union of quota regions \(\mathcal V_\infty=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}\{\nu\in\mathbb R_{\ge 0}^{m}:\mathbf 1^\top\nu=1,\ p_j\le\nu_c\le q_j\ \forall c\}\), compute the normalized aggregate limit \(x^\infty\) of QRDA on \(r\)-fold replications with \(r\mu_t\in\mathbb Z\), and determine whether this limit exists, is school-feasible, and is aggregate-fair.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are ordered schools \(C=(c_1,\ldots,c_m)\), with \(m\ge1\). Each type has a strict complete preference \(\succ_t\) over schools; each school has a strict complete priority \(\succ_c\) over types. All student–school pairs are acceptable.
The input includes \(\ell\ge1\) rational pairs satisfying \(0\le p_j\le 1/m\le q_j\le1\), defining \(\mathcal V_\infty=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}\{\nu\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{m}:\sum_c\nu_c=1,\ p_j\le\nu_c\le q_j\ \forall c\}\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,\ell,L\).
For every positive integer \(r\) with \(r\mu_t\in\mathbb Z\) for all types and \(V_r=\{v\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{m}:v/r\in\mathcal V_\infty\}\ne\varnothing\), create \(r\mu_t\) students of each type. Schools rank type blocks by \(\succ_c\), breaking priorities within each type by increasing copy index at every school.

An aggregate allocation has \(x_{tc}\ge0\) and \(\sum_cx_{tc}=\mu_t\); it is school-feasible if \((\sum_tx_{tc})_{c\in C}\in\mathcal V_\infty\), and aggregate-fair if no \(t,u,c,d\) satisfy \(x_{td}>0\), \(x_{uc}>0\), \(c\succ_t d\), and \(t\succ_c u\).

Does \(x^\infty\) always exist and yield a school-feasible, aggregate-fair allocation? Is deciding its existence and computing it exactly when it exists polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,\ell,L\), or NP-hard?

For finite societies, QRDA terminates with a feasible, fair matching under nonempty unions of symmetric M-convex constraints. The full version also establishes an \(O(mn)\) implementation for \(n\) students, assuming constant-time feasibility checks ([verified source](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0289965)). Partial resolution: rational limits and an effective convergence test

The replication limit need not exist. Whenever it exists, it is school-feasible and aggregate-fair.

The main additional result proved here is that the integer outcome sequence has an effective finite description: apart from finitely many exceptions, it is affine on residue classes of replication sizes. Consequently:

  • There are finitely many accumulation points, all rational.
  • Existence of the limit is decidable, and an existing limit can be computed exactly.
  • A general algorithm has bit running time

$$ 2^{2^{2^{(m+\tau+\ell+L)^{O(1)}}}}. $$

This does not settle whether the general problem is polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard. That classification remains open in this write-up.

1. Finite termination and properties of accumulation points

Let
$$ a_j(r)=\lceil rp_j\rceil,\qquad b_j(r)=\lfloor rq_j\rfloor. $$
The integer feasible vectors in component $j$ satisfy
$$ \sum_c v_c=r,\qquad a_j(r)\le v_c\le b_j(r). $$
This component is nonempty exactly when
$$ ma_j(r)\le r\le mb_j(r). $$
Necessity follows by summing the bounds. For sufficiency, start every coordinate at $a_j(r)$ and distribute the remaining $r-ma_j(r)$ units, adding at most $b_j(r)-a_j(r)$ to each coordinate. The displayed inequalities ensure that this is possible.

Fix an admissible $r$ and write
$$ Q_r=\max_{v\in V_r,\ c\in C}v_c. $$
Because a feasible vector sums to $r$, we have $mQ_r\ge r$.

Starting from equal capacities $Q_r$, cyclic reductions keep capacities within one unit of each other. After exactly $mQ_r-r$ reductions, their sum is $r$, so every capacity is either $\lfloor r/m\rfloor$ or $\lceil r/m\rceil$.

Choose a nonempty component $j$. Its nonemptiness gives
$$ a_j(r)\le\lfloor r/m\rfloor,\qquad b_j(r)\ge\lceil r/m\rceil. $$
Thus this balanced capacity vector belongs to $V_r$.

Whenever total capacity is at least $r$, DA assigns every student. Otherwise, an unmatched student has applied to every school, and all schools must be full. The number assigned would then equal total capacity and be at least $r$, contradicting the existence of an unmatched student. At the balanced stage, school counts therefore equal capacities. This proves termination by that stage.

The returned matching is fair. A student preferring another school to her assignment applied there and was rejected. At a rejecting school, every subsequently retained student outranks the rejected student. The same argument also shows that such a school is full.

Proposition 1

Every accumulation point of $x^{(r)}$ is an aggregate allocation that is school-feasible and aggregate-fair.

Proof. Let $D$ clear all mass denominators. Every positive multiple of $mD$ is admissible: type populations are integral, and the integer uniform school vector is feasible. Thus admissible replication sizes are unbounded.

Every returned allocation satisfies
$$ x^{(r)}_{tc}\ge0,\qquad \sum_c x^{(r)}_{tc}=\mu_t. $$
These conditions define a compact set. They persist under limits.

The school-total map is continuous, and $\mathcal V_\infty$ is a finite union of closed sets. School feasibility therefore persists under limits.

Fix $t,u,c,d$ with $c\succ_t d$ and $t\succ_c u$. Finite fairness implies
$$ x^{(r)}_{td}x^{(r)}_{uc}=0. $$
Indeed, positive entries would supply a copy of $t$ assigned to $d$ and a copy of $u$ assigned to $c$, with the former preferring $c$ and outranking the latter there. Taking limits preserves this equality. Since both entries are nonnegative, they cannot both be positive. This proves aggregate fairness. $\square$

2. A counterexample to universal existence

Take $m=3$, three types $a,b,d$, and masses
$$ \mu_a=\frac12,\qquad \mu_b=\mu_d=\frac14. $$

Use the following preferences and priorities.

| Type | Preference |
|---|---|
| $a$ | $c_1\succ_a c_2\succ_a c_3$ |
| $b$ | $c_2\succ_b c_1\succ_b c_3$ |
| $d$ | $c_3\succ_d c_1\succ_d c_2$ |

| School | Priority |
|---|---|
| $c_1$ | $b\succ_{c_1}a\succ_{c_1}d$ |
| $c_2$ | $a\succ_{c_2}b\succ_{c_2}d$ |
| $c_3$ | $d\succ_{c_3}a\succ_{c_3}b$ |

Set $\ell=1$, $p_1=0$, and $q_1=3/8$. These bounds satisfy the input hypotheses.

Mass integrality requires $4\mid r$. Put $Q=\lfloor3r/8\rfloor$. For $r=8k$, the vector $(3k,3k,2k)$ is feasible. For $r=8k+4$, $k\ge1$, the vector $(3k+1,3k+1,2k+2)$ is feasible. Their permutations show that the initial capacity is $Q$. The remaining positive multiple $r=4$ is inadmissible.

For every admissible $r$, initial total capacity is at least $r$. DA assigns everyone, and all school counts are at most $Q$. Since the constraint has only an upper bound, QRDA returns this initial DA outcome.

For $r=8k$, there are $4k$ copies of $a$ and $2k$ each of $b,d$, with capacities $3k$. School $c_1$ rejects $k$ copies of $a$. They join the $2k$ copies of $b$ at $c_2$, exactly filling it. No further rejection occurs. Hence
$$ x^{(8k)}= \begin{pmatrix} 3/8&1/8&0\\ 0&1/4&0\\ 0&0&1/4 \end{pmatrix}, $$
with rows ordered as $a,b,d$.

For $r=8k+4$, populations are $4k+2,2k+1,2k+1$, and capacities are $3k+1$.

All copies of $d$ remain at $c_3$, where they have highest priority and fit. A copy of $b$ reaching $c_1$ is permanently retained, because the entire population of $b$ fits there and has highest priority. Thus no copy of $b$ reaches $c_3$.

The combined population of $a,b$ is $6k+3$, exceeding the combined capacity of $c_1,c_2$ by one. Full assignment therefore puts some copy of $a$ at $c_3$. Fairness then excludes any copy of $b$ at $c_2$: that copy of $a$ prefers $c_2$ and outranks $b$ there. All $2k+1$ copies of $b$ must consequently be at $c_1$.

A copy of $a$ reaching $c_3$ was rejected from both earlier schools, which remain full. The count rows are therefore
$$ a:(k,3k+1,1),\qquad b:(2k+1,0,0),\qquad d:(0,0,2k+1). $$
Consequently,
$$ \lim_{k\to\infty}x^{(8k+4)}= \begin{pmatrix} 1/8&3/8&0\\ 1/4&0&0\\ 0&0&1/4 \end{pmatrix}. $$
The two subsequential limits differ. Thus $x^\infty$ does not always exist.

3. Encoding DA without expanding the population

The common copy-index priorities permit a description using only integer type–school counts.

Fix nonnegative integer populations $n_t$ and capacities $\kappa_c$, with
$$ \sum_c\kappa_c\ge\sum_t n_t. $$
For an integer count matrix $Y$, define
$$ H_c(Y)=\sum_tY_{tc}, $$
$$ W_{tc}(Y)=\sum_{d:\ c\succ_t d}Y_{td}, \qquad B_{tc}(Y)=\sum_{u:\ t\succ_c u}Y_{uc}. $$

A full matching is called stable if there is no student–school pair in which the student prefers that school and the school either has a vacancy or holds a lower-priority student.

Lemma 2: stable count matrices

A matrix $Y$ is the count matrix of a full stable matching exactly when
$$ Y_{tc}\ge0,\qquad \sum_cY_{tc}=n_t,\qquad H_c(Y)\le\kappa_c, $$
and, for every $t,c$,
$$ W_{tc}(Y)=0 \quad\text{or}\quad \bigl(H_c(Y)=\kappa_c\ \text{and}\ B_{tc}(Y)=0\bigr). $$

Moreover, its full stable matching is uniquely determined by $Y$: assign each type’s copies, in increasing index, to schools in that type’s preference order according to the counts.

Proof. In a stable matching, an earlier copy cannot receive a worse school than a later copy of the same type. Otherwise, the earlier copy and the later copy’s school form a blocking pair. Thus every stable matching has the asserted canonical assignment.

If $W_{tc}(Y)>0$, some copy of $t$ prefers $c$ to her assignment. Stability requires $c$ to be full and to hold no lower-priority type, giving the displayed condition.

Conversely, construct the canonical assignment from a matrix satisfying the conditions. Consider a copy of $t$ assigned below $c$. The condition makes $c$ full and excludes lower-priority types there. Every copy of $t$ assigned to $c$ has a smaller index than the considered copy, by canonical assignment. Hence every student at $c$ outranks that copy. No blocking pair exists. $\square$

Write the preference order of $t$ as
$$ c_{t,1}\succ_t\cdots\succ_t c_{t,m}, $$
and define prefix counts
$$ F_{t,k}(Y)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}Y_{t,c_{t,i}}. $$

For canonical assignments with the same populations, every copy weakly prefers its assignment under $Y$ to its assignment under $Z$ exactly when
$$ F_{t,k}(Y)\ge F_{t,k}(Z) \qquad\text{for every }t,k. $$
To verify the reverse implication, a copy with index $i$ assigned within the first $k$ schools under $Z$ satisfies $i\le F_{t,k}(Z)\le F_{t,k}(Y)$, so is also assigned within that prefix under $Y$. The forward implication follows by counting copies assigned within each prefix.

Lemma 3: DA through prefix inequalities

Let $S(n,\kappa,Y)$ denote the conditions in Lemma 2. The DA count matrix is the unique matrix satisfying
$$ S(n,\kappa,Y) \quad\text{and}\quad \forall Z\, \left( S(n,\kappa,Z)\Longrightarrow \bigwedge_{t,k}F_{t,k}(Y)\ge F_{t,k}(Z) \right). $$

Proof. We prove the needed student-optimality property directly.

Fix any full stable matching $M$. Suppose DA rejects an edge belonging to $M$, and consider the first such rejection. Let student $s$ be rejected by school $c=M(s)$. At that moment $c$ holds $\kappa_c$ students, all outranking $s$. Since $M$ assigns $s$ to $c$, at least one held student $h$ is assigned elsewhere under $M$.

Student $h$ has proposed to $c$. If she preferred $M(h)$ to $c$, she would previously have been rejected by $M(h)$, contradicting the choice of the first rejected edge belonging to $M$. Since $M(h)\ne c$, she strictly prefers $c$ to $M(h)$. Together with $c$ preferring $h$ to $s$, this gives a blocking pair for $M$, a contradiction.

Thus DA never rejects any student’s edge in $M$. Its final assignment is weakly preferred by every student to her assignment in $M$. DA is full and stable by the argument in Section 1. Lemma 2 and the prefix characterization therefore imply that its matrix satisfies the displayed formula.

Conversely, a matrix satisfying the formula dominates the DA matrix, while student-optimality makes the DA matrix dominate it. All prefix counts are equal. Taking differences of successive prefixes makes all entries equal. $\square$

Both lemmas use only linear equalities, inequalities, Boolean operations, and quantification over integer count matrices.

4. A Presburger formula for the entire QRDA outcome sequence

Presburger arithmetic is first-order integer arithmetic using addition and order. Multiplication by a fixed integer is allowed as notation for repeated addition; multiplication of two variables is not used below.

All count variables in this section range over nonnegative integers. Such quantifiers can equivalently be expressed over all integers by adding nonnegativity guards.

Write $\mu_t=\alpha_t/\delta_t$. Define the population predicate
$$ \operatorname{Pop}(r,n): \quad r\ge1\ \text{and}\ \delta_t n_t=\alpha_t r\ \text{for every }t. $$

Define feasible school counts by
$$ \operatorname{Feas}(r,v): \quad \sum_c v_c=r \ \text{and}\ \bigvee_{j=1}^{\ell} \bigwedge_{c=1}^{m} \left(rp_j\le v_c\le rq_j\right). $$
After clearing each input denominator, these are linear integer inequalities.

The initial capacity is characterized by
$$ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Init}(r,Q):\quad& \exists v\, \left(\operatorname{Feas}(r,v)\ \land\ \bigvee_c v_c=Q\right)\\ &{}\land \forall w\, \left(\operatorname{Feas}(r,w)\Longrightarrow \bigwedge_c w_c\le Q\right). \end{aligned} $$
This predicate holds precisely when $V_r$ is nonempty and $Q=Q_r$.

After $k$ reductions, write $k=ms+b$, with $0\le b<m$. The capacities are specified by
$$ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Path}(r,Q,k,\kappa):\quad \exists s,b\quad& k=ms+b,\quad b<m,\quad k\le mQ-r,\\ &\bigwedge_{i=1}^{m} \left[ (b\ge i\ \land\ \kappa_{c_i}+s+1=Q) \ \lor\ (b<i\ \land\ \kappa_{c_i}+s=Q) \right]. \end{aligned} $$
Their total is $mQ-k\ge r$, so the full-DA characterization applies.

Let $\operatorname{DA}(n,\kappa,Y)$ be Lemma 3’s formula, and put
$$ \begin{aligned} A(r,Q,n,k,Y):\quad \exists\kappa\quad& \operatorname{Path}(r,Q,k,\kappa)\\ &{}\land\operatorname{DA}(n,\kappa,Y)\\ &{}\land\operatorname{Feas}\bigl(r,(H_c(Y))_{c\in C}\bigr). \end{aligned} $$

Finally define
$$ \begin{aligned} G(r,Y):\quad\exists Q,n,k\quad& \operatorname{Pop}(r,n)\land\operatorname{Init}(r,Q) \land A(r,Q,n,k,Y)\\ &{}\land \forall k'\, \left(k'<k\Longrightarrow \neg\exists Z\,A(r,Q,n,k',Z)\right). \end{aligned} $$

Proposition 4

The solutions of $G$ are exactly the pairs
$$ (r,Y^{(r)}),\qquad Y^{(r)}=r x^{(r)}, $$
for admissible $r$.

Proof. The population and initialization predicates impose exactly admissibility and QRDA’s initial capacity. The path predicate describes each cyclic reduction stage through the balanced stage. Lemma 3 identifies that stage’s unique DA count matrix. Predicate $A$ requires feasibility, and the final universal condition excludes every earlier feasible stage. Section 1 ensures that some feasible stage occurs within this path. Thus $G$ describes precisely the returned outcome. $\square$

The formula does not contain one variable per student. Its length is polynomial in the support input. More specifically, it can be constructed with length
$$ N= O\!\left( \bigl(\ell m+m\tau(m+\tau)+m+\tau\bigr) \bigl(L+\log(m+\tau+\ell+2)\bigr) \right). $$
Indeed, feasibility contributes $O(\ell m)$ inequalities; stability and prefix formulas have $O(m\tau(m+\tau))$ variable occurrences. The remaining predicates have polynomial size, and each coefficient has $O(L)$ bits. Every displayed predicate is expanded only a constant number of times.

5. Effective decomposition into arithmetic rays

We use the following precise computational result.

Presburger decomposition theorem. An integer-arithmetic formula of length $N$ can be converted into a finite union
$$ \bigcup_i \left\{ b+\sum_{p\in P_i}k_p p: b\in B_i,\ k_p\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0} \right\}, $$
where $B_i,P_i$ are finite integer-vector sets and each $P_i$ is linearly independent. The conversion has a triply exponential running-time bound in $N$. This is Corollary 5.2 of [Chistikov, Haase, and Mansutti, *Geometric decision procedures and the VC dimension of linear arithmetic theories*](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/166499/1/WRAP-Geometric-decision-procedures-VC-dimension-2022.pdf).

The theorem applies to $G$: all coefficients are fixed integers after denominator clearing, and all its operations are Boolean operations and integer quantifiers. Its free coordinates are $r$ and the $m\tau$ entries of $Y$.

The graph property imposes a particularly strong restriction on the resulting decomposition.

Lemma 5

Suppose a set in nonnegative integer coordinates is the graph of a function of its first coordinate. In any linear component contained in that graph:

  • A period whose first coordinate is zero is the zero vector.
  • All nonzero periods are collinear.
  • If the period set is linearly independent, it contains at most one period, and that period has positive first coordinate.

Proof. A base and every period are nonnegative: a negative coordinate in a period would, after sufficiently many additions, produce a point outside the nonnegative orthant.

Write a period as $(h,v)$. If $h=0$, the base and the base plus this period have the same first coordinate. Functionality forces $v=0$.

For two periods $(h_1,v_1)$ and $(h_2,v_2)$ with positive first coordinates, the points
$$ b+h_2(h_1,v_1) \quad\text{and}\quad b+h_1(h_2,v_2) $$
have equal first coordinates. Functionality gives
$$ h_2v_1=h_1v_2. $$
The periods are therefore collinear. Linear independence permits at most one nonzero period and excludes zero periods. $\square$

Splitting each finite base set $B_i$ into its individual bases, the theorem and lemma give
$$ \{(r,Y):G(r,Y)\} = F\ \cup\ \bigcup_{i=1}^{s} \left\{ (b_{i,0},b_i)+k(h_i,v_i): k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0} \right\}, $$
where $F$ is finite, $h_i>0$, and all listed vectors are nonnegative integer vectors. There is at least one ray because admissible replication sizes are unbounded.

Theorem 6: rational accumulation points and convergence criterion

The accumulation points of $x^{(r)}$ are exactly the distinct matrices
$$ \Lambda_i=\frac{v_i}{h_i}, \qquad 1\le i\le s. $$
Consequently, $x^\infty$ exists exactly when all $\Lambda_i$ are equal.

Proof. On ray $i$,
$$ r=b_{i,0}+kh_i,\qquad Y^{(r)}=b_i+kv_i, $$
so
$$ x^{(r)} = \frac{v_i}{h_i} + \frac{b_i-b_{i,0}v_i/h_i}{r}. $$
As $k$ tends to infinity, this converges to $\Lambda_i$, proving that each listed matrix is an accumulation point.

Conversely, take any subsequence with replication sizes tending to infinity along which $x^{(r)}$ converges. Its graph points eventually avoid $F$. Because there are finitely many rays, infinitely many of these points lie on one ray. That further subsequence converges to the corresponding $\Lambda_i$, so the original subsequential limit equals it.

If two slopes differ, the corresponding rays give distinct subsequential limits. If all slopes equal $\Lambda$, let
$$ K=\max_i \left\|b_i-b_{i,0}\Lambda\right\|_\infty. $$
Outside the finite exceptional set,
$$ \left\|x^{(r)}-\Lambda\right\|_\infty\le\frac K r. $$
Thus the entire sequence converges. $\square$

In particular, every existing limit is rational and has an $O(1/r)$ convergence bound, with an instance-dependent constant.

There is also an eventual residue-class description. Let
$$ H=\operatorname{lcm}(h_1,\ldots,h_s). $$
For each residue modulo $H$, a ray either contains every sufficiently large integer in that residue or contains none. Thus admissibility is eventually a union of residue classes. On each such class, the output is eventually affine:
$$ Y^{(r)}=r\Lambda_a+\Gamma_a, $$
with rational matrices $\Lambda_a,\Gamma_a$. If several rays cover the same residue, their affine expressions agree at infinitely many replication sizes; equality at those sizes forces both their slopes and their intercepts to agree.

6. Exact algorithm and its running time

The resulting algorithm is finite and explicit:

  • Construct $G$.
  • Compute its decomposition with linearly independent period sets.
  • Discard empty components and components without a period.
  • For each remaining period $(h,v)$, compute the rational matrix $v/h$.
  • If these matrices differ, report that the limit does not exist. Otherwise, return their common value.

Correctness follows from Lemma 5 and Theorem 6. The algorithm can also return all accumulation points.

For completeness, the cited decomposition theorem gives the sharper bound
$$ 2^{(a+2)^{d^{O(h)}}}, $$
where $a$ bounds coefficient bit lengths, $d$ bounds the number of free variables in a subformula, and $h$ is syntactic nesting depth. Each is at most the formula length $N$. Consequently, decomposition takes at most
$$ 2^{2^{2^{N^{O(1)}}}} $$
bit operations. Scanning the explicit output and comparing rational slopes takes polynomial time in that output’s bit length, preserving this bound.

Substituting the polynomial bound for $N$ gives
$$ 2^{2^{2^{(m+\tau+\ell+L)^{O(1)}}}}. $$
This bound depends on the support encoding, without expanding any replication into individual students.

7. Polynomial-time computation for at most two schools

The restricted polynomial-time result can also be proved directly.

For $m=1$, necessarily
$$ x^\infty_{t,c_1}=\mu_t. $$

For $m=2$, define
$$ \beta_j=\min(q_j,1-p_j), \qquad \beta=\max_j\beta_j. $$
Each component, expressed through its first coordinate, is the interval $[1-\beta_j,\beta_j]$. These intervals are nested, so their union is $[1-\beta,\beta]$, with $\beta\ge1/2$.

For admissible $r$, put $Q_r=\lfloor r\beta\rfloor$. The feasible integer school vectors satisfy
$$ v_{c_1}+v_{c_2}=r,\qquad r-Q_r\le v_{c_i}\le Q_r. $$
Their endpoint vectors attain $Q_r$, so both initial capacities are $Q_r$. Admissibility gives $2Q_r\ge r$. Initial DA assigns everyone; its upper capacity bounds automatically imply the lower bounds. Thus QRDA returns initially.

Let $G_i$ contain the types preferring $c_i$, and, for $t\in G_i$, put
$$ S_{ti}=\sum_{\substack{u\in G_i\\u\succ_{c_i}t}}\mu_u. $$
Then
$$ x^\infty_{t,c_i} = \min\{\mu_t,\max\{0,\beta-S_{ti}\}\}, \qquad x^\infty_{t,c_{3-i}}=\mu_t-x^\infty_{t,c_i}. $$

To prove this, write $h_r=Q_r/r\ge1/2$. At most one school’s first-choice demand exceeds $h_r$. If neither does, everyone receives her first choice. Otherwise, the overloaded school retains its highest-priority first-choice applicants. Rejected applicants move to the other school, whose total demand becomes $1-h_r\le h_r$, so it rejects nobody. The finite first-choice allocation is therefore the displayed formula with $h_r$ replacing $\beta$. Since $h_r\to\beta$, continuity proves the limiting formula.

Scanning constraints and priorities uses $O(m\tau+\ell)$ exact arithmetic operations. A common denominator has $O(L)$ bits, as do all intermediate rational values. Elementary arithmetic gives the bit bound
$$ O((m\tau+\ell)L^2). $$

8. Remaining open question

Universal existence is disproved, and feasibility and aggregate fairness whenever the limit exists are proved. The general sequence has an effective arithmetic-ray description, giving decidability, rationality, and exact computation with a triply exponential upper bound.

The missing classification is whether deciding convergence and computing an existing limit admit a polynomial-time algorithm in $m,\tau,\ell,L$, or whether an appropriate version of these problems is NP-hard. No hardness reduction or general polynomial-time bound is established here.
