The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a planar cell graph \(H=(P,F)\), three alternatives \(C=\{a_1,a_2,a_3\}\), rational masses \(\mu_{v,a}\ge 0\) for singleton-approval types with \(\sum_{v,a}\mu_{v,a}=1\), and a rational threshold \(q\), do there exist two representatives \(r_1,r_2\in C\) (repetitions allowed) and allocations \(x_{v,a,j}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_{j=1}^{2}x_{v,a,j}=\mu_{v,a}\), such that each district has nonempty connected positive-mass support and \(\sum_{v,a,j}x_{v,a,j}\mathbf{1}[r_j=a]\ge q\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Given a finite simple undirected planar cell graph \(H=(P,F)\), alternatives \(C=\{a_1,a_2,a_3\}\), and a binary-rational threshold \(q\), a type \((v,a)\) is located at cell \(v\) and approves only \(a\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; unlisted types have mass zero, and \(\sum_{v\in P,a\in C}\mu_{v,a}=1\).
Choose exactly two representatives \(r_1,r_2\in C\), allowing repetitions, and real allocations \(x_{v,a,j}\geq 0\) satisfying \(x_{v,a,1}+x_{v,a,2}=\mu_{v,a}\) for every type.
For each \(j\in\{1,2\}\), require \(S_j=\{v\in P:\sum_{a\in C}x_{v,a,j}>0\}\) to be nonempty and \(H[S_j]\) to be connected. The supports may overlap; cells with zero allocated mass cannot provide connectivity.
Feasibility additionally requires \(\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sum_{v\in P}x_{v,r_j,j}\geq q\). There are no district-size constraints.
State running times in \(m=|C|=3\), \(\tau\), \(|P|\), \(|F|\), and the total input bit length \(L\).

Is this continuous feasibility problem solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard?

The discrete version, with one indivisible singleton-approval voter per vertex, is NP-hard on planar graphs with three alternatives and exactly two districts, including when representatives may repeat. [Talmon, 2017](https://www.ifaamas.org/AAMAS/aamas2017/proceedings/pdfs/p633.pdf)
No result settling this exact continuous problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made location part of each type: a type is a cell–approved-alternative pair, and omitted pairs have zero mass.
  • Specified a finite simple undirected graph and interpreted connected support as connectivity of the induced subgraph on cells with strictly positive district mass.
  • Specified real allocations and explicitly allowed overlapping cell supports, as permitted by splitting type masses between districts.
  • Retained arbitrary binary-rational thresholds and imposed no district-size constraints, consistent with the utilitarian Chamberlin–Courant formulation.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The continuous feasibility problem is polynomial-time solvable. In fact, the algorithm works for arbitrary finite simple undirected graphs; planarity is unnecessary.

With \(m=3\), its bit running time is bounded by \(O((|P|+|F|+\tau)L^2)\). Keeping the dependence on the number of alternatives explicit, the bound is \(O(m^2(|P|+|F|+\tau)L^2)\).

1. Remove cells with no mass

Define the total mass at cell \(v\) by \(w_v=\sum_{a\in C}\mu_{v,a}\), and let

\(V^+=\{v\in P:w_v>0\},\qquad G=H[V^+].\)

For every allocation, conservation of mass implies \(S_1\cup S_2=V^+\). Moreover, each connected support lies entirely within one connected component of \(G\). Consequently, if \(G\) has more than two connected components, the instance is infeasible, independently of the threshold.

The normalization \(\sum_{v,a}\mu_{v,a}=1\) ensures that \(V^+\) is nonempty.

If \(G\) has exactly two connected components \(D_1,D_2\), each district must occupy one entire component: neither support can meet both components, and together the supports must cover all occupied cells. Thus the maximum satisfaction is exactly

\(\displaystyle \max_{a\in C}\sum_{v\in D_1}\mu_{v,a}+\max_{a\in C}\sum_{v\in D_2}\mu_{v,a}.\)

This value is attained by allocating all mass in each component to its district and choosing a maximizing representative there. Representatives may coincide. Comparing this value with \(q\) settles this case.

Henceforth, assume that \(G\) is connected.

2. Score bounds and repeated representatives

For each alternative \(a\), define its total approving mass by

\(\displaystyle W_a=\sum_{v\in V^+}\mu_{v,a}.\)

If both representatives equal \(a\), satisfaction is always exactly \(W_a\), because every type approving \(a\) is satisfied regardless of its allocation. Splitting every positive type equally between the two districts gives \(S_1=S_2=V^+\), so both supports are nonempty and connected.

Therefore, if \(q\leq\max_{a\in C}W_a\), the instance is feasible.

For distinct representatives \(r,s\), assign \(r\) to district \(1\) and \(s\) to district \(2\). Set \(U_{r,s}=W_r+W_s\). Conservation gives the identity

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{sat}(x)=U_{r,s}-\sum_{v\in V^+}\bigl(x_{v,r,2}+x_{v,s,1}\bigr).\)

In particular, satisfaction cannot exceed \(U_{r,s}\). Equality holds precisely when

\(x_{v,r,2}=x_{v,s,1}=0\quad\text{for every }v\in V^+.\)

This follows because every summand in the loss is nonnegative.

3. Every strict threshold below the bound is feasible

Lemma. For distinct representatives \(r,s\), if \(q<U_{r,s}\), there is a feasible allocation.

Proof. Write \(U=U_{r,s}\), and choose the positive rational

\(\displaystyle \varepsilon=\min\left\{\frac14,\frac{U-q}{2}\right\}.\)

Allocate a fraction \(1-\varepsilon\) of every type approving \(r\) to district \(1\), and its remaining fraction to district \(2\). Allocate types approving \(s\) symmetrically. Split every other positive type equally between the districts.

Both districts receive positive mass at every occupied cell. Thus \(S_1=S_2=V^+\), and connectivity follows from the assumed connectivity of \(G\).

The satisfaction is \((1-\varepsilon)U\). Since \(U\leq1\),

\(\displaystyle (1-\varepsilon)U\geq U-\varepsilon\geq U-\frac{U-q}{2}=\frac{U+q}{2}>q.\)

All allocation and threshold requirements are therefore satisfied. \(\square\)

The equality case requires a separate argument: arbitrarily small positive losses cannot meet an exact threshold.

4. A complete criterion for attaining equality

Fix distinct representatives \(r,s\) with \(W_r>0\) and \(W_s>0\). Let \(t\) be the third alternative, and define

\(T_1=\{v:\mu_{v,r}>0\},\qquad T_2=\{v:\mu_{v,s}>0\},\)

\(A_1=\{v:\mu_{v,r}+\mu_{v,t}>0\},\qquad A_2=\{v:\mu_{v,s}+\mu_{v,t}>0\}.\)

All these sets are subsets of \(V^+\).

Choose any \(u_1\in T_1\), and let \(K_1\) be its connected component in \(G[A_1]\). Likewise, choose any \(u_2\in T_2\), and let \(K_2\) be its connected component in \(G[A_2]\).

Lemma. An allocation attaining satisfaction \(U_{r,s}\) exists if and only if

\(T_1\subseteq K_1,\qquad T_2\subseteq K_2,\qquad K_1\cup K_2=V^+.\)

Necessity. Equality in the score bound forces all mass approving \(r\) into district \(1\), and all mass approving \(s\) into district \(2\). Hence \(T_1\subseteq S_1\) and \(T_2\subseteq S_2\).

District \(1\) receives no mass approving \(s\). Every cell in its support must therefore have positive mass approving \(r\) or \(t\), so \(S_1\subseteq A_1\). Similarly, \(S_2\subseteq A_2\).

Because \(S_1\) is connected and contains \(u_1\), it lies within \(K_1\). Thus \(T_1\subseteq K_1\); the corresponding conclusion holds for district \(2\). Finally,

\(V^+=S_1\cup S_2\subseteq K_1\cup K_2\subseteq V^+,\)

which proves the covering condition.

Sufficiency. Suppose the three conditions hold. Allocate all mass approving \(r\) to district \(1\), and all mass approving \(s\) to district \(2\). Allocate mass approving \(t\) as follows:

  • At cells in \(K_1\setminus K_2\), allocate it entirely to district \(1\).
  • At cells in \(K_2\setminus K_1\), allocate it entirely to district \(2\).
  • At cells in \(K_1\cap K_2\), split it equally.

These rules cover every occupied cell and conserve every type’s mass.

We verify that the resulting supports are exactly \(K_1,K_2\). At a cell in \(K_1\setminus K_2\), the inclusion \(T_2\subseteq K_2\) implies \(\mu_{v,s}=0\). All its positive mass therefore goes to district \(1\). The symmetric statement holds in \(K_2\setminus K_1\).

At a cell in the intersection, positive mass approving \(t\) supplies both districts. If that mass is zero, membership in both \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) implies \(\mu_{v,r}>0\) and \(\mu_{v,s}>0\); these masses again supply both districts.

Consequently, the supports are exactly the nonempty connected sets \(K_1,K_2\). Every type approving either selected representative is satisfied, giving satisfaction \(U_{r,s}\). \(\square\)

5. Decision algorithm and correctness

First compute the connected components of the occupied graph \(G\).

  • If there are more than two components, reject.
  • If there are exactly two, compare \(q\) with the maximum derived in Section 1.
  • If there is one component, compute all totals \(W_a\). Accept if \(q\leq\max_a W_a\).
  • Otherwise, examine every unordered pair of distinct alternatives \(r,s\):
  • If \(q>U_{r,s}\), discard the pair.
  • If \(q<U_{r,s}\), accept by Section 3.
  • If \(q=U_{r,s}\), apply the criterion in Section 4 and accept if it holds.
  • Reject if no pair succeeds.

The equality test’s hypotheses hold whenever it is reached. Indeed, Step 3 has already established \(q>\max_a W_a\). If \(q=W_r+W_s\), neither \(W_r\) nor \(W_s\) can be zero.

Every acceptance has an explicit feasible allocation from the preceding proofs. Conversely, any feasible allocation uses either repeated representatives, covered by Step 3, or a distinct pair. For that pair, the score bound excludes \(q>U_{r,s}\); strict inequality is covered by Section 3, and equality necessarily passes the criterion in Section 4. Thus the algorithm is complete.

6. Running time and exact arithmetic

The support list constructs the masses and occupied cells without introducing any unlisted positive types. There are at most \(\tau\) occupied cells.

Using adjacency lists, the initial graph computation takes \(O(|P|+|F|+\tau)\) operations for \(m=3\). Each equality test uses two graph searches and a scan of the occupied cells, taking \(O(|P|+|F|)\) operations. There are at most \(O(m^2)\) representative pairs.

For exact arithmetic, take the product of the denominators appearing in the masses and threshold as a common denominator. Its binary length is \(O(L)\), since the sum of the input denominator lengths is at most \(L\). Scaling the inputs and performing all subsequent comparisons therefore uses integers of \(O(L)\) bits. Ordinary integer arithmetic yields the conservative bit bound

\(\displaystyle O\!\left(m^2(|P|+|F|+\tau)L^2\right).\)

For \(m=3\), this is \(O((|P|+|F|+\tau)L^2)\). The constructions also produce rational allocations of polynomial encoding length.

7. Why exact equality matters

Consider a three-cell path whose endpoint cells each carry mass \(1/4\) approving \(a_1\), while its middle cell carries mass \(1/2\) approving \(a_2\). Alternative \(a_3\) has no mass.

Every threshold \(q<1\) is feasible by Section 3. Threshold \(q=1\) is infeasible: full satisfaction requires representatives \(a_1,a_2\), with both endpoints entirely in the first district and the middle cell entirely in the second. The first support is disconnected. Thus the supremum satisfaction need not be attained.

The discrete hardness result concerns a partition into disjoint connected districts. [Talmon, Theorem 1](https://www.ifaamas.org/AAMAS/aamas2017/proceedings/pdfs/p633.pdf) Continuous splitting and overlapping supports change that requirement, and the algorithm above settles the stated continuous problem.
