The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite rational supports \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) of upstream and downstream user types, with masses \(\mu_t\) and type-specific endowments, benefit parameters, pollution contributions, and fallback payoffs, aggregate utilities and fallback payoffs using \(\mu_t\) and the paper's forms in Eqs. (4)–(5). Decide whether a common nonnegative transfer rate \(\alpha\) and type-level water allocations \(x_t\) satisfy both associations' aggregate participation conditions against their ATS/ATI fallbacks, and compute a feasible agreement if one exists.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: disjoint nonempty lists \(T_1,T_2\) of upstream and downstream types, with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Let \(\tau=|T_1|+|T_2|\) be the support size, not the number of possible types; there are \(m=2\) associations, and \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Each type has rational endowment \(e_t>0\), benefit parameters \(a_t,b_t>0\), opportunity-cost parameter \(\beta_t\geq0\), and supplied disagreement payoff \(d_t\in\mathbb Q\), designated ATS upstream and ATI downstream. Write \(f_t(x)=a_tx-b_tx^2/2\); upstream types additionally have rational pollution coefficients \(c_t\geq0\).
An agreement chooses real per-capita allocations \(x_t\) and a common rate \(\alpha\geq0\). Define \(M_2=\sum_{s\in T_2}\mu_s\), total compensatory water \(P=\sum_{t\in T_1}\mu_tc_tx_t\), and downstream compensatory water per capita \(p=P/M_2\).
Require \(0\leq x_t\leq e_t/(1+c_t)\) upstream, \(x_s\geq e_s+p\) downstream, and \(\sum_{t\in T_1\cup T_2}\mu_tx_t=\sum_{t\in T_1\cup T_2}\mu_te_t\). Traded water must be positive: \(Q=\sum_{t\in T_1}\mu_t[e_t-(1+c_t)x_t]>0\).
Utilities are \(u_t=f_t(x_t)+\alpha[e_t-(1+c_t)x_t]+\beta_tc_tx_t\) upstream and \(u_s=f_s(x_s)-\alpha(x_s-e_s-p)-\beta_sp\) downstream; the resource constraints ensure that aggregate payments balance.
Participation requires \(\sum_{t\in T_i}\mu_tu_t\geq\sum_{t\in T_i}\mu_td_t\) for each \(i\in\{1,2\}\). Decide whether such an agreement exists and compute one when it does.

Is this agreement-feasibility problem in \(\mathrm P\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For two individual agents, agreement is characterized by the derived clearing price lying between the upstream reservation-price lower bound and downstream reservation-price upper bound; this is an analytic characterization, not a hardness theorem ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.05844)).
A later bilateral model reports a unique equilibrium making both agents better off ([source](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12597-021-00555-z)); no complexity result for the specified support-based mass problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalize masses globally to one, interpret endowments and allocations per capita, and require both associations to have positive mass.
  • Distribute aggregate upstream compensatory water across downstream types in proportion to their masses, giving every downstream individual exposure P/M_2; the bilateral definition supplies no heterogeneous distribution rule.
  • Restore the pollution-adjusted upstream consumption cap and downstream compensatory-water floor used in the bilateral analysis.
  • Require full water allocation to preserve balanced monetary transfers under the common rate.
  • Require positive traded water, interpreting agreement as participation in trade rather than a zero-trade disagreement allocation.
  • Use positive endowments and quadratic benefit coefficients, nonnegative opportunity-cost parameters and pollution coefficients, and real nonnegative allocations and transfer rates. Interpret the pollution coefficient as a per-unit rate, rather than imposing the bound on total negative water directly on that coefficient.
  • Retain the graded question's supplied fallback payoffs as fixed rational input utilities. The bilateral downstream comparison instead evaluates a fallback at a consumption-dependent compensatory-water level, so this convention is an explicit extension.
  • Use weak aggregate participation inequalities, matching the displayed bilateral participation conditions; impose no individual-type participation or best-response requirement.

Result

For the specified problem with \(m=2\), agreement feasibility is in \(\mathrm P\). A feasible agreement can also be computed exactly in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), using real-algebraic representations for its coordinates.

The proof uses separability to reduce the allocation problem to four real parameters. It handles the strict requirement \(Q>0\) exactly.

1. Eliminating the common price

Define the rational constants
\(D_i=\sum_{t\in T_i}\mu_t d_t\),
\(E_i=\sum_{t\in T_i}\mu_t e_t\), and
\(E=E_1+E_2\).

For an allocation satisfying the resource constraints, define its aggregate utilities before payments by

\(A(x)=\sum_{t\in T_1}\mu_t\bigl(f_t(x_t)+\beta_t c_t x_t\bigr)\),

\(B(x)=\sum_{s\in T_2}\mu_s f_s(x_s)-p\sum_{s\in T_2}\mu_s\beta_s\).

Write \(R=\sum_{t\in T_1}\mu_t x_t\). Conservation and \(M_2p=P\) imply

\(\sum_{s\in T_2}\mu_s(x_s-e_s-p)=E-R-E_2-P=E_1-R-P=Q\).

Consequently, aggregate association utilities are

\(U_1=A(x)+\alpha Q\), and \(U_2=B(x)-\alpha Q\).

When \(Q>0\), the variable \(w=\alpha Q\) ranges over all nonnegative real numbers. Participation is therefore equivalent to the existence of \(w\) satisfying

\(\max\{0,D_1-A(x)\}\leq w\leq B(x)-D_2\).

This interval is nonempty precisely when

\(B(x)\geq D_2\), and \(A(x)+B(x)\geq D_1+D_2\). (1)

Indeed, its upper endpoint must be at least both \(0\) and \(D_1-A(x)\), giving exactly these two inequalities.

Thus agreement feasibility is equivalent to allocation feasibility together with \(Q>0\) and (1). Once such an allocation is found, a valid rate is

\(\displaystyle \alpha=\frac{\max\{0,D_1-A(x)\}}{Q}\). (2)

2. Canonical allocations for fixed upstream water and pollution

Consider any feasible allocation, and fix its values of \(R\) and \(P\). These values also fix \(p=P/M_2\) and \(Q=E_1-R-P\).

We can replace its upstream and downstream allocations independently by utility-maximizing allocations with the same aggregate quantities.

Upstream replacement

Put \(h_t=e_t/(1+c_t)>0\). Maximize

\(\sum_{t\in T_1}\mu_t\bigl((a_t+\beta_t c_t)y_t-b_t y_t^2/2\bigr)\)

subject to

\(0\leq y_t\leq h_t\),
\(\sum_{t\in T_1}\mu_t y_t=R\), and
\(\sum_{t\in T_1}\mu_t c_t y_t=P\). (3)

The original upstream allocation makes this feasible. Its feasible set is compact, and the objective is strictly concave because every \(\mu_t b_t>0\). Hence a unique maximizer exists.

This replacement increases or preserves \(A\), and preserves \(R,P,p,Q\).

Downstream replacement

Independently, maximize

\(\sum_{s\in T_2}\mu_s f_s(y_s)\)

subject to

\(y_s\geq e_s+p\), and
\(\sum_{s\in T_2}\mu_s y_s=E-R\). (4)

Again, the original allocation establishes nonemptiness. Since \(p\geq0\), all coordinates are positive; the weighted-sum equality gives the finite bounds
\(y_s\leq(E-R)/\mu_s\).
Thus the feasible set is compact. Strict concavity gives a unique maximizer.

The pollution-cost term in \(B\) is constant when \(p\) is fixed. This replacement therefore increases or preserves \(B\).

Both replacements preserve all resource constraints and \(Q>0\), while preserving (1).

It follows that whenever an agreement exists, one exists whose allocations solve (3) and (4) for their own values of \(R,P\).

3. Multiplier formulas, including boundary cases

We next express these canonical allocations using only three multipliers and \(p\).

The multiplier existence used here does not require strict feasibility. To justify it, let \(y^*\) maximize a differentiable concave function \(F\) over a polyhedron. For every feasible \(y\), the segment from \(y^*\) to \(y\) is feasible, so optimality implies

\(\nabla F(y^*)\mathbin{\cdot}(y-y^*)\leq0\).

Hence \(y^*\) also maximizes the linear objective
\(\nabla F(y^*)\mathbin{\cdot}y\)
over that polyhedron. Linear-programming duality, equivalently the polyhedral normal-cone form of Farkas’ lemma, supplies equality multipliers and nonnegative multipliers for the active bounds. This applies even when the equalities are dependent or the feasible set has empty interior.

Upstream formula

Applying this observation to (3), there are unrestricted real multipliers \(\lambda,\eta\) such that, with

\(H_t=a_t+\beta_t c_t-\lambda-\eta c_t\),

the maximizing allocation satisfies:

  • If \(y_t=0\), then \(H_t\leq0\).
  • If \(0<y_t<h_t\), then \(H_t-b_t y_t=0\).
  • If \(y_t=h_t\), then \(H_t-b_t h_t\geq0\).

For completeness, these follow from the stationarity equations

\(\mu_t(a_t+\beta_t c_t-b_t y_t)=\lambda\mu_t+\eta\mu_t c_t-\ell_t+v_t\),

where \(\ell_t,v_t\geq0\) vanish unless the lower or upper bound, respectively, is active. Division by the positive mass \(\mu_t\) gives the stated conditions.

Define
\(\operatorname{clip}_{[0,h]}(z)=\min\{h,\max\{0,z\}\}\).
The conditions above give

\(\displaystyle y_t=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,h_t]}\left(\frac{a_t+\beta_t c_t-\lambda-\eta c_t}{b_t}\right)\). (5)

Downstream formula

Applying the same argument to (4), there is an unrestricted real multiplier \(\kappa\) with

\(\mu_s(a_s-b_s y_s)=\kappa\mu_s-\ell_s\),

where \(\ell_s\geq0\) and \(\ell_s=0\) whenever \(y_s>e_s+p\).

At an interior coordinate, \(y_s=(a_s-\kappa)/b_s\). At the lower bound, stationarity gives
\((a_s-\kappa)/b_s\leq e_s+p\).
Consequently,

\(\displaystyle y_s=\max\left\{e_s+p,\frac{a_s-\kappa}{b_s}\right\}\). (6)

These formulas cover all boundary and degenerate cases allowed by the premises.

4. An equivalent problem in four real variables

Let \(z=(\lambda,\eta,\kappa,p)\in\mathbb R^4\). Define \(X_t(z)\) and \(X_s(z)\) by (5) and (6), respectively, and put

\(P(z)=\sum_{t\in T_1}\mu_t c_tX_t(z)\),

\(Q(z)=E_1-\sum_{t\in T_1}\mu_t(1+c_t)X_t(z)\),

\(A(z)=\sum_{t\in T_1}\mu_t\bigl(f_t(X_t(z))+\beta_t c_tX_t(z)\bigr)\),

\(B(z)=\sum_{s\in T_2}\mu_s f_s(X_s(z))-p\sum_{s\in T_2}\mu_s\beta_s\).

Consider the following conditions on these four variables:

  • \(p\geq0\).
  • \(P(z)=M_2p\).
  • \(\sum_{t\in T_1}\mu_tX_t(z)+\sum_{s\in T_2}\mu_sX_s(z)=E\).
  • \(Q(z)>0\).
  • \(B(z)\geq D_2\).
  • \(A(z)+B(z)\geq D_1+D_2\). (7)

Claim. The original agreement problem is feasible if and only if (7) has a solution.

Proof. Given an agreement, Section 2 produces canonical allocations with the same \(R,P,Q\) and with no smaller \(A,B\). Section 3 supplies \(\lambda,\eta,\kappa\) representing those allocations. Together with their \(p\), these parameters satisfy (7).

Conversely, suppose \(z\) satisfies (7). Formula (5) enforces every upstream bound, while (6) enforces every downstream lower bound. Conditions 2 and 3 enforce compensation consistency and water conservation. Conditions 4–6 give positive trade and the price-eliminated participation conditions. Equation (2) then supplies a valid nonnegative rate. This proves the claim. \(\square\)

5. Exact polynomial-time solution of the four-variable problem

Although (7) contains clipping functions, their branches can be enumerated in polynomial time.

The branches are determined by the signs of the following affine polynomials in \(z\):

\(H_t=a_t+\beta_t c_t-\lambda-\eta c_t\),

\(H_t-b_t h_t\),

\(J_s=a_s-\kappa-b_s(e_s+p)\).

There are
\(N=2|T_1|+|T_2|\leq2\tau\)
such polynomials.

On any nonempty sign cell, every allocation coordinate is affine:

  • Upstream, \(X_t\) equals \(0\), \(H_t/b_t\), or \(h_t\).
  • Downstream, \(X_s\) equals \(e_s+p\) or \((a_s-\kappa)/b_s\).

On a threshold equality, the adjoining formulas agree. Thus cells with zero signs must be included, and cause no ambiguity.

Polynomially many sign cells

An arrangement of \(N\) affine hyperplanes in \(\mathbb R^4\), including all its faces, has \(O((N+1)^4)\) nonempty sign cells.

Here is a justification covering degeneracies. A sign cell of codimension \(k\) has an affine hull defined by \(k\) independent hyperplanes from the arrangement. There are at most \(\binom Nk\) choices. In that affine hull, the cell is a full-dimensional region of the restricted arrangement. An arrangement in dimension \(4-k\) has at most
\(\sum_{j=0}^{4-k}\binom Nj\)
regions, by the usual recurrence obtained upon adding one hyperplane. Summing over \(0\leq k\leq4\) gives the asserted polynomial bound. Multiple counting only increases this upper bound.

The cells can be enumerated incrementally. Start with \(\mathbb R^4\); when adding an affine polynomial, test each existing cell with its sign set to negative, zero, and positive, retaining the nonempty intersections. The bound above ensures only polynomially many tests.

The exact algebraic algorithm used

We use the standard fixed-dimensional semialgebraic sampling theorem:

For fixed numbers of variables and fixed degree bound, a set described by a Boolean formula over rational polynomial equalities and strict or weak inequalities can be tested for emptiness in deterministic polynomial bit time. If nonempty, an exact real-algebraic sample point can be returned in polynomial bit time and polynomial output size. Unbounded sets and sets of empty interior are allowed.

This is a consequence of the semialgebraic roadmap and sampling results stated with coefficient bit bounds in [Basu’s survey, Section 3.1.1, Theorem 3.6 and its sampling consequence](https://www.math.purdue.edu/~sbasu/raag_survey2011_final.pdf).

First apply this theorem to the linear systems arising during sign-cell enumeration: there are four variables, degree at most one, and polynomially many constraints.

Next, for each retained cell, substitute its affine allocation formulas into (7). The resulting conditions consist of:

  • The affine sign conditions defining the cell.
  • Affine compensation and conservation equalities.
  • The affine inequalities \(p\geq0\) and \(Q(z)>0\).
  • Two polynomial inequalities of degree at most two, expressing participation.

The sampling theorem therefore applies again, now with four variables and degree bound two, both independent of \(\tau\).

Each cell uses \(O(\tau)\) polynomials. Their rational coefficients have bit lengths polynomial in \(L\): they are formed from the input by rational products, divisions by positive \(b_t,1+c_t,M_2\), and sums over the support. Clearing denominators using positive integers preserves all signs and produces integer coefficients of polynomial bit length.

There are polynomially many cells and polynomial work per cell. Hence the complete algorithm runs in time polynomial in \(\tau,L\). In particular, it tests the strict inequality \(Q>0\) directly, without substituting a numerical tolerance.

6. Computing an agreement

If every cell fails its feasibility test, the equivalence in Section 4 proves that no agreement exists.

Otherwise, let \(z^*\) be the exact algebraic sample returned for a successful cell. Output

\(x_t=X_t(z^*)\) for every supported type, and
\(\displaystyle \alpha=\frac{\max\{0,D_1-A(z^*)\}}{Q(z^*)}\).

The denominator is positive by (7). Section 1 proves participation, and Section 4 proves all resource constraints.

The sample point can be represented using a common real algebraic number \(\theta\), specified by an integer polynomial and root-isolating data, with its four coordinates given as rational functions of \(\theta\). The allocations are affine functions of those coordinates, and the rate is a rational function after an exact sign comparison. They therefore retain polynomial-size exact representations and can be computed in polynomial time.

The algorithm uses each supplied mass only as a binary rational coefficient. Its complexity depends on the encoded support size, not on an expansion of the masses into individual agents. This settles the specified problem with \(m=2\).
