The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational masses \(\lambda_r\) over finitely many reported-ranking blocks, per-capita chore supplies \(\rho\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^q\), and latent additive cost vectors consistent with each ranking, choose a mass-serial block order, integer quota densities \(a_\ell\) satisfying \(\sum_\ell\lambda_\ell a_\ell=\sum_j\rho_j\), and a feasible measurable allocation of integral bundles \(B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^q\) minimizing the worst-case ratio \(c\cdot B/\mathsf{MMS}^{\infty}(c,\rho)\); determine whether the optimum is \(\Theta(\log R)\) for \(R=\sum_j\rho_j\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A unit mass of agents is partitioned into reported-ranking blocks of rational masses \(\lambda_\ell\). There are \(q\) chore types with rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_j\), and each agent receives an integral bundle \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^q\). An additive cost vector \(c\ge0\) is consistent with a block’s ranking when its coordinates follow that ranking.
For a cost vector \(c\), define \(\mathsf{MMS}^{\infty}(c,\rho)=\inf_{\mu:\,\mathbb E_\mu B=\rho}\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{B\sim\mu}c\cdot B\), where \(\mu\) ranges over distributions on integral bundles.
A mass-serial allocation orders the blocks, assigns each block an integer quota \(a_\ell\) with \(\sum_\ell\lambda_\ell a_\ell=R:=\sum_j\rho_j\), and lets each block take \(\lambda_\ell a_\ell\) mass of its cheapest remaining chore types. Its agents receive integral bundles of size \(a_\ell\) whose aggregate matches those picks. Minimize the worst ratio \(c\cdot B/\mathsf{MMS}^{\infty}(c,\rho)\) over agents, consistent cost vectors with positive denominator, and admissible allocations.
Target. Is deciding whether this minimum is at most a given rational threshold in \(\mathsf P\), or is it NP-hard? Does the worst-case minimum for \(R\ge2\) grow as \(\Theta(\log R)\), or is it \(o(\log R)\)?
Nearest known results. For finitely many agents and indivisible chores, the best consecutive-picking rule has worst-case MMS approximation ratio \(\Theta(\log(m/n))\). That result establishes neither a complexity classification nor a bound for the mass model above.

Prover output (gpt-6-astra / medium, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The decision problem is NP-hard, even when admissible allocations exist and \(2<R<3\). The proposed asymptotic dichotomy does not hold: the optimal ratio is unbounded over feasible instances with the fixed value \(R=5/2\). If one additionally restricts \(R\) to integers, the worst-case optimum is exactly \(R\).

These conclusions use the stated allowance of zero supplies. Blocks in all constructions can have distinct reported rankings.

1. A reduction to integer quotas

First consider an instance with just one positive-supply chore type, of supply \(R>0\). All other types have supply zero. Write \(c_*\) for the cost of the positive-supply type.

Any distribution with the prescribed expectation contains no zero-supply chores almost surely. Consequently,
\[ \mathsf{MMS}^{\infty}(c,\rho)=c_*\lceil R\rceil. \]
Indeed, a distribution supported on bundles containing at most \(\lceil R\rceil-1\) copies cannot have mean \(R\). Conversely, mixing bundles with \(\lfloor R\rfloor\) and \(\lceil R\rceil\) copies achieves mean \(R\) and maximum cost \(c_*\lceil R\rceil\).

For any feasible quota vector, every block receives only the positive-supply type, irrespective of the serial order. Its agents therefore have cost \(a_\ell c_*\). For every consistent cost vector with positive denominator, the ratio is \(a_\ell/\lceil R\rceil\). Thus the optimal ratio is exactly
\[ \frac{1}{\lceil R\rceil} \min\left\{\max_\ell a_\ell: a_\ell\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0},\ \sum_\ell\lambda_\ell a_\ell=R\right\}. \tag{1} \]

Distinct rankings impose no obstacle. For any number of blocks, introduce sufficiently many zero-supply types and give the blocks distinct permutations of those types. The positive-supply type can occupy the same position in every ranking.

2. NP-hardness, with feasibility guaranteed and \(2<R<3\)

We reduce from the standard NP-complete problem SUBSET SUM. Its input consists of positive integers \(w_1,\ldots,w_n\) and a target \(t\), with \(1\le t\le S:=\sum_iw_i\). The question is whether some subset sums to \(t\).

Set
\[ H=4S+1,\qquad L=\sum_{i=1}^n8^{i-1}. \]
Introduce \(2n+1\) positive integer weights:
\[ A_i=4(H8^{i-1}+w_i),\qquad D_i=4H8^{i-1},\qquad E=1. \]
Let
\[ W=\sum_i(A_i+D_i)+E=8HL+4S+1, \qquad U=4(HL+t). \]
Make one block for each weight, with mass equal to that weight divided by \(W\). Use the one-positive-supply construction from Section 1, with
\[ R=3-\frac UW. \]

We have \(U>0\) and
\[ W-U=4HL+4(S-t)+1>0. \]
Hence \(2<R<3\), so \(\lceil R\rceil=3\). The decision threshold will be \(1\).

The instance is always feasible: give the block of weight \(E=1\) quota \(3W-U\), and give every other block quota zero. Their weighted quota sum is exactly \(R\).

By (1), the answer at threshold \(1\) is yes exactly when there are quotas in \(\{0,1,2,3\}\) satisfying
\[ \sum_i(A_i a_i+D_i b_i)+e=3W-U. \]
Define the integer deficits
\[ x_i=3-a_i,\qquad y_i=3-b_i,\qquad z=3-e. \]
The same condition becomes
\[ \sum_i(A_i x_i+D_i y_i)+z=U, \qquad x_i,y_i,z\in\{0,1,2,3\}. \tag{2} \]

All terms except \(z\) are divisible by \(4\). Therefore (2) forces \(z=0\). Dividing by \(4\) gives
\[ H\sum_i8^{i-1}(x_i+y_i)+\sum_iw_ix_i=HL+t. \tag{3} \]
Because
\[ 0\le\sum_iw_ix_i\le3S<H,\qquad 0<t<H, \]
reduction modulo \(H\) in (3) implies
\[ \sum_iw_ix_i=t. \]
Subtracting this equality from (3) yields
\[ \sum_i8^{i-1}(x_i+y_i)=L. \]
Each coefficient \(x_i+y_i\) lies between zero and six. Uniqueness of base-eight representation therefore gives \(x_i+y_i=1\) for every \(i\). In particular, \(x_i\in\{0,1\}\), and the indices with \(x_i=1\) solve the SUBSET SUM instance.

Conversely, any subset summing to \(t\) gives a solution by taking its indicator as \(x_i\), setting \(y_i=1-x_i\), and taking \(z=0\). Reversing the deficit substitution gives admissible quotas bounded by three.

All constructed integers have polynomial bit length: the largest power \(8^{n-1}\) has \(O(n)\) bits. Distinct rankings can also be specified using polynomially many dummy types.

This proves NP-hardness. No claim of membership in NP for the full problem is needed here.

3. Unbounded ratio at the fixed value \(R=5/2\)

Let \(P\ge8\) be any even integer. Take two blocks with masses
\[ \lambda_1=\frac1P,\qquad \lambda_2=\frac{P-1}{P}, \]
and one positive-supply type with supply \(5/2\). One additional zero-supply type allows the blocks to have distinct rankings.

Writing the quotas as \(a,b\), feasibility is exactly
\[ a+(P-1)b=\frac{5P}{2}. \]
Nonnegativity implies
\[ b\le\frac{5P}{2(P-1)}<3, \]
so \(b\in\{0,1,2\}\). For these three possibilities, the smallest attainable maximum quota occurs at \(b=2\), where
\[ a=\frac P2+2. \]
Indeed, decreasing \(b\) increases \(a\) by \(P-1\), and even at \(b=2\) we have \(a>b\).

Equation (1) now gives the exact optimum:
\[ \operatorname{OPT}=\frac{P/2+2}{3}. \]
This tends to infinity while \(R=5/2\) remains fixed. Thus, as stated, there is no finite worst-case bound depending only on \(R\).

This phenomenon also occurs at arbitrarily large fixed noninteger values. For any integer \(r\ge2\), set \(R=r+1/2\) and choose even \(P>2r+2\). The same calculation gives the exact optimum
\[ \operatorname{OPT}=\frac{P/2+r}{r+1}. \]

4. For integer \(R\), the exact worst-case value is \(R\)

This additional result shows that imposing integer total supply still does not recover logarithmic growth.

*Universal upper bound.*

Let \(R=r\) be a positive integer. Give every block quota \(r\), which always satisfies the aggregate quota constraint.

Every aggregate pick vector of a block can be realized by integral bundles of size \(r\). Specifically, if a block of mass \(\lambda\) picks amounts \(p_j\), distribute the bundle \(r e_j\) to the fraction \(p_j/(\lambda r)\) of its agents. These fractions sum to one and produce exactly the required aggregates.

For any type \(j\) with \(\rho_j>0\),
\[ \mathsf{MMS}^{\infty}(c,\rho)\ge c_j. \]
To justify this, every distribution with mean \(\rho\) has positive probability of containing at least one copy of type \(j\). Its essential maximum cost is therefore at least \(c_j\); taking the infimum preserves this lower bound.

Every allocated bundle has size \(r\) and uses only positive-supply types. Consequently,
\[ c\cdot B\le r\max_{j:\rho_j>0}c_j \le r\,\mathsf{MMS}^{\infty}(c,\rho). \]
Thus every instance with integer \(R=r\) has optimum at most \(r\).

*Matching lower bound.*

Fix an integer \(r\ge2\), and let \(k=2r\). We first choose rational block masses satisfying
\[ \sum_{\ell=1}^k\lambda_\ell=1,\qquad \frac1{2k}<\lambda_\ell<\frac1r, \]
such that the only feasible integer quota vector is \(a_\ell=r\) for every block.

Here is a proof that such masses exist. The displayed inequalities define a nonempty relatively open subset of the simplex: the uniform vector lies in it. Every feasible quota satisfies \(a_\ell<2kr\), so only finitely many integer quota vectors need consideration. For any vector other than \((r,\ldots,r)\), the equation
\[ \sum_\ell\lambda_\ell a_\ell=r \]
either has no solutions in the simplex or defines a proper affine hyperplane there. Finitely many proper hyperplanes cannot cover a nonempty open subset. Their complement contains a rational point, since rational points are dense in the simplex. Choose that point as the mass vector.

Now introduce a cheap type of supply \(r-1\), an expensive type of supply \(1\), and zero-supply dummy types. Every block ranks the cheap type below the expensive type in cost; permuting the dummy types makes all rankings distinct.

Every admissible allocation must use quota \(r\) in every block. Consider the last block in any serial order. Its pick volume is
\[ r\lambda_{\mathrm{last}}<1. \]
Before this block acts, the earlier blocks have picked more than \(r-1\) total mass. Since all blocks prefer the cheap type, that type has been exhausted. The last block therefore receives only expensive chores, and each of its agents receives \(r\) copies.

Take the consistent cost vector assigning cost one to the expensive type and zero to every other type. Its MMS is exactly one: the expensive supply gives the lower bound one, and the deterministic bundle containing \(r-1\) cheap chores and one expensive chore achieves it. Every agent in the last block has ratio \(r\).

The lower bound holds for every order and every admissible allocation. Together with the upper bound, it proves that the worst-case optimum for integer \(R=r\) is exactly \(r\).
