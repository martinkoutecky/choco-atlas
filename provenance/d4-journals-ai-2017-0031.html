The input also contains fixed scores \(\hat\rho\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{C}\) and an arbitrary binary-rational initial state \(x^0\), where \(x^0_{t,a}\ge0\) and \(\sum_{a\in C}x^0_{t,a}=\mu_t\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.

1. Definitions and finite-state representation

A legal sequence is universally terminating from $x^0$ if every maximal legal sequence starting there is finite.

For each type $t$, define
$$ k_t=\bigl|\{a\in C:x^0_{t,a}>0\}\bigr|, \qquad K=\sum_{t\in T}k_t. $$
Positive type masses imply
$$ \tau\le K\le m\tau. $$

Label each positive initial entry as an atom, retaining its initial mass and type. Every reachable entry is the sum of the atoms of its type currently located at that candidate. A move transfers all those atoms together. Thus atoms can merge but cannot split.

An assignment of these $K$ labeled atoms to candidates determines a numerical state. Consequently, the number of reachable numerical states is at most
$$ N=m^K. $$

Let $D$ be the product of the denominators appearing in the input masses, initial entries, and fixed scores. Its binary length is $O(L)$. After scaling by $D$, every reachable entry and score is an integer with $O(L)$ bits: entries are sums of initial entries and are at most one before scaling, while
$$ \hat\rho(c)\le s_x(c)\le\hat\rho(c)+1. $$
Thus all winner and legality tests admit exact arithmetic with polynomial bit complexity.

For running-time statements, write
$$ H=L+K\log_2(m+1). $$
In particular,
$$ H\le L+m\tau\log_2(m+1). $$

2. A three-candidate cycle from a truthful state

Weighted direct replies can cycle, as discussed in [Meir et al., Example 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.05837). The following construction establishes this with just three candidates and independently verifies every move.

Take $C=\{a,b,c\}$ and the following five distinct supported rankings:

| Type | Mass | Ranking |
|---|---:|---|
| $1$ | $23/64$ | $a\succ_1 c\succ_1 b$ |
| $2$ | $2/64$ | $b\succ_2 a\succ_2 c$ |
| $3$ | $12/64$ | $b\succ_3 c\succ_3 a$ |
| $4$ | $7/64$ | $c\succ_4 a\succ_4 b$ |
| $5$ | $20/64$ | $c\succ_5 b\succ_5 a$ |

The masses are positive dyadic rationals and sum to one. Set
$$ \hat\rho=\frac{1}{64}(1,14,0). $$
Initially, each type places its entire mass at its first-ranked candidate. Thus the initial position tuple is
$$ (a,b,b,c,c). $$

In the table, positions record the candidates supported by types $1,\ldots,5$, and scores are multiplied by $64$.

| Positions | Scaled scores $(a,b,c)$ | Winner | Next move |
|---|---:|---|---|
| $(a,b,b,c,c)$ | $(24,28,27)$ | $b$ | Type $4$: $c\to a$ |
| $(a,b,b,a,c)$ | $(31,28,20)$ | $a$ | Type $3$: $b\to c$ |
| $(a,b,c,a,c)$ | $(31,16,32)$ | $c$ | Type $2$: $b\to a$ |
| $(a,a,c,a,c)$ | $(33,14,32)$ | $a$ | Type $5$: $c\to b$ |
| $(a,a,c,a,b)$ | $(33,34,12)$ | $b$ | Type $1$: $a\to c$ |
| $(c,a,c,a,b)$ | $(10,34,35)$ | $c$ | Type $3$: $c\to b$ |
| $(c,a,b,a,b)$ | $(10,46,23)$ | $b$ | Type $5$: $b\to c$ |
| $(c,a,b,a,c)$ | $(10,26,43)$ | $c$ | Type $1$: $c\to a$ |
| $(a,a,b,a,c)$ | $(33,26,20)$ | $a$ | Type $4$: $a\to c$ |
| $(a,a,b,c,c)$ | $(26,26,27)$ | $c$ | Type $2$: $a\to b$ |
| $(a,b,b,c,c)$ | $(24,28,27)$ | $b$ | Repeat |

Every destination is the unique resulting winner. The ten required preference improvements, in order, are
$$ a\succ_4 b,\quad c\succ_3 a,\quad a\succ_2 c,\quad b\succ_5 a,\quad c\succ_1 b, $$
$$ b\succ_3 c,\quad c\succ_5 b,\quad a\succ_1 c,\quad c\succ_4 a,\quad b\succ_2 c. $$
Each transfer moves an entire positive entry. Each type always has exactly one cohort, so no merging occurs.

The last state equals the first state. Repeating the ten moves therefore gives an infinite legal sequence. It is an admissible maximal sequence because no fairness condition restricts the scheduler.

A terminal state is also reachable

From the same truthful initial state, instead move type $1$ from $a$ to $c$. This gives positions
$$ (c,b,b,c,c) $$
and scaled scores
$$ (1,28,50). $$
The move is legal because $c$ becomes the unique winner and $c\succ_1 b$.

This state is terminal:

  • Type $1$ prefers only $a$ to $c$. Moving back produces scores $(24,28,27)$ and winner $b$, so that move is illegal.
  • Type $2$ prefers $b$ and $a$ to $c$. Its source is already $b$, so its only possible improving destination is $a$. That transfer produces $(3,26,50)$, still won by $c$.
  • Type $3$ prefers only $b$ to $c$, but its only positive entry is already at $b$.
  • Types $4$ and $5$ rank $c$ first.

Thus even with three candidates and a truthful initial state, existence of a reachable terminal state does not imply universal termination.

Three is the minimum candidate count permitting a cycle

For $m=1$, no move exists.

For $m=2$, every legal destination must be the moving type’s preferred candidate, and the source must be its less-preferred candidate. After the transfer, the less-preferred entry is zero. It can never receive another legal transfer, because the less-preferred candidate cannot be an improving destination.

Hence each type moves at most once, and every sequence has at most $\tau$ moves. Together with the construction above, this proves that the minimum candidate count permitting a cycle is exactly three.

3. A sharper move bound under universal termination

Define the falling factorial
$$ (m)_r=m(m-1)\cdots(m-r+1). $$

Let $p(x)$ be the number of positive entries. A move into an empty entry preserves $p(x)$; a move into a positive entry decreases it by exactly one. Every type retains positive total mass, so any sequence has at most
$$ h=K-\tau $$
merging moves.

Between consecutive merging moves, the partition of initial atoms into current cohorts remains fixed. If type $t$ has $r_t$ cohorts during such a phase, those cohorts occupy distinct candidates. There are therefore at most
$$ P(r)=\prod_{t\in T}(m)_{r_t} $$
possible labeled cohort placements during that phase.

On a universally terminating instance, a numerical state cannot repeat along a legal sequence: the intervening closed walk could otherwise be repeated forever. A phase consequently has at most $P(r)-1$ nonmerging moves.

If a sequence has $q$ merges and phase cohort-count vectors
$$ r^{(0)},r^{(1)},\ldots,r^{(q)}, $$
its total number of moves is at most
$$ \sum_{i=0}^{q}\bigl(P(r^{(i)})-1\bigr)+q = \sum_{i=0}^{q}P(r^{(i)})-1. $$

An explicit polynomial-time computable bound

Set
$$ P_0=\prod_{t\in T}(m)_{k_t}. $$
Form the multiset
$$ \mathcal D= \biguplus_{t\in T} \{m-k_t+1,m-k_t+2,\ldots,m-1\}. $$
It has $h=K-\tau$ elements. List them in nondecreasing order:
$$ d_1\le d_2\le\cdots\le d_h. $$
Define
$$ P_i=\frac{P_0}{d_1d_2\cdots d_i} \quad (1\le i\le h), \qquad B=\sum_{i=0}^{h}P_i-1. $$

These quantities are integers. Indeed, reducing a type’s cohort count from $r$ to $r-1$ divides its placement count by
$$ \frac{(m)_r}{(m)_{r-1}}=m-r+1. $$
For each type, these divisors occur in increasing order. Sorting all divisors therefore permits an order respecting each type’s required merge order.

After any $i$ actual merges, the placement count equals $P_0$ divided by a product of $i$ elements of $\mathcal D$. That product is at least $d_1\cdots d_i$. Thus its placement count is at most $P_i$. The preceding phase bound proves that every legal sequence on a universally terminating instance has at most
$$ U=\min\{m^K-1,B\} $$
moves. In particular,
$$ U\le \min\left\{ m^K-1,\; (K-\tau+1)\prod_{t\in T}(m)_{k_t}-1 \right\}. $$

All intermediate integers have $O(K\log(m+1))$ bits. Using elementary integer arithmetic, sorting and computing $U$ takes
$$ O\!\left(L+K^3\log^2(m+1)\right) $$
bit operations.

This is a polynomial-time computation of a valid upper bound. Neither its magnitude nor a terminal-state construction is thereby shown to be polynomially bounded.

4. General decision and construction algorithms

Universal termination is equivalent to absence of a reachable cycle

If a directed cycle of numerical states is reachable, a scheduler can reach it and repeat it indefinitely.

Conversely, an infinite legal sequence visits infinitely many states in a graph with at most $N=m^K$ vertices. Some state repeats, producing a nonempty directed closed walk and hence a directed cycle.

A finite maximal sequence ends at a terminal state by definition. Therefore universal termination is exactly the absence of a reachable directed cycle.

Polynomial-space upper bound

Nondeterministically attempt to execute exactly $N$ legal moves, rejecting a branch when it cannot continue.

A successful branch visits $N+1$ states and thus repeats a state. Conversely, a reachable cycle supplies a legal sequence of $N$ moves.

The algorithm stores the current numerical state, exact arithmetic workspace, and a counter with
$$ O(K\log(m+1)) $$
bits. This is polynomial space, and every branch halts.

Savitch’s theorem gives
$$ \mathrm{NPSPACE}=\mathrm{PSPACE}. $$
Deterministic polynomial space is closed under complement. Hence
$$ \boxed{\text{Universal termination belongs to }\mathrm{PSPACE}.} $$
This does not establish membership in $\mathrm P$ or hardness.

Exact exponential algorithm, including graph lookup

Use labeled atom assignments as vertices. An edge transfers all atoms of one type at a selected source to a selected destination, provided the corresponding numerical move is legal.

Every numerical legal trajectory lifts from the initial assignment by tracking its atoms. Conversely, every labeled path projects to a numerical legal trajectory. Consequently, an infinite numerical trajectory exists exactly when the reachable labeled graph contains a cycle: either trajectory is infinite in a finite corresponding graph.

There are at most $N$ vertices and at most $\tau m(m-1)$ outgoing edges per vertex.

Encode an assignment by its $K$ candidate indices. Store visited assignments in a binary trie. Each encoding has $O(K\log(m+1))$ bits, so insertion and lookup take that many bit operations; no linear scan through visited vertices is needed.

Exact arithmetic, successor construction, trie lookup, and graph analysis give the conservative bound
$$ O\!\left(H^3+m^K\tau m^2H^2\right). $$
Within this time one can:

  • Decide universal termination.
  • If termination holds, return a reachable sink, hence a terminal numerical state.
  • Compute the exact maximum sequence length by longest-path dynamic programming on the acyclic reachable graph.

Direct simulation under the termination promise

For a proposed transfer, only the source and destination scores change. The three highest current score-priority keys suffice to identify the highest unaffected competitor after excluding those two candidates. When fewer than three candidates exist, retain all candidates.

Scanning all possible moves therefore costs $O(\tau m^2H)$ bit operations per state. Under universal termination, simulation reaches a terminal state within
$$ O\!\left(H^3+(U+1)\tau m^2H\right) $$
bit operations.

This remains an exponential-time construction in general.

Because supported rankings are distinct, $\tau\le m!$. The exhaustive algorithm therefore has running time $g(m)L^{O(1)}$ for a computable function $g$. In particular, for every fixed $m$, all three exhaustive tasks above are polynomial-time solvable in $L$.

5. Two initially unsplit cohorts always terminate

Suppose $\tau=2$ and each type initially has exactly one positive entry. Each type thereafter retains exactly one cohort.

Assume, for contradiction, that a reachable nonempty closed walk exists. Choose one with the fewest moves.

Two cyclically consecutive moves cannot belong to the same type. Indeed, suppose that type moves its cohort from $a$ to $b$ and then from $b$ to $c$. If $c=a$, the numerical state returns to its state before these two moves, contradicting the two strict winner improvements. Otherwise, transferring directly from $a$ to $c$ produces the same final state. Its resulting winner is $c$, and transitivity makes it preferable to the original winner. This legal shortcut contradicts minimality.

Thus the two types alternate around the closed walk.

After any move, the moving type supports the current winner. If both cohorts coincide at some state, the next move separates them. Once separated, alternating moves cannot reunite them: the next mover would have to move to the other cohort’s candidate, which is the current winner and cannot be an improving destination. A closed walk therefore cannot contain a coincident state.

At every state of the closed walk, the next mover consequently supports a candidate different from the current winner. Its move leaves the current winner’s score unchanged. Since a different candidate becomes winner, the winning score-priority key strictly increases.

Strict increase around a closed walk is impossible. This contradiction proves universal termination.

There are at most $m^2$ states, so every sequence has at most
$$ m^2-1 $$
moves. Direct simulation constructs a terminal state in
$$ O(H^3+m^4H) $$
bit operations.

For completeness, if $\tau=1$ with arbitrary initial splitting, every move strictly improves the winner in the sole supported ranking. Thus every sequence has at most $m-1$ moves, and simulation takes $O(H^3+m^3H)$ bit operations.

The three-candidate construction rules out extending unconditional convergence from two to three candidates, even for truthful initial states and unique winners.

The general results establish a polynomial-space upper bound, an explicit exponential algorithm, and a polynomial-time computable exponential move bound. They do not settle the requested general decision classification or polynomial-time terminal-state construction.
