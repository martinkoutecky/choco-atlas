The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) item categories, a finite set \(\mathcal T\) of buyer types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and a WHT-sparse value function \(\tilde v_t:\{0,1\}^m\to\mathbb R_{\ge 0}\) for each type, find an optimal allocation of type mass to integral bundles. Equivalently, maximize \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^m} z_{t,x}\tilde v_t(x)\), subject to \(\sum_x z_{t,x}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,x}x_jz_{t,x}\le q_j\) for each category \(j\), and \(z_{t,x}\ge0\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(\mathcal T\) of \(\tau\ge1\) buyer types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to one. Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge1\) item categories with binary-rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\ge0\); each buyer receives an integral bundle \(x\in\{0,1\}^m\), including the empty bundle.
Each valuation is supplied explicitly by distinct frequencies \(S_t\subseteq\{0,1\}^m\) and nonzero signed binary-rational coefficients \(c_{t,y}\): \(\tilde v_t(x)=\sum_{y\in S_t}c_{t,y}(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^m y_jx_j}\). These are normalized Walsh–Hadamard coefficients, and \(\tilde v_t(x)\ge0\) for every bundle is promised; neither monotonicity nor \(\tilde v_t(0)=0\) is required.
Let \(k=\max_t|S_t|\) and let \(L\) be the total binary input length. Type mass may be split arbitrarily among bundles.
Find an exact welfare-maximizing allocation, represented by its positive bundle masses, for \(\max_{z\ge0}\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^m}z_{t,x}\tilde v_t(x)\), subject to \(\sum_xz_{t,x}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,x}x_jz_{t,x}\le q_j\) for every category.

Is this continuous allocation problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finitely many buyers and one unit of each item, Fourier-sparse welfare maximization has an exact mixed-integer formulation; this establishes no polynomial-time bound ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.10749)).
Related fractional configuration LPs are polynomial-time solvable with demand-oracle access ([source](https://theory.stanford.edu/~jvondrak/data/robust-welfare.pdf)); that access is not supplied here. No result settling this explicit-coefficient mass problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified the valuation encoding as explicit frequency vectors and signed binary-rational normalized Walsh–Hadamard coefficients, with sparsity included in the input rather than fixed.
  • Treated nonnegativity on all bundles as an input promise; imposed no monotonicity or empty-bundle normalization, which the discrete approximation definition does not require.
  • Specified nonnegative rational supplies and positive item and support counts; retained the stated total-mass normalization.
  • Specified unrestricted splitting of type mass and representation of an optimal allocation by its positive bundle masses.
  • Defined L as total binary input length and k as maximum Fourier support size.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The problem is NP-hard, even with \(\tau=1\), all supplies equal to \(\tfrac12\), and every nonconstant frequency supported on exactly two coordinates. The reduction below also satisfies \(\tilde v(0)=0\).

Taking all supplies equal to one would already embed unconstrained valuation maximization. We instead prove hardness with genuinely scarce supplies.

1. Source problem and construction. We reduce from unweighted Max-Cut: given a simple undirected graph \(G=(V,E)\) and an integer \(B\), decide whether some partition of \(V\) cuts at least \(B\) edges. This problem is NP-complete. [Garey, Johnson, and Stockmeyer, *Some simplified NP-complete graph problems*](https://www.math.ucdavis.edu/~deloera/MISC/LA-BIBLIO/trunk/JohnsonDavid2.pdf).

We may restrict to graphs with at least one edge, since edgeless instances are directly decidable. Label the vertices by \(V=\{1,\ldots,m\}\). Construct one buyer type of mass \(\mu=1\), and set \(q_j=\tfrac12\) for every category.

Write \(e_j\) for the binary vector with a single one in coordinate \(j\). Supply the following frequency list and coefficients:

  • The zero frequency, with coefficient \(c_0=|E|/2\).
  • For each edge \(\{u,v\}\in E\), the frequency \(e_u+e_v\), with coefficient \(c_{e_u+e_v}=-\tfrac12\).

Here \(e_u+e_v\) is a binary vector because the endpoints are distinct. Simplicity of the graph ensures that all listed frequencies are distinct. Every coefficient is nonzero, and the sparsity is \(k=|E|+1\).

The resulting valuation is exactly
\(\displaystyle \tilde v(x)=\frac{|E|}{2}-\frac12\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}(-1)^{x_u+x_v}=\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}\frac{1-(-1)^{x_u+x_v}}2.\)

For each edge, its summand is one when \(x_u\ne x_v\), and zero otherwise. Thus \(\tilde v(x)\) counts the edges crossing the partition encoded by \(x\). In particular, \(\tilde v(x)\ge0\) for every bundle, so the nonnegativity promise holds, and \(\tilde v(0)=0\).

This is precisely the stipulated Walsh–Hadamard character expansion; no additional normalization factor is needed.

2. The allocation optimum equals the maximum cut size. Define
\(\displaystyle C(G)=\max_{x\in\{0,1\}^m}\tilde v(x).\)

For our single type, write \(z_x\) for its bundle masses. Every feasible allocation satisfies \(z_x\ge0\) and \(\sum_xz_x=1\). Consequently,
\(\displaystyle \sum_xz_x\tilde v(x)\le\sum_xz_xC(G)=C(G).\)

This upper bound applies to every fractional allocation.

For the reverse bound, choose a maximum-cut vector \(x^\star\), and let \(\bar x^\star=\mathbf1-x^\star\) be its coordinatewise complement. Complementation preserves whether the endpoints of each edge differ, so
\(\tilde v(\bar x^\star)=\tilde v(x^\star)=C(G)\).

Allocate mass \(\tfrac12\) to each of \(x^\star\) and \(\bar x^\star\), and zero to every other bundle. These two bundles are distinct because \(m\ge1\). Their masses sum to one, and for every category \(j\), consumption is
\(\displaystyle \frac12x^\star_j+\frac12(1-x^\star_j)=\frac12=q_j.\)

Hence this allocation is feasible and has welfare \(C(G)\). Together with the upper bound, this proves
\(\operatorname{OPT}=C(G)\).

In particular, arbitrary splitting of buyer mass does not increase the optimum beyond the largest cut, and it permits that largest cut value despite the supply constraints.

3. Polynomial reduction and recovery from an allocation. The constructed instance has \(m=|V|\), \(\tau=1\), and \(k=|E|+1\). Each frequency uses \(m\) bits. All numerical inputs are integers or halves, and the constant coefficient requires \(O(\log(|E|+1))\) bits. Thus the total encoding length and construction time are polynomial in the graph input length.

Using threshold \(B\), the proved equality gives
\(\operatorname{OPT}\ge B\) if and only if \(G\) has a cut of size at least \(B\). Therefore even deciding whether the optimum reaches a given threshold is NP-hard.

The requested allocation output also reveals a maximum cut directly. For any optimal allocation,
\(\displaystyle 0=C(G)-\sum_xz_x\tilde v(x)=\sum_xz_x\bigl(C(G)-\tilde v(x)\bigr).\)

Each summand is nonnegative. Thus every bundle with positive mass satisfies \(\tilde v(x)=C(G)\). Since the masses sum to one, at least one such bundle exists. Reading any positive bundle from the returned list therefore yields a maximum cut.

A polynomial-time exact allocation algorithm would consequently solve Max-Cut in polynomial time. This argument does not depend on large output: the constructed instance always admits an optimal allocation with exactly two positive masses, both equal to \(\tfrac12\).

4. Relation to demand-oracle algorithms. For these valuations, a demand query at zero prices asks for a bundle maximizing \(\tilde v(x)\), which is exactly a maximum cut. Thus polynomial-time solvability with a supplied demand oracle does not give a polynomial-time algorithm from these explicit Fourier coefficients.

The target problem is therefore NP-hard; a polynomial-time exact algorithm in \(m,\tau,k,L\) would imply \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).
