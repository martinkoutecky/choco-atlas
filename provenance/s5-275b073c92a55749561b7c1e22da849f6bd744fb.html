The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two alternatives \(a_1,a_2\) with uniform \(\mathcal D\), a hidden society over the two ranking types \(a_1\succ a_2\) and \(a_2\succ a_1\), with masses either \((1/2+2\epsilon,1/2-2\epsilon)\) or the reverse, and oracle access that samples a type, how many preference queries are necessary to return an element of the \(\epsilon\)-PVC with probability at least \(1-\delta\)? Prove an \(\Omega(\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^2)\) lower bound for valid small \(\epsilon\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is specified by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Here this specification is hidden from the algorithm, which receives only the oracle access defined below. Let \(L\) denote the encoding bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L,\epsilon,\delta\).
Let \(M=\{a_1,a_2\}\), so \(m=2\), and let \(\mathcal D\) be the known uniform distribution on \(M\). The two strict ranking types are \(a_1\succ a_2\) and \(a_2\succ a_1\), so \(\tau=2\).
For binary rational parameters \(0<\epsilon\leq 1/8\) and \(0<\delta\leq 1/8\), the hidden mass vector is either \((1/2+2\epsilon,1/2-2\epsilon)\) or its reverse; masses sum to \(1\). Both possibilities are known to the algorithm.
A coalition consists of quantities \(t_r\in[0,p_r]\) of each type \(r\), with total mass \(t=\sum_r t_r>0\). It \(\epsilon\)-blocks \(a\) if a nonempty \(S\subseteq M\) satisfies \(\mu_{\mathcal D}(S)>1-t+\epsilon\), and every type with \(t_r>0\) strictly prefers every member of \(S\) to \(a\). The \(\epsilon\)-PVC consists of the unblocked alternatives.
Each preference query independently samples a type according to the hidden masses and reveals its ranking. The algorithm may randomize and must output one alternative; its success probability must be at least \(1-\delta\) under each hidden society.
Let \(q^*(\epsilon,\delta)\) be the smallest fixed upper bound on the number of queries of any such algorithm.

Does \(q^*(\epsilon,\delta)\) require \(\Omega(\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta))\) queries uniformly over the stated parameter domain, or can a smaller asymptotic query bound suffice?

The finite-voter query model has a stated \(\Omega(\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta))\) discriminative-query lower bound; its displayed two-type construction uses a margin exactly equal to \(\epsilon\), which does not establish blocking under the strict inequality above. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.16751v1#S5.SS1)
A general two-distribution testing lower bound is also established there, but no separate result explicitly addressing this mass-society PVC formulation was verified. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.16751v1#S5.SS1)

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted the support-list convention as specifying the hidden instance, rather than revealing its masses to the algorithm, to preserve the explicitly requested oracle model.
  • Normalized society mass to one and allowed coalitions to include arbitrary portions of types, replacing voter fractions by coalition mass while retaining strict blocking and strict preferences.
  • Restricted epsilon and delta to positive binary rationals at most one eighth, ensuring positive type masses and a nontrivial confidence domain.
  • Defined each preference query as a fresh independent type sample revealing its ranking, and measured complexity by a fixed worst-case query cap rather than expected stopping time.
  • Retained the graded question's margin of twice epsilon; the checked full versions still use the erroneous equality-boundary construction.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

Interpreting success as outputting an alternative in the \(\epsilon\)-PVC, the answer is yes:

\[ q^*(\epsilon,\delta)=\Theta\!\left(\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta)\right) \]

uniformly over the stated parameter domain. The constants are absolute. Below, all logarithms except \(\log_2\) are natural.

1. The strict blocking condition forces identification

Write \(p_i\) for the mass of the type that ranks \(a_i\) first.

If a coalition blocks \(a_2\), its nonempty blocking set must be \(\{a_1\}\): strict preference excludes \(a_2\) itself. Every participating type must therefore rank \(a_1\) first, so its total mass satisfies \(t\leq p_1\). The blocking inequality is precisely

\[ \frac12>1-t+\epsilon \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad t>\frac12+\epsilon. \]

Conversely, if \(p_1>\frac12+\epsilon\), the coalition containing the entire first type blocks \(a_2\). Consequently,

\[ a_2\text{ is }\epsilon\text{-blocked} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p_1>\frac12+\epsilon. \]

The symmetric statement holds for \(a_1\).

Denote the society with \(p_1=\frac12+2\epsilon\) by \(H_+\), and its reverse by \(H_-\). The preceding equivalence gives

\[ C_\epsilon(H_+)=\{a_1\}, \qquad C_\epsilon(H_-)=\{a_2\}, \]

where \(C_\epsilon\) denotes the \(\epsilon\)-PVC. Thus successful selection requires distinguishing the two societies.

This also identifies the equality issue in the construction displayed in [Theorem 5.2 of the cited paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.16751v1#S5.SS1): with majority mass exactly \(\frac12+\epsilon\), the strict blocking condition fails, and both alternatives are unblocked. The present \(2\epsilon\) margin avoids that issue.

2. A lower bound covering randomized and early-stopping algorithms

Encode each oracle response by \(X=1\) when the sampled type ranks \(a_1\) first, and \(X=0\) otherwise. Its distributions under the two societies are

\[ P=\operatorname{Bernoulli}\!\left(\frac12+2\epsilon\right), \qquad Q=\operatorname{Bernoulli}\!\left(\frac12-2\epsilon\right). \]

Consider any algorithm with a fixed upper bound of \(q\) queries. Generate \(q\) independent responses in advance; an algorithm that stops earlier simply ignores the unused responses. Its randomized output is therefore described by a common conditional distribution \(K(z\mid x)\), where \(x\in\{0,1\}^q\) and \(z\in\{a_1,a_2\}\).

For distributions \(A,B\) on a finite set, define their relative entropy by

\[ D(A\Vert B)=\sum_y A(y)\log\frac{A(y)}{B(y)}. \]

The joint distributions of responses and output are \(P^{\otimes q}(x)K(z\mid x)\) and \(Q^{\otimes q}(x)K(z\mid x)\). Cancelling the common conditional probabilities in the likelihood ratio gives joint relative entropy \(qD(P\Vert Q)\).

Let \(u\) and \(v\) be the probabilities of outputting \(a_1\) under \(H_+\) and \(H_-\), respectively. Applying the log-sum inequality separately to each output value yields

\[ qD(P\Vert Q) \geq u\log\frac uv+(1-u)\log\frac{1-u}{1-v}. \]

For completeness, the log-sum inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality for the convex function \(s\mapsto s\log s\), using weights proportional to the denominators. Thus this step also applies to randomized output rules.

Success in both societies requires \(u\geq1-\delta\) and \(v\leq\delta\). Writing \(h(u)=-u\log u-(1-u)\log(1-u)\), the last expression equals

\[ u\log(1/v)+(1-u)\log(1/(1-v))-h(u). \]

Since the second term is nonnegative and \(h(u)\leq\log2\), it is at least \((1-\delta)\log(1/\delta)-\log2\). Because \(\delta\leq1/8\),

\[ qD(P\Vert Q) \geq \left(\frac78-\frac13\right)\log(1/\delta) \geq \frac12\log(1/\delta). \]

It remains to bound the information in one response. Set \(x=4\epsilon\), so \(0<x\leq1/2\). Direct calculation gives

\[ D(P\Vert Q)=x\log\frac{1+x}{1-x}. \]

Furthermore,

\[ \log\frac{1+x}{1-x} =2\int_0^x\frac{ds}{1-s^2} \leq\frac{2x}{1-x^2} \leq\frac83x. \]

Therefore \(D(P\Vert Q)\leq\frac{128}{3}\epsilon^2\), and every admissible algorithm satisfies

\[ q\geq\frac{3}{256}\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta). \]

This bound holds throughout the entire stated domain, including its endpoints.

3. A matching upper bound

Take \(n\) independent samples and output the alternative ranked first by a sample majority, breaking ties arbitrarily.

Under either society, let \(Y\) count samples ranking the uniquely correct alternative first. Then \(Y\) is binomial with parameters \(n\) and \(p=\frac12+2\epsilon\). Failure implies \(Y\leq n/2\).

Set \(\lambda=\log(p/(1-p))>0\). Markov’s inequality and independence give

\[ \Pr(Y\leq n/2) \leq \mathbb E\!\left[e^{-\lambda(Y-n/2)}\right] = \left(pe^{-\lambda/2}+(1-p)e^{\lambda/2}\right)^n. \]

The expression in parentheses equals \(2\sqrt{p(1-p)}=\sqrt{1-16\epsilon^2}\). Using \(\log(1-z)\leq-z\) for \(0\leq z<1\), we obtain

\[ \Pr(\text{failure})\leq e^{-8n\epsilon^2}. \]

An exactly computable sample schedule for binary rational inputs is

\[ b=\left\lceil\log_2(1/\delta)\right\rceil, \qquad N=\left\lceil\frac{b}{8\epsilon^2}\right\rceil. \]

Since \(b\geq\log(1/\delta)\), using \(N\) samples achieves failure probability at most \(\delta\). Moreover, \(N=O(\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta))\) uniformly for \(\delta\leq1/8\). Together with the lower bound, this proves the claimed order.

4. Running time and encoding length

Here \(m=\tau=2\). After computing \(N\), sample majority takes \(O(N)\) word operations, or conservatively \(O(N\log(N+1))\) bit operations with binary counters. The hidden society’s encoding length \(L\) does not affect this query-processing time.

Parameter preprocessing uses exact integer arithmetic. If \(L\) includes the known parameter encodings, a conservative total bit-time bound is

\[ O\!\left(L^2+ \epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta) \log\!\left(2+\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta)\right)\right). \]

If \(L\) counts only the hidden support specification, replace the preprocessing term \(L^2\) by \((L+\ell_\epsilon+\ell_\delta)^2\), where \(\ell_\epsilon,\ell_\delta\) are the actual parameter encoding lengths. Those lengths cannot generally be bounded by the parameters’ magnitudes alone.

Combining this with the preceding testing bound yields exactly
