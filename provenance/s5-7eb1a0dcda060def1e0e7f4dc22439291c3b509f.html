The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), a deterministic scoring rule with tie-breaking, a finite set \(U\) of voter preferences over outcomes, and an initial rational distribution \(\mu_0\) over types \((u,\pi)\in U\times\mathfrak{L}(C)\), decide whether there is a finite sequence from \(\mu_0\) to a sink. Each step transfers a nonzero amount of mass between ballots while preserving each preference type, moves no more mass from a source type than it contains, and requires every voter preference type with positive moved mass to strictly prefer the new winning outcome to the old one. A sink is a distribution admitting no such transfer; determine the decision problem’s complexity in the input parameters, including the support size \(\tau\) and encoding length \(L\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) have \(m\geq 1\) candidates, let \(1\leq k\leq m\), and let \(O=\binom{C}{k}\). Ballots are strict rankings \(\mathfrak L(C)\); each member \(u\) of a finite nonempty set \(U\) is an explicitly listed strict preference ordering \(\succ_u\) over \(O\).
The input scoring rule is a positional scoring vector \(a_1\geq\cdots\geq a_m\geq0\) of binary rationals and a fixed candidate priority order. At distribution \(\mu\), candidate \(c\) scores \(\sum_{u,\pi}\mu(u,\pi)a_{\operatorname{rank}_\pi(c)}\); the outcome \(W(\mu)\) consists of the top \(k\) candidates, resolving equal scores by priority.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types \((u,\pi)\) with positive initial mass, each mass a binary rational. Masses sum to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
States are nonnegative real distributions on \(U\times\mathfrak L(C)\) preserving each preference type's initial total mass; all ballots remain available, including those absent initially.
A step chooses nonnegative real transfers \(f_{u,\pi,\rho}\), with \(f_{u,\pi,\pi}=0\), positive total transfer, and \(\sum_\rho f_{u,\pi,\rho}\leq\mu(u,\pi)\). It produces \(\mu'(u,\pi)=\mu(u,\pi)-\sum_\rho f_{u,\pi,\rho}+\sum_\rho f_{u,\rho,\pi}\), and requires \(W(\mu')\succ_u W(\mu)\) for every \(u\) with positive outgoing transfer.
A sink admits no such step. Sink reachability asks whether a finite sequence of steps from the input \(\mu_0\) ends at a sink; a sequence of length zero is allowed.

Is this continuous sink-reachability problem in \(\mathrm P\), or is it NP-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,L,k\), and \(|U|\).

For finite societies, fixed-formula monadic least-fixed-point logic with counting has polynomial data complexity on an explicitly represented improvement graph, yielding polynomial-time sink reachability in that graph's size; the graph can be exponential in the society's input size. [Das–Ramanujam–Simon](https://www.cse.iitk.ac.in/users/simon/papers/mlfpc.pdf)
No result settling this exact continuous sink-reachability problem was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restored the committee-size parameter k and outcome domain of k-element candidate subsets from the discrete voting model.
  • Interpreted the unspecified scoring rule as positional scoring selecting the top k candidates, with a nonnegative nonincreasing binary-rational score vector and fixed candidate priority order supplied as input.
  • Specified preference types as explicitly listed strict total orders over committees, making their representation part of L.
  • Normalized total mass to one and allowed arbitrary nonnegative real intermediate masses and transfers.
  • Made steps simultaneous collections of transfers across any number of preference types and ballots, with aggregate outgoing mass bounded by each source's current mass.
  • Allowed every ranking as a destination even if absent from the initial support, and allowed a zero-step path when the initial state is already a sink.

Result

The problem is NP-hard, already for
$$ k=2,\qquad a=(1,0,\ldots,0). $$

The reduction below has the stronger property that every positive instance reaches a sink in one step, whereas every negative instance has no sink anywhere. Thus the hardness does not depend on long improvement paths.

1. Redistribution and plurality scores

For plurality, only the first-ranked candidate matters. Write
$$ v_{u,c}=\sum_{\pi:\operatorname{rank}_\pi(c)=1}\mu(u,\pi). $$
Then $v_{u,c}\geq0$, $\sum_c v_{u,c}=p_u$, where $p_u$ is type $u$’s preserved mass, and candidate $c$ has score $\sum_u v_{u,c}$.

We first justify arbitrary redistribution by improving types.

Redistribution lemma. Suppose $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are ballot distributions for one preference type, with equal total mass. Define
$$ d_\pi=\max(\alpha_\pi-\beta_\pi,0),\qquad e_\pi=\max(\beta_\pi-\alpha_\pi,0). $$
Their totals are equal; denote the common total by $D$. If $D>0$, set
$$ f_{\pi,\rho}=\frac{d_\pi e_\rho}{D}. $$
The supports of $d$ and $e$ are disjoint, so $f_{\pi,\pi}=0$. The outgoing and incoming totals are respectively $d_\pi$ and $e_\pi$. Consequently, these transfers respect the outgoing-capacity constraints and produce $\beta$. If $D=0$, the distributions already coincide.

It follows that, when changing outcome from $X$ to $Y$, every type preferring $Y$ to $X$ may be redistributed arbitrarily. Every other type must remain fixed: without outgoing transfers, that type also has no incoming transfers, since transfers preserve the preference type.

In particular, an improving coalition of total mass $p>0$ may produce any aggregate first-place vector of total mass $p$. To realize a prescribed vector $z$, assign type $u$ in that coalition the vector $(p_u/p)z$, and choose ballots realizing its first-place masses. The lemma implements these replacements. If the outcome changes, at least one replacement is nonzero, ensuring positive total transfer.

2. Two elementary attack tests

Fix two candidates $x_1,x_2$, having the highest and second-highest priorities, and put
$$ X=\{x_1,x_2\}. $$

Suppose a state has first-place votes only for $x_1,x_2$. For a proposed improving coalition of mass $p$, let $b_1,b_2$ be the scores contributed by the types outside the coalition. Thus
$$ b_1+b_2=1-p. $$

Disjoint target

Let $Y=\{y,z\}$ be disjoint from $X$. Both target candidates have lower priority than $x_1,x_2$.

The coalition can produce outcome $Y$ exactly when
$$ p>2\max(b_1,b_2). $$

Indeed, both target candidates must strictly exceed $\max(b_1,b_2)$, so their combined coalition score must exceed twice that quantity. Conversely, if the displayed inequality holds, assigning coalition mass $p/2$ to each target candidate elects exactly $Y$.

Target retaining one selected candidate

For $Y=\{x_2,y\}$, the excluded candidate $x_1$ has highest priority. Consequently, both target scores must strictly exceed $b_1$. Coalition votes for excluded candidates can be reassigned to target candidates, so feasibility is equivalent to
$$ p>b_1+\max(0,b_1-b_2). $$
To check sufficiency, if $b_2>b_1$, assigning all coalition mass to $y$ works whenever $p>b_1$. If $b_2\leq b_1$, allocate slightly more than $b_1-b_2$ to $x_2$ and slightly more than $b_1$ to $y$. Necessity follows from these same score requirements.

For $Y=\{x_1,y\}$, $x_1$ may tie the excluded $x_2$, whereas $y$ must strictly exceed it. The corresponding condition is
$$ p>b_2+\max(0,b_2-b_1). $$

Thus, writing $b$ for the fixed score of the excluded member of $X$, either partial target is blocked exactly when
$$ b\geq\min\left(p,\frac13\right). $$

Here is the calculation. Set $s=1-p$ and define
$$ F(b)=b+\max(0,2b-s). $$
The attack is blocked exactly when $F(b)\geq p$. The function $F$ is strictly increasing. If $p\leq1/3$, then $p\leq s/2$ and $F(p)=p$, giving threshold $b=p$. If $p\geq1/3$, then $s/2\leq1/3$ and
$$ F(1/3)=1-s=p, $$
giving threshold $b=1/3$.

These tests account for priority ties exactly.

3. Reduction from satisfiability

We reduce from the classical NP-complete problem of satisfiability for conjunctive formulas with at most three literals per clause. [Schaefer’s satisfiability paper](https://doi.org/10.1145/800133.804350) records this NP-completeness result.

Let the formula have variables $z_1,\ldots,z_n$ and $q$ clauses. Add unused variables if necessary so that $n\geq3$. Delete tautological clauses and repeated occurrences of a literal within a clause. Each remaining clause therefore has between one and three distinct literals. These operations preserve satisfiability.

Define
$$ w=\frac1{6n},\qquad \alpha=\frac{1-3w}{4},\qquad \gamma=\frac13-w. $$
The following inequalities and identities will be used:
$$ 0<\alpha<\gamma<\frac13,\qquad \gamma>2w, $$
$$ 2\gamma+2w=\frac23, $$
$$ 2(\alpha+w)=1-(2\alpha+w). $$

Mass groups

Create six anchor groups and two literal groups per variable:

| Group | Mass |
|---|---:|
| $A_1,B_1$ | $\alpha$ each |
| $A_2,B_2$ | $\gamma-\alpha$ each |
| $A_3,B_3$ | $w$ each |
| $P_i,N_i$, for $1\leq i\leq n$ | $w$ each |

Use the notation
$$ A_\alpha=\{A_1\},\qquad A_\gamma=\{A_1,A_2\},\qquad A=\{A_1,A_2,A_3\}, $$
and similarly for $B$.

Each full anchor group has mass $1/3$. The literal groups have combined mass $2nw=1/3$, so all masses sum to $1$.

The groups will shortly be split into explicitly listed preference types. Group notation always means the union of those types.

Tests and their fixed groups

Each test specifies a collection $S$ of groups that will prefer $X$ to its target. All groups outside $S$ will prefer the target to $X$, so they form precisely the improving coalition.

Each test has four target committees, called its copies.

| Test | Fixed groups $S$ | Target shape |
|---|---|---|
| Anchor $A$ | $A$ | $\{x_2,y\}$ |
| Anchor $B$ | $B$ | $\{x_1,y\}$ |
| Pair $i$, first test | $A_\gamma\cup B_\gamma\cup\{P_i,N_i\}$ | $\{x_1,y\}$ |
| Pair $i$, second test | $A_\gamma\cup B_\gamma\cup\{P_i,N_i\}$ | $\{x_2,y\}$ |
| Boolean test $i$ | $A_\alpha\cup B_\alpha\cup\{P_i\}$ | $\{y,z\}$, disjoint from $X$ |
| Clause test | $A_\gamma$ together with its literal groups | $\{x_2,y\}$ |

The group representing literal $z_i$ is $P_i$; the group representing $\neg z_i$ is $N_i$.

Except for the following specified sharing, use fresh outsider candidates for every target copy. For copy zero of the two anchor tests, use respectively
$$ \{x_2,h\},\qquad \{x_1,h\}, $$
with the same outsider $h$. Give candidates priority
$$ x_1\triangleright x_2\triangleright h, $$
followed by all other candidates in any fixed order.

All target committees are distinct. Add two fresh candidates $s_1,s_2$ and designate
$$ S_0=\{s_1,s_2\} $$
as the initial outcome.

There are $3n+q+2$ tests.

Strict preference lists

Replace each mass group $g$ by four preference types
$$ (g,0),(g,1),(g,2),(g,3), $$
each with one quarter of the group’s mass.

For a test $t$, denote its four target copies by $D_t^0,\ldots,D_t^3$. Type $(g,j)$ orders them as
$$ D_t^j\succ D_t^{j+1}\succ D_t^{j+2}\succ D_t^{j+3}, $$
with superscripts taken modulo $4$.

Construct its complete strict preference list as follows:

  • List the test blocks for which $g\notin S$, in a fixed order of tests, using the cyclic order just specified within each block.
  • List $X$.
  • List the test blocks for which $g\in S$, again using the specified internal orders.
  • List all remaining committees in any fixed order, with $S_0$ last.

Every committee appears exactly once. In particular:

  • the improving coalition from $X$ to any copy of test $t$ consists exactly of groups outside that test’s $S$;
  • every type prefers $X$ to every undesignated committee;
  • every type prefers $X$ to $S_0$.

The preference types are distinct. Different groups have different memberships in the test sets $S$: anchor memberships distinguish the $A$ and $B$ groups, Boolean and pair tests distinguish their pieces and the literal groups. Within a group, the four cyclic orders differ.

Initial support

Choose two strict ballots, one placing $s_1$ first and one placing $s_2$ first. For every preference type, place half its mass on each ballot.

The initial scores are $1/2$ for each of $s_1,s_2$, and zero for every other candidate. Thus
$$ W(\mu_0)=S_0. $$

This completes the explicitly represented instance.

4. Every sink must elect $X$

First consider any undesignated committee $Z\neq X$. Every type prefers $X$ to $Z$. Redistributing the entire society with half the first-place mass on each of $x_1,x_2$ therefore gives an admissible step from $Z$ to $X$. No state electing $Z$ is a sink.

Next consider a designated committee $D_t^j$. Among the four clones of every group, exactly three prefer $D_t^{j-1}$ to $D_t^j$. Hence the total mass preferring this improvement is exactly $3/4$.

That coalition can give first-place mass $3/8$ to each member of $D_t^{j-1}$. Every candidate outside the target receives only unchanged mass, at most $1/4$. Thus the target is elected, independently of the original ballot state.

Consequently, no designated committee can be the outcome of a sink either.

We have proved:
$$ \text{Every sink in the constructed instance has outcome }X. $$

5. Any sink determines a satisfying assignment

Assume a sink exists. Its outcome is $X$ by the preceding section. We now derive its voting structure without assuming that ballots avoid outsider candidates.

Anchor tests force the anchor votes

For anchor test $A$, the fixed types are exactly group $A$, of mass $1/3$, and the target copy zero is $\{x_2,h\}$. Its improving coalition has mass $2/3$.

If every fixed candidate score is strictly less than $1/3$, the coalition can assign $1/3$ to each target member, electing the target.

Otherwise, some candidate $d$ receives the entire fixed mass $1/3$. If $d$ belongs to the target, the same coalition allocation elects the target. If $d$ is outside the target and $d\neq x_1$, both target candidates have higher priority than $d$, so they win the three-way score tie at $1/3$.

Thus blocking this attack requires that all group-$A$ first-place mass be on $x_1$.

Applying the same argument to anchor test $B$, whose copy zero is $\{x_1,h\}$, forces all group-$B$ first-place mass onto $x_2$: the only excluded candidate having higher priority than a target member is $x_2$.

Therefore, at every sink,
$$ A\text{ votes entirely for }x_1,\qquad B\text{ votes entirely for }x_2. $$

Pair tests eliminate votes for outsiders

Fix variable $i$. The fixed groups in both pair tests have total mass
$$ 2\gamma+2w=\frac23. $$
Their selected-candidate scores $b_1,b_2$ satisfy $b_1,b_2\geq\gamma$. Every fixed outsider score is at most $2w$, and
$$ 2w<\gamma. $$
The improving coalition has mass $1/3$.

If $b_1\geq b_2$ and $b_2<1/3$, assign all coalition mass to the outsider $y$ in the target $\{x_1,y\}$. Candidate $y$ then scores at least $1/3>b_2$. Candidate $x_1$ outranks $x_2$, including when their scores tie. Every other outsider has score below $\gamma\leq b_2$. Hence the target wins, contradicting sinkhood.

If $b_2>b_1$ and $b_1<1/3$, assigning all coalition mass to the outsider in target $\{x_2,y\}$ similarly elects that target.

Thus sinkhood forces
$$ \min(b_1,b_2)\geq\frac13. $$
Since the entire fixed mass is $2/3$, necessarily
$$ b_1=b_2=\frac13, $$
and no fixed mass votes for outsiders.

The anchor prefixes contribute $\gamma$ to each selected candidate. Consequently, groups $P_i,N_i$ together contribute exactly $w$ to each of $x_1,x_2$, and nothing elsewhere.

Doing this for every variable proves that the entire society votes only for $x_1,x_2$.

Boolean tests force binary choices

Let $z$ be group $P_i$’s first-place mass on $x_1$. The preceding result gives $0\leq z\leq w$, with its remaining mass $w-z$ on $x_2$.

For Boolean test $i$, the fixed scores are
$$ b_1=\alpha+z,\qquad b_2=\alpha+w-z. $$
The improving coalition has mass
$$ p=1-(2\alpha+w)=2(\alpha+w). $$
Its target is disjoint from $X$. By the disjoint-target test, blocking the attack requires
$$ \max(\alpha+z,\alpha+w-z)\geq\alpha+w. $$
Equivalently,
$$ \max(z,w-z)\geq w. $$
Together with $0\leq z\leq w$, this forces
$$ z\in\{0,w\}. $$

The pair constraint then forces $N_i$ entirely onto the opposite selected candidate.

Define the truth assignment by
$$ z_i\text{ is true exactly when }P_i\text{ votes for }x_1. $$
Under this assignment, a literal’s group votes for $x_1$ exactly when that literal is true.

Clause tests require a true literal

Consider a clause containing $d$ distinct literals, where $1\leq d\leq3$. Let $t$ of those literals be true.

The fixed groups in its test give scores
$$ b_1=\gamma+tw,\qquad b_2=(d-t)w, $$
and the improving coalition has mass
$$ p=1-\gamma-dw=\frac23-(d-1)w>\frac13. $$
The target retains $x_2$ and excludes $x_1$. By the partial-target test, blocking it requires
$$ b_1\geq\frac13. $$
Substituting $\gamma=1/3-w$ gives
$$ \frac13-w+tw\geq\frac13, $$
so $t\geq1$.

Every clause therefore has a true literal. We have proved that existence of any sink implies satisfiability.

6. A satisfying assignment produces a reachable sink

Conversely, suppose the formula is satisfiable. Construct a state as follows:

  • All $A$ groups vote for $x_1$.
  • All $B$ groups vote for $x_2$.
  • If $z_i$ is true, $P_i$ votes for $x_1$ and $N_i$ for $x_2$.
  • If $z_i$ is false, reverse those two literal-group votes.

Each selected candidate scores
$$ \frac13+nw=\frac12, $$
and every outsider scores zero. Thus the outcome is $X$.

We verify that every possible improving target is blocked.

For either anchor test, the fixed score of the excluded selected candidate is $1/3$, while the coalition mass is $2/3$. The partial-target threshold is $1/3$, so all copies are blocked.

For either pair test, fixed scores are $b_1=b_2=1/3$ and coalition mass is $p=1/3$. Again the partial-target threshold blocks all copies.

For a Boolean test, its fixed scores are $\alpha+w$ and $\alpha$, in some order. Its coalition mass is $2(\alpha+w)$. A disjoint target requires the strict inequality
$$ p>2\max(b_1,b_2), $$
which fails at equality.

For a clause test, at least one literal is true, so
$$ b_1=\gamma+tw\geq\gamma+w=\frac13. $$
Its coalition mass exceeds $1/3$, and the partial-target threshold therefore blocks all copies.

All undesignated committees are below $X$ in every type’s preferences, so none can be the target of an admissible step. The constructed state is a sink.

Finally, every preference type ranks $X$ above the initial outcome $S_0$. The redistribution lemma therefore permits a single admissible step from $\mu_0$ to this sink. Its total transfer is positive because the outcome changes.

Hence
$$ \varphi\text{ is satisfiable} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mu_0\text{ can reach a sink}. $$

7. Size, running time, and classification

For a formula with $n\geq3$ variables and $q$ remaining clauses, the construction has
$$ k=2,\qquad m=16n+4q+11, $$
$$ |U|=4(2n+6)=8n+24,\qquad \tau=2|U|=16n+48. $$
The candidate count follows from seven anchor-test outsiders, $8n$ pair-test outsiders, $8n$ Boolean-test outsiders, $4q$ clause-test outsiders, and the four candidates $x_1,x_2,s_1,s_2$.

Every mass has binary encoding length $O(\log(n+1))$. All ballots and all strict preference lists are explicitly written. Since $k=2$,
$$ |O|=\binom m2. $$
Using candidate indices, the construction can be generated in
$$ O\!\left((|U|m^2+\tau m)\log(m+1)\right) $$
bit operations, and its total bit length satisfies
$$ L= O\!\left((n+1)(n+q+1)^2\log(n+q+2)\right). $$
Thus this is a polynomial-time many-one reduction, with fixed $k=2$, $\tau=O(m)$, and $|U|=O(m)$.

The unrestricted continuous sink-reachability problem is therefore NP-hard, and cannot have an algorithm polynomial in $L$ unless $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$. The proof establishes hardness; it does not assert membership in $\mathrm{NP}$ or NP-completeness.
