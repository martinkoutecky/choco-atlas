1. Admissibility is characterized by triples

For a triple $T=\{a,b,c\}$, write $abc$ for the ranking $a\succ b\succ c$, and define
$$ H_T=\{abc,bca,cab\}, \qquad K_T=\{acb,cba,bac\}. $$
For a domain $D$, let
$$ D|_T=\{v|_T:v\in D\}. $$

Proposition 1. A nonempty domain $D$ is admissible if and only if every triple $T$ satisfies
$$ H_T\not\subseteq D|_T \qquad\text{and}\qquad K_T\not\subseteq D|_T. $$
When these conditions hold, strict majority is acyclic even for arbitrary real probability masses.

Proof. Suppose first that $D|_T$ contains $abc,bca,cab$. Choose rankings realizing these three restrictions and give them masses $3/8,3/8,1/4$, respectively. The rankings are distinct because their restrictions differ. Each comparison in
$$ a\succ_\mu b\succ_\mu c\succ_\mu a $$
is opposed by exactly one ranking, whose mass is below $1/2$. Thus all three edges occur. These masses have terminating binary expansions. The family $K_T$ similarly produces the reverse cycle.

For the converse, first exclude majority triangles. Consider a prospective triangle
$$ a\succ_\mu b,\qquad b\succ_\mu c,\qquad c\succ_\mu a. $$
The intersection of the events $a\succ_v b$ and $b\succ_v c$ consists exactly of rankings restricting to $abc$. If $abc$ is absent, these events are disjoint, so their masses sum to at most $1$. They cannot both have mass greater than $1/2$.

Likewise, absence of $bca$ prevents the second and third edges from occurring together, and absence of $cab$ prevents the third and first. Since at least one member of $H_T$ is absent, the displayed triangle is impossible. The condition concerning $K_T$ excludes the reverse triangle. This reasoning applies to arbitrary real probability masses.

It remains to exclude longer cycles when pairwise ties occur. Suppose a society nevertheless has a directed cycle, and define its signed margins by
$$ M_{ab}=\sum_v\mu(v) \bigl(\mathbf 1[a\succ_v b]-\mathbf 1[b\succ_v a]\bigr). $$
Let $\delta$ be the smallest nonzero absolute margin. It exists and is positive because the cycle has strict edges. Fix $w\in D$ and choose a dyadic rational satisfying
$$ 0<\varepsilon<\delta/2. $$
Replace $\mu$ by
$$ \mu_\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)\mu+\varepsilon\delta_w. $$
The new margins satisfy
$$ M^\varepsilon_{ab} =(1-\varepsilon)M_{ab} +\varepsilon \bigl(\mathbf 1[a\succ_w b]-\mathbf 1[b\succ_w a]\bigr). $$
Hence
$$ |M^\varepsilon_{ab}-M_{ab}|\leq 2\varepsilon<\delta. $$
Every previously strict comparison retains its direction. Every previously tied comparison acquires margin $\varepsilon$ or $-\varepsilon$. Thus the new majority relation is a tournament containing the original cycle.

Every cyclic tournament contains a directed triangle. Indeed, take a shortest directed cycle. If it has more than three vertices, the edge between its first and third vertices either closes a triangle or shortcuts the cycle, contradicting minimality. This contradicts the preceding exclusion of majority triangles.

The argument works for real masses. When the original masses are rational, the perturbed masses remain rational; when they are dyadic, they remain dyadic. $\square$

Consequences. Every inadmissible domain has a witness with support size three and constant-size dyadic masses. Moreover, the domains here are exactly the usual Condorcet domains defined using odd finite electorates: a forbidden family gives a three-voter cycle, whereas its absence excludes cycles for every electorate. Maximality and equivalence therefore coincide with the discrete notions. The supplied generation paper uses precisely odd-electorate admissibility, inclusion-maximality, and equivalence under relabeling. [Definitions](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.20865v1#S2).

3. Normalization and polynomial output length

Put
$$ t=\binom m3, \qquad \alpha=1\succ2\succ\cdots\succ m. $$
Call a domain normalized if it contains $\alpha$.

Every nonempty domain is equivalent to a normalized one: if a ranking lists alternatives as $v_1,\ldots,v_m$, relabel $v_i$ as $i$. Relabeling preserves admissibility and maximality.

For each triple, write its alternatives as $a<b<c$. Its restriction in $\alpha$ is $abc\in H_T$. A normalized admissible domain must therefore omit:

  • at least one of the two restrictions $bca,cab$;
  • at least one of the three restrictions in $K_T$.

There are six ways to choose such a pair of omissions.

Let $s$ assign one such pair to every triple, and define
$$ E_s=\{v\in\mathcal L(A): v|_T\text{ avoids both omissions selected by }s \text{ for every }T\}. $$
The ranking $\alpha$ belongs to $E_s$, so this domain is nonempty. Its restrictions omit a member of each cyclic family, so Proposition 1 makes it admissible.

If $D$ is normalized and maximal, choose the omissions from restrictions absent in $D$. Then
$$ D\subseteq E_s. $$
Maximality forces equality. Thus every normalized maximal domain occurs among the $6^t$ descriptions, and every equivalence class has a normalized representative. Consequently,
$$ 1\leq C(m)\leq 6^{\binom m3}. $$
The lower bound follows because singleton domains are admissible and the finite ambient set permits a maximal extension.

In particular, the binary output length is at most
$$ 1+\left\lfloor\binom m3\log_2 6\right\rfloor=O(m^3). $$

Here the external input is the stated recurrence and inequality from [Liversidge, Theorem 5.9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.00751). Thus the binary output length is $\Omega(m^2)$ and $O(m^3)$.

4. A symmetry restriction and a reduced Burnside formula

For a domain $D$, define its relabeling automorphism group by
$$ \operatorname{Aut}(D)= \{\pi\in S_m:\pi D=D\}. $$

Proposition 2. For every nonempty admissible domain $D$, the order of $\operatorname{Aut}(D)$ is a power of two.

Proof. First observe that a permutation fixing a strict ranking must be the identity: it must fix the alternative occupying each position.

Suppose $g\in\operatorname{Aut}(D)$ has odd order $r>1$. Choose $w\in D$. Its orbit
$$ w,gw,\ldots,g^{r-1}w $$
has exactly $r$ distinct rankings. Otherwise some nonidentity power of $g$ would fix $w$.

Consider the electorate consisting of these $r$ rankings, one voter per ranking. Since $r$ is odd, no pairwise comparison ties. Proposition 1 makes its majority tournament acyclic, so it is a strict linear order.

Relabeling by $g$ permutes this electorate. It consequently preserves its majority order. But the only permutation preserving a strict linear order is the identity, contradicting $r>1$.

Thus $\operatorname{Aut}(D)$ has no nonidentity element of odd order. Cauchy’s theorem states that, for a finite group, every prime divisor of its order is the order of some group element. Applied to the finite subgroup $\operatorname{Aut}(D)\subseteq S_m$, it excludes every odd prime divisor. Its order is therefore a power of two. $\square$

The action of $\operatorname{Aut}(D)$ on $D$ is free, because fixing a ranking forces a permutation to be the identity. Hence
$$ |\operatorname{Aut}(D)|\mid |D|. $$
Also, by Lagrange’s theorem,
$$ |\operatorname{Aut}(D)|\mid m!, \qquad |\operatorname{Aut}(D)|\leq 2^{v_2(m!)}\leq 2^{m-1}, $$
where
$$ v_2(m!)=\sum_{j\geq1}\left\lfloor\frac m{2^j}\right\rfloor. $$
The final inequality follows because the displayed integer sum is strictly less than $\sum_{j\geq1}m/2^j=m$.

Let $\mathcal M_m$ be the set of all labelled maximal admissible domains, and write
$$ B(m)=|\mathcal M_m|. $$
For $\pi\in S_m$, let
$$ F(\pi)=|\{D\in\mathcal M_m:\pi D=D\}|. $$

Counting pairs $(D,\pi)$ with $\pi D=D$ gives Burnside’s formula
$$ C(m)=\frac1{m!}\sum_{\pi\in S_m}F(\pi). $$
To justify it directly, each orbit contributes
$$ |\text{orbit}|\, |\operatorname{Aut}(D)|=m! $$
to the sum, by the orbit-stabilizer theorem.

Proposition 2 shows that
$$ F(\pi)=0 \quad\text{whenever the order of }\pi \text{ is not a power of two}. $$
Indeed, any such permutation fixing a domain would belong to a group whose order is a power of two, which Lagrange’s theorem forbids.

A permutation has order a power of two exactly when all its cycle lengths are powers of two. Write such a cycle type as
$$ \lambda=(1^{a_0}2^{a_1}4^{a_2}\cdots), \qquad \sum_{j\geq0}2^j a_j=m. $$
Choose a permutation $\pi_\lambda$ of this type, and put
$$ F_\lambda=F(\pi_\lambda), \qquad z_\lambda=\prod_{j\geq0}(2^j)^{a_j}a_j!. $$
Conjugate permutations have equal fixed-domain counts: relabeling by a conjugating permutation gives a bijection between their fixed domains. The number of permutations of type $\lambda$ is $m!/z_\lambda$, because cyclic rotations and permutations of equal-length cycles account for the denominator. Therefore
$$ \boxed{ C(m)= \sum_{\substack{a_0,a_1,\ldots\geq0\\ \sum_j2^j a_j=m}} \frac{F_\lambda}{z_\lambda}. } $$

The number of terms is at most
$$ \prod_{j=0}^{\lfloor\log_2m\rfloor} \left(\left\lfloor\frac m{2^j}\right\rfloor+1\right) \leq (m+1)^{1+\lfloor\log_2m\rfloor} = 2^{O((\log m)^2)}. $$

This reduces the symmetry sum to a quasipolynomial number of cycle types. It does not give an efficient method for computing $F_\lambda$; the identity term is already the total labelled count $B(m)$.

There is also an arithmetic consequence. Every orbit has size
$$ \frac{m!}{2^r} $$
for some $r\leq v_2(m!)$. Consequently,
$$ \frac{m!}{2^{v_2(m!)}}\mid B(m), $$
and
$$ \frac{B(m)}{m!} \leq C(m) \leq \frac{2^{v_2(m!)}B(m)}{m!}. $$

$$ D=D(R(D)). $$

$$ s=c(R). $$

The containment condition is checked by testing whether
$$ \pi^{-1}(\alpha)\in E_s. $$

$$ E_{c(R(D))}=D, $$
and the test fixes that description uniquely. By Lemma 3, distinct maximal domains have distinct actual restriction tuples. Every equivalence class has a nonempty finite set of normalized representatives, whose restriction tuples have a unique lexicographic minimum. Each candidate requires
$$ m!\operatorname{poly}(m) $$

$$ 6^{\binom m3}m!\operatorname{poly}(m) = 2^{O(m^3)}. $$
Sequential scans retain only the current description, restriction tuple, ranking, relabeling permutation, and counter. Each has polynomial length, including the counter by Section 3. The algorithm therefore uses polynomial space.

These nine assignments give nine distinct omitted pairs. Relabeling changes the alternative but preserves its position, yielding three classes.

6. A counting-class upper bound

The normalized descriptions also prove
$$ C\in\#\mathrm P^{\mathrm{NP}}, $$
where this class counts accepting paths of nondeterministic polynomial-time machines with an NP oracle.

The machine guesses one valid six-choice description per triple, using three bits per choice and rejecting the two unused encodings. Thus each description has exactly one guessing path.

For every triple and each of its six restrictions, an NP query asks whether a ranking in $E_s$ realizes that restriction. A ranking is a witness of length $O(m\log m)$, and membership in $E_s$ is checked in polynomial time. These queries reconstruct the actual tuple $R(E_s)$.

The machine then applies the tests in Section 5. The duplicate-description test is polynomial-time. The two remaining rejection conditions are NP queries:

  • there exists an outside ranking that can be added admissibly;
  • there exists a relabeling giving a normalized representative with smaller restriction tuple.

Their witnesses are a ranking and a permutation, respectively, and both are verified in polynomial time.

Oracle processing is deterministic after the description is guessed; witnesses supplied to the oracle do not multiply the counted paths. The number of accepting paths is therefore exactly $C(m)$.

This does not establish membership in ordinary $\#\mathrm P$.

7. The unary-input obstruction to hardness

Proposition 4. If $C$, queried with unary parameters, is $\#\mathrm P$-hard under polynomial-time Turing reductions, then
$$ \#\mathrm P\subseteq\mathrm{FP}/\mathrm{poly}, \qquad \mathrm{NP}\subseteq\mathrm P/\mathrm{poly}. $$

Here $\mathrm{FP}/\mathrm{poly}$ permits polynomial-length advice depending only on input length.

Proof. Fix $f\in\#\mathrm P$ and a polynomial-time oracle algorithm computing $f$ using $C$. For inputs of length $n$, let $p(n)$ bound its running time. Every unary query has parameter at most $p(n)$.

Provide the advice table
$$ C(3),C(4),\ldots,C(\lfloor p(n)\rfloor). $$
Section 3 bounds each entry’s length by $O(p(n)^3)$. Including delimiters, the table has length $O(p(n)^4)$.

Replace every oracle call by a table lookup. This simulates adaptive queries as well as nonadaptive ones, in polynomial time with advice depending only on $n$. Hence $f\in\mathrm{FP}/\mathrm{poly}$.

For any language in NP, the number of accepting paths of its nondeterministic polynomial-time machine is a $\#\mathrm P$ function. Membership is equivalent to that count being positive. The preceding simulation therefore gives a polynomial-time decision procedure with polynomial advice. $\square$

Thus, under the explicit assumption
$$ \mathrm{NP}\not\subseteq\mathrm P/\mathrm{poly}, $$
the proposed hardness alternative is impossible. This conditional statement supplies no uniform polynomial-time algorithm for $C$.
