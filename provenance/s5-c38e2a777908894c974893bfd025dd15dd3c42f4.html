The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), target \(p\), fixed rational \(\alpha\), a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over strict rankings with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), an eligible-pool capacity \(\nu_t\) for each ranking type measured in units of baseline mass, and rational budget \(\kappa\), decide whether there are additions \(0\le x_t\le\nu_t\) with \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\) such that \(p\) maximizes the Copeland-\(\alpha\) score under \(\mu+x\). Pairwise margins are \(\Delta_{\mu+x}(a,b)=\sum_t(\mu_t+x_t)(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_t b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_t a])\), and the signs of those margins determine wins, losses, and ties.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix a rational \(\alpha\in[0,1]\). Given a finite nonempty candidate set \(C\), with \(m=|C|\), and a target \(p\in C\), consider constructive control by adding divisible voter mass under Copeland-\(\alpha\).
A society is given by its support: the list of its types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational. Types are strict total rankings of \(C\); \(\tau\) is the baseline support size, not the number of possible rankings.
The baseline masses satisfy \(\mu_t\geq0\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). An eligible pool is separately specified by its \(\rho\) positive binary-rational capacities \(\nu_t\), measured in baseline-mass units; its total mass need not equal one. Let \(T\) be the union of the two supports, assigning zero to omitted masses or capacities.
Given a binary-rational budget \(\kappa\geq0\), additions are real masses \(x_t\) satisfying \(0\leq x_t\leq\nu_t\) for \(t\in T\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}x_t\leq\kappa\); rankings remain fixed.
For distinct candidates, define \(\Delta_x(a,b)=\sum_{t\in T}(\mu_t+x_t)(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_t b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_t a])\). A positive margin is a win and a zero margin is a tie.
The score is \(s_x(a)=|\{b\in C\setminus\{a\}:\Delta_x(a,b)>0\}|+\alpha|\{b\in C\setminus\{a\}:\Delta_x(a,b)=0\}|\). Success means \(s_x(p)\geq s_x(a)\) for every \(a\in C\); tied winners are accepted without tie-breaking.

For each fixed rational \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), is deciding whether successful additions exist in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m,\tau,\rho,L\), where \(L\) is the total input bit length.

The discrete problem of adding at most a given number of unweighted voters from a specified pool is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete for every fixed rational \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), including strict rankings and acceptance of tied winners. [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0809.4484)
No complexity classification for exactly the divisible-mass problem with specified pool capacities was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restricted the fixed rational alpha to [0,1], as required by the discrete rule.
  • Required nonnegative binary-rational pool capacities and budget; allowed a zero budget and an empty eligible pool.
  • Specified the eligible pool separately so that it can contain ranking types absent from the baseline, as the discrete definition permits; omitted entries have zero mass or capacity.
  • Used tau for baseline support size and rho for eligible-pool support size, both counting only types with positive mass or capacity.
  • Allowed real divisible additions, with no normalization constraint on pool mass or the resulting total mass.

Classification

For every fixed rational \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), the specified divisible-mass control problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete.

A deterministic algorithm runs in time
\[ O\!\left((\tau+\rho)m^2\operatorname{poly}(L)\right) + 3^{\binom m2}\operatorname{poly}(m,\rho,L). \]
Thus the problem is polynomial-time solvable for every fixed candidate count \(m\).

The supplied paper establishes the discrete adding-voters result in [Theorem 4.22](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0809.4484). The reduction below establishes hardness directly for divisible masses, including arbitrary fractional choices.

1. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) and the running-time bound

Let \(N=\binom m2\). Only eligible types need addition variables, so there are \(\rho\) variables.

For each unordered candidate pair, guess whether its final margin is positive, zero, or negative. These \(N\) signs determine all Copeland scores, which can be compared exactly because \(\alpha\) is fixed and rational. Reject a pattern unless it makes \(p\) a tied or sole winner.

We can test realizability of a remaining pattern by rational linear programming. Introduce an auxiliary variable \(\eta\), impose
\[ 0\leq x_t\leq\nu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_t\leq\kappa,\qquad 0\leq\eta\leq1, \]
and impose the following pairwise constraints:

  • For a guessed zero margin, impose \(\Delta_x(a,b)=0\).
  • For a guessed positive margin, impose \(\Delta_x(a,b)\geq\eta\).
  • For a guessed negative margin, impose \(-\Delta_x(a,b)\geq\eta\).

Maximize \(\eta\). The pattern is realizable precisely when this LP is feasible and its optimum is positive.

Indeed, a positive optimum gives all the required strict inequalities. Conversely, if the pattern is realizable, the finitely many margins required to be nonzero have a positive minimum absolute value. Choosing \(\eta\) as that minimum, capped at one, gives a feasible positive value. If every guessed margin is zero, any feasible additions permit \(\eta=1\).

The LP has at most \(\rho+1\) variables and \(O(m^2+\rho)\) constraints. Its coefficients are rational with bit lengths polynomial in \(L\). The feasible region is bounded by the capacity constraints and the bounds on \(\eta\). The polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming therefore applies, including exact comparison of the optimum with zero.

Consequently, the guessed sign pattern is a polynomial-length certificate with a deterministic polynomial-time verifier. This proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\), despite the permission to use real additions.

Computing the baseline margins and eligible-type coefficients takes
\[ O\!\left((\tau+\rho)m^2\operatorname{poly}(L)\right) \]
time. Enumerating the \(3^N\) sign patterns gives the stated deterministic running time. No enumeration of all possible rankings is needed.

2. A normalized margin-realization lemma

Lemma. Suppose rational, antisymmetric prescribed margins \(d(a,b)\) satisfy
\[ W:=\sum_{\{a,b\}\subseteq C}|d(a,b)|\leq1. \]
There is a society of strict rankings, of total mass one and support size at most \(m(m-1)+2\), realizing exactly these margins. If the prescribed margins are dyadic, all constructed masses are dyadic.

Proof. For each nonzero prescribed margin, orient its pair as \(a,b\) so that \(d(a,b)>0\). Let \(R\) be any ordering of the remaining candidates. Give each of the rankings
\[ a\succ b\succ R, \qquad \operatorname{rev}(R)\succ a\succ b \]
mass \(d(a,b)/2\).

Together, these rankings contribute margin \(d(a,b)\) to \(a\) against \(b\). Every other pair receives zero net margin: comparisons involving one of \(a,b\) and a remaining candidate reverse between the two rankings, as do comparisons between remaining candidates.

The total mass used is \(W\). Give an arbitrary ranking and its reverse mass \((1-W)/2\) each. These contribute zero to every margin and bring the total mass to one.

Discard zero masses and merge duplicate rankings. There are at most two rankings per unordered pair, plus two filler rankings. The construction preserves dyadic rationality. \(\square\)

3. Reduction from \(\mathrm{3SAT}\)

We use the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete satisfiability problem with clauses containing between one and three literals and no variable repeated within a clause. This restriction follows from ordinary \(\mathrm{3SAT}\) by deleting tautological clauses and duplicate literals. An unused variable can be added to ensure that the variable count is positive.

Let the input formula have variables \(z_1,\ldots,z_n\), where \(n\geq1\), and \(\ell\) clauses. Set
\[ \varepsilon=\frac1{32}. \]

3.1 Candidates and eligible masses

Besides \(p\) and a rival \(r\), introduce test candidates
\[ G,\quad L_i,H_i,B_i\quad(1\leq i\leq n), \quad Z_j\quad(1\leq j\leq\ell). \]
Their number is
\[ q=3n+\ell+1. \]

Introduce \(D=2n+3\) dummy candidates. Thus
\[ m=q+D+2=5n+\ell+6. \]

Choose \(\delta=2^{-h}\), where \(h\) is the least integer satisfying
\[ 2^h\geq8(n+2)m^2. \]

For each variable, introduce two eligible ranking types \(t_i^+\) and \(t_i^-\), each with capacity \(\delta\). Write their additions as
\[ x_{t_i^+}=\delta u_i,\qquad x_{t_i^-}=\delta v_i. \]
Set the budget to \(\kappa=n\delta\). Hence
\[ 0\leq u_i,v_i\leq1,\qquad s:=\sum_{i=1}^n(u_i+v_i)\leq n. \]

For clause \(j\), let \(w_j\) be its total normalized literal mass: a literal \(z_i\) contributes \(u_i\), and a literal \(\neg z_i\) contributes \(v_i\).

We will realize the following final margins:

| Test candidate \(A\) | Normalized margin \(\Delta_x(p,A)/\delta\) |
|---|---|
| \(G\) | \(s-n+\varepsilon\) |
| \(L_i\) | \(s-n+2\varepsilon-2u_i\) |
| \(H_i\) | \(2u_i-s+n-2+2\varepsilon\) |
| \(B_i\) | \(s-n+2+2\varepsilon-2(u_i+v_i)\) |
| \(Z_j\) | \(2w_j-s+n-1\) |

Each expression has coefficient either positive one or negative one for every addition variable. For the clause expressions, this uses the fact that no variable occurs twice in a clause.

These coefficients can be realized by strict eligible rankings. For each type, place:

  • \(r\) first;
  • test candidates whose coefficients for that type are negative;
  • \(p\);
  • test candidates whose coefficients are positive;
  • all dummy candidates.

Within each group, use a fixed label order. Thus every eligible ranking is strict, and its comparisons with \(p\) give exactly the required coefficients.

The constant terms in the table specify the required baseline margins against the test candidates.

The eligible rankings are distinct. Type \(t_i^+\) is the only type having a positive coefficient against \(H_i\). Among the negative types, \(t_i^-\) is distinguished by its negative coefficient against \(B_i\), while \(t_j^-\) has a positive coefficient there whenever \(j\neq i\). Thus \(\rho=2n\).

3.2 Fixed comparisons and score padding

Prescribe the following fixed outcomes:

  • \(p\) defeats every dummy and loses to \(r\).
  • \(r\) defeats every test candidate and exactly \(n+2\) dummies.
  • Every dummy defeats every test candidate.
  • The dummy candidates form a regular tournament, each defeating exactly \(n+1\) other dummies.
  • Comparisons between test candidates have arbitrary fixed orientations.

The regular tournament exists explicitly: index the \(D=2n+3\) dummies modulo \(D\), and let dummy \(a\) defeat dummy \(b\) when
\[ b-a\pmod D\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}. \]
Exactly one direction is selected for each pair, and every dummy has \(n+1\) wins.

Give every prescribed fixed comparison baseline margin of magnitude \(2n\delta\). Any permitted additions change a margin by at most their total mass, which is at most \(n\delta\). Consequently, all these comparisons retain their prescribed strict outcomes.

Define
\[ K=q+n+3. \]
For all permitted additions,
\[ s_x(r)=q+1+(n+2)=K. \]

A dummy scores at most
\[ q+(n+1)+1=K-1: \]
it defeats all tests, has \(n+1\) internal wins, loses to \(p\), and receives at most one point against \(r\).

A test candidate loses to \(r\) and all dummies. It receives at most \(q-1\) points against other tests and at most one against \(p\), so its score is at most
\[ q<K. \]

Next, the two endpoint-test margins satisfy
\[ \Delta_x(p,L_i)+\Delta_x(p,H_i) =\delta(-2+4\varepsilon)<0. \]
At least one of these margins is therefore strictly negative. Since every comparison contributes at most one point, the pair contributes at most one point to \(p\), for every \(\alpha\in[0,1]\).

Candidate \(p\) has \(D\) fixed dummy wins. Its \(n\) endpoint-test pairs contribute at most \(n\), and its remaining \(n+\ell+1\) test comparisons contribute at most \(n+\ell+1\). Hence
\[ s_x(p)\leq D+n+(n+\ell+1) =4n+\ell+4=K. \]

It follows that successful additions must attain equality. Thus:

  • Each comparison against \(G,B_i,Z_j\) contributes exactly one point.
  • Each pair of comparisons against \(L_i,H_i\) contributes exactly one point.

In particular, all compulsory-test margins are nonnegative, and exactly one of the margins against \(L_i,H_i\) is nonnegative.

This implication also holds when \(\alpha=1\): a tie can contribute one point, but a negative margin cannot.

3.3 Realizing the normalized baseline

All prescribed baseline margins have magnitude at most
\[ 2(n+2)\delta. \]
This bound covers both the table’s constant terms and the fixed margins of magnitude \(2n\delta\).

Therefore,
\[ \sum_{\{a,b\}}|d(a,b)| \leq\binom m2\,2(n+2)\delta \leq m^2(n+2)\delta \leq\frac18. \]

The margin-realization lemma applies. It constructs a baseline society of total mass exactly one, using strict rankings and positive dyadic masses after zero masses are discarded and duplicates are merged.

Together with the separately specified eligible capacities, this completes a valid instance of the problem.

4. Correctness of the reduction

4.1 A satisfying assignment yields successful additions

Given a satisfying assignment, choose

These additions respect the capacities and use exactly the budget, since \(s=n\).

The normalized margin against \(G\) is \(\varepsilon>0\). Each normalized margin against \(B_i\) is \(2\varepsilon>0\).

For each variable, the endpoint test corresponding to its chosen truth value has normalized margin \(2\varepsilon>0\); the other has margin
\[ -2+2\varepsilon<0. \]
Thus each endpoint-test pair contributes exactly one point.

Every clause contains a satisfied literal, so \(w_j\geq1\). Its normalized margin is
\[ 2w_j-1\geq1>0. \]

Consequently, \(p\) attains score \(K\). Candidate \(r\) also has score \(K\), and every other candidate has smaller score. Since tied winners are accepted, these additions succeed for every \(\alpha\in[0,1]\).

4.2 Successful fractional additions yield a satisfying assignment

Suppose arbitrary permitted real additions succeed. The score argument forces all compulsory-test margins to be nonnegative.

Let
\[ e=n-s. \]
The budget gives \(e\geq0\), and the gate comparison gives
\[ s-n+\varepsilon\geq0. \]
Therefore,
\[ 0\leq e\leq\varepsilon. \]

For each variable, exactly one of its endpoint-test margins is nonnegative. Declare \(z_i\) true when the margin against \(H_i\) is nonnegative, and false when the margin against \(L_i\) is nonnegative.

If \(z_i\) is false, its low-test inequality gives
\[ -e+2\varepsilon-2u_i\geq0, \]
hence
\[ u_i\leq\varepsilon-\frac e2\leq\varepsilon. \]

If \(z_i\) is true, its high-test inequality gives
\[ 2u_i+e-2+2\varepsilon\geq0, \]
hence
\[ u_i\geq1-\varepsilon-\frac e2. \]

The compulsory comparison against \(B_i\) gives
\[ -e+2+2\varepsilon-2(u_i+v_i)\geq0, \]
so
\[ u_i+v_i\leq1+\varepsilon-\frac e2. \]
For a true variable, subtracting its lower bound on \(u_i\) yields
\[ v_i\leq2\varepsilon. \]

Thus every literal that is false under the extracted assignment has normalized mass at most \(2\varepsilon\):

  • A false positive literal uses \(u_i\leq\varepsilon\).
  • A false negative literal uses \(v_i\leq2\varepsilon\).

Suppose some clause were unsatisfied. It has at most three literals, so
\[ w_j\leq6\varepsilon. \]
Its normalized margin would satisfy
\[ \frac{\Delta_x(p,Z_j)}{\delta} =2w_j+e-1 \leq12\varepsilon+\varepsilon-1 =\frac{13}{32}-1<0. \]
This contradicts the compulsory nonnegative margin against \(Z_j\).

Every clause is therefore satisfied. The argument covers all real fractional additions, including solutions containing pairwise ties.
