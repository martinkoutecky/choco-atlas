The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two layers of \([0,1]\), a finite set \(T\) of monotone \(K\)-Lipschitz valuation types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and \(\varepsilon>0\), compute a feasible contiguous three-piece division \(A(x,y)\) using one short and one long knife, together with assignments for each external birthday choice \(r\in\{1,2,3\}\). Represent each assignment by masses \(z^{(r)}_{tj}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{j=1}^3 z^{(r)}_{tj}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}z^{(r)}_{tj}=1/3\); require \(z^{(r)}_{tj}>0\) only if \(v_t(A_j(x,y))+\varepsilon\ge\max_{k\in\{1,2,3\}}v_t(A_k(x,y))\).

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable in the stated oracle model. The algorithm uses \(O(\tau s^2)\) valuation queries and unit-cost arithmetic operations, apart from polynomial overhead for rational input and output, where \(s=1+\lceil\log_2(1+K/\varepsilon)\rceil\).

Because the external birthday individual has zero society mass, the assignment requirements are identical for all three choices. We construct one assignment \(z\) and set \(z^{(r)}=z\) for every \(r\).

The proof adapts the weighted-preference and partial-monotonicity ideas in [Igarashi–Meunier, Section 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2106.02262v4). The essential additional ingredient is a rational-lattice argument: denominator clearing is used only for arithmetic, never to create replicated agents.

1. Rational weighted preference labels

Write each mass as \(\mu_t=a_t/b_t\), with positive integers \(a_t,b_t\), and let \(D=\prod_{t\in T}b_t\). Then \(D\mu_t\) is an integer, and \(\log_2D\le L\).

Relabel the layers, if necessary, so that the supported types weakly preferring the entire top layer to the entire bottom layer have total mass at least \(\frac12\). This takes \(O(\tau)\) queries.

Choose the smallest power of two \(N\) satisfying \(N\ge\max\{1,6K/\varepsilon\}\). Consider the grid with vertices \(u=(k/N,h/N)\), where \(0\le k,h\le N\).

At every queried vertex \(u=(x,y)\), give each type \(t\) a label \(c_t(u)\in\{1,2,3\}\), selecting an exactly most valuable bundle according to these rules:

  • Exclude zero-length bundles.
  • Among the remaining maximizers, choose bundle \(1\) if available.
  • If bundles \(2\) and \(3\) remain tied, choose \(2\) when \(x\le y\), and \(3\) otherwise.

These rules always select a maximizer. Indeed, Lipschitz continuity gives every zero-length bundle the value of the empty bundle. Monotonicity gives every bundle at least that value. If the maximum exceeds the empty-bundle value, every maximizer has positive length; otherwise every bundle is a maximizer and some bundle has positive length.

Define the weighted label vector by

\(F_j(u)=\sum_{t:c_t(u)=j}\mu_t,\qquad F(u)=(F_1(u),F_2(u),F_3(u)).\)

Thus \(F(u)\) belongs to the simplex \(\Delta=\{z\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^3:z_1+z_2+z_3=1\}\), and every coordinate of \(F(u)\) is an integer multiple of \(1/D\). Evaluating \(F(u)\), including all labels, costs \(O(\tau)\) operations.

2. Monotonicity and boundary symmetry

Fix a grid column \(x=k/N\). As \(y\) increases, bundle \(A_1\) grows, while \(A_2\) and \(A_3\) shrink, up to ignored endpoints.

Consequently, once a type chooses label \(1\), it continues choosing label \(1\) at every higher vertex of that column. Its chosen bundle remains a maximizer by monotonicity, and the tie rule continues selecting it. Therefore \(F_1(k/N,h/N)\) is nondecreasing in \(h\).

At the lower boundary, bundle \(A_1\) has zero length and is excluded, so \(F_1(x,0)=0\).

At the upper boundary, bundle \(A_1\) is the entire top layer, and each other bundle is contained in the bottom layer. Every type weakly preferring the entire top layer therefore selects label \(1\). Hence \(F_1(x,1)\ge\frac12\).

Finally, the divisions at \((0,y)\) and \((1,y)\) have identical bundle \(1\), with bundles \(2\) and \(3\) exchanged. The tie rules respect this exchange:

\(F(1,y)=(F_1(0,y),F_3(0,y),F_2(0,y)).\)

For \(y<1\), the rule for a tie between \(2\) and \(3\) selects exchanged labels. At \(y=1\), one of these bundles is empty. If bundle \(1\) is not selected, the nonempty bottom-layer bundle must strictly exceed its value, and so the exchanged label is again selected.

3. A rational-lattice lemma

Set

\(\omega=(\frac13,\frac13,\frac13),\qquad \delta=\frac{1}{12D^2},\qquad q=(\frac13-\delta,\frac13+\frac{\delta}{2},\frac13+\frac{\delta}{2}).\)

Write \(\alpha=q_1\) and \(\beta=q_2=q_3\).

Every integer multiple of \(1/D\) strictly below \(\frac13\) is at least \(1/(3D)\) below it. Since \(\delta<1/(3D)\), there is no such multiple in \([\alpha,\frac13)\). In particular, \(\alpha\) is not an integer multiple of \(1/D\).

Lemma. Let \(P\) be a finite set of points in \(\Delta\), each having coordinates that are integer multiples of \(1/D\). If \(q\in\operatorname{conv}P\), then \(\omega\in\operatorname{conv}P\).

Proof. For distinct \(u,v\in P\), consider \(\phi(z)=\det(z,u,v)\), with the three vectors as rows. Since \(Du,Dv\) have integer coordinates, cofactor expansion gives integers \(A,B,C\), each of absolute value at most \(D^2\), such that

\(\phi(z)=(Az_1+Bz_2+Cz_3)/D^2.\)

Therefore

\(\phi(\omega)=(A+B+C)/(3D^2),\)

and

\(\phi(q)-\phi(\omega)=\delta(-A+(B+C)/2)/D^2.\)

The latter difference has absolute value at most \(2\delta=1/(6D^2)\). Whenever \(\phi(\omega)\ne0\), its absolute value is at least \(1/(3D^2)\). Thus a nonzero \(\phi(\omega)\) has the same sign as \(\phi(q)\). In particular,

  • \(\phi(q)>0\) implies \(\phi(\omega)\ge0\);
  • \(\phi(q)<0\) implies \(\phi(\omega)\le0\);
  • \(\phi(q)=0\) implies \(\phi(\omega)=0\).

If \(\operatorname{conv}P\) is two-dimensional in the plane \(z_1+z_2+z_3=1\), it is the intersection of the closed halfplanes supported by its polygon edges. Each edge joins two points of \(P\). The implications above show that \(\omega\) satisfies every supporting halfplane satisfied by \(q\), proving membership.

If the hull is a segment \([u,v]\), its endpoints belong to \(P\). Neither endpoint has first coordinate \(\alpha\), so, after exchanging them, \(u_1<\alpha<v_1\). The lattice observation gives \(u_1<\frac13\le v_1\). Also \(\phi(q)=0\), hence \(\phi(\omega)=0\), so \(\omega\) lies on the same affine line as \(u,v\). Because the first coordinates of the endpoints differ, the first coordinate parametrizes this line, and the preceding inequalities place \(\omega\) in the segment.

A singleton hull cannot contain \(q\), since its first coordinate would have to equal the nonlattice number \(\alpha\). This exhausts the cases. \(\square\)

4. Finding a square whose preference hull contains the target

We now find a grid square \(S\) such that \(q\) belongs to the convex hull of the weighted label vectors at its four corners. The lattice lemma will then give the same conclusion for \(\omega\).

4.1. Crossing points on columns

For a column \(k\), monotonicity and the boundary inequalities imply that there is a unique index \(h_k\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}\) satisfying

\(F_1(k/N,h_k/N)<\alpha<F_1(k/N,(h_k+1)/N).\)

Strictness holds because no vertex first coordinate equals \(\alpha\). Binary search finds \(h_k\) using \(O(\log N)\) vertex evaluations.

Let \(G_k\) be the unique point of the segment joining these two weighted label vectors whose first coordinate is \(\alpha\). It is computed by rational interpolation. Define \(\sigma_k=G_{k,2}-\beta\).

If \(\sigma_k=0\), then \(G_k=q\), so either square incident to that vertical grid edge has the desired convex-hull property.

Otherwise, boundary symmetry gives

\(G_N=(G_{0,1},G_{0,3},G_{0,2}),\qquad \sigma_N=-\sigma_0,\)

because \(\beta=(1-\alpha)/2\).

Starting with columns \(0,N\), binary search on the column index therefore either finds a zero sign or produces adjacent columns \(k,k+1\) with opposite signs. No monotonicity of these signs is needed: at each step retain the subinterval whose endpoints have opposite signs.

This stage costs \(O(\tau(1+\log N)^2)\).

4.2. Crossing points inside the final strip

Let \(a=h_k\) and \(b=h_{k+1}\).

If \(a=b\), the two crossing segments are opposite vertical edges of the same square. Their crossing images have first coordinate \(\alpha\) and second coordinates on opposite sides of \(\beta\). Their joining segment therefore contains \(q\), proving that this square works.

Suppose next that \(a<b\). At every horizontal grid edge in the strip at height \(h/N\), where \(a+1\le h\le b\), the left endpoint has first preference coordinate above \(\alpha\), and the right endpoint has it below \(\alpha\). Its image segment therefore has a unique crossing point \(H_h\) with first coordinate \(\alpha\).

Consider the list

\(G_k,\ H_{a+1},\ H_{a+2},\ldots,H_b,\ G_{k+1}.\)

Every consecutive pair comes from two edges of a common grid square:

  • \(G_k,H_{a+1}\) use the square in row \(a\);
  • \(H_h,H_{h+1}\) use the square in row \(h\);
  • \(H_b,G_{k+1}\) use the square in row \(b\).

The first and last points have opposite signs of the second coordinate minus \(\beta\). Binary search on the list therefore finds either a point equal to \(q\), or a consecutive pair with opposite signs. Every intermediate list point can be evaluated directly from its two grid-edge endpoints.

For a consecutive pair with opposite signs, \(q\) lies on their joining segment. Each point is itself a convex combination of two corner images of their common square. Hence \(q\) belongs to the convex hull of that square’s four corner images.

When \(a>b\), the same construction starts from the right column, which has the lower crossing row, and ends at the left column.

This second search uses \(O(\tau(1+\log N))\) operations. We have therefore found the required square without traversing all rows or columns.

5. Constructing exact rational assignments

Let \(u_1,\ldots,u_4\) be the corners of the resulting square \(S\). By the lattice lemma,

\(\omega\in\operatorname{conv}\{F(u_1),F(u_2),F(u_3),F(u_4)\}.\)

Compute nonnegative rational coefficients \(\lambda_i\) satisfying

\(\sum_{i=1}^4\lambda_i=1,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^4\lambda_iF(u_i)=\omega.\)

For completeness, these coefficients can be found by enumerating the subsets of at most three corner images and solving their rational linear systems. Such a subset suffices: choose a convex representation with minimum positive support. If its support is affinely dependent, an affine dependence permits changing the coefficients, while preserving their sum and represented point, until one positive coefficient becomes zero. This contradicts minimality. The support is therefore affinely independent and has size at most three in the two-dimensional simplex. Its coefficients are the unique solution of a rational linear system and hence are rational.

Choose any corner \(p=(x,y)\) of \(S\) as the output division. Define

\(z_{tj}=\mu_t\sum_{i:c_t(u_i)=j}\lambda_i.\)

These are nonnegative rational numbers. Since each corner gives each type exactly one label,

\(\sum_{j=1}^3z_{tj}=\mu_t.\)

Moreover,

\(\sum_tz_{tj}=\sum_{i=1}^4\lambda_iF_j(u_i)=\frac13.\)

Thus the row and column constraints hold exactly.

6. Verifying the additive error

Any two points \(u,p\) in the same grid square differ by at most \(1/N\) in each coordinate. The function \((x,y)\mapsto\max\{x,y\}\) also changes by at most \(1/N\).

For intervals, the measure of symmetric difference is at most the sum of the absolute changes in their two endpoints. Applying this layer by layer gives

\(\sum_{\ell=1}^2\lambda(A_j(u)_\ell\triangle A_j(p)_\ell)\le3/N\)

for every bundle \(j\). Indeed, bundle \(1\) contributes at most \(1/N\), bundle \(2\) at most \(2/N\), and bundle \(3\) at most \(3/N\).

Consequently, for every type \(t\) and bundle \(j\),

\(\lvert v_t(A_j(u))-v_t(A_j(p))\rvert\le3K/N.\)

If \(c_t(u)=j\), then \(j\) is an exact maximizer at \(u\). For every bundle \(k\),

\(v_t(A_k(p))-v_t(A_j(p))\le v_t(A_k(u))-v_t(A_j(u))+6K/N\le6K/N\le\varepsilon.\)

If \(z_{tj}>0\), some corner with positive coefficient has label \(j\) for type \(t\). Applying the inequality at that corner proves

\(v_t(A_j(p))+\varepsilon\ge\max_kv_t(A_k(p)).\)

Set \(z^{(r)}_{tj}=z_{tj}\) for all birthday choices \(r\). These assignments satisfy every requested condition.

7. Complexity and encoding

The grid size satisfies \(\log N=O(1+\log(1+K/\varepsilon))\). The searches use \(O(\tau(1+\log N)^2)\) unit-cost operations.

Although \(D\) can be exponentially large numerically, its bit length is at most \(L\). The algorithm performs arithmetic with \(D\) and the masses; it never performs a loop of length \(D\). The perturbation has bit length \(O(L)\), and all search interpolations have polynomial bit length.

The final coefficients come from linear systems of order at most three with rational coefficients of polynomial bit length. Thus the assignment masses have polynomial encoding length. The output coordinates have denominator \(N\) and bit length \(O(1+\log(1+K/\varepsilon))\).

This establishes the required polynomial-time bound in the supported-type input model.
