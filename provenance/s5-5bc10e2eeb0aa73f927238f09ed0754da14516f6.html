The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite alternative set \(X\), a finite family of nonempty menus \(\mathcal B\), and rational population shares \(p_{xB}\) for \(x\in B\), decide whether there is a distribution \(\mu\) over strict rankings of \(X\) such that \(p_{xB}=\sum_{t:\operatorname{top}_t(B)=x}\mu_t\) for every \(B\in\mathcal B\) and \(x\in B\). Each individual chooses one alternative; the shares are the fractions of the population making each choice.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(X\) be a nonempty finite set of \(m\) alternatives, and let \(\mathcal B\subseteq 2^X\setminus\{\varnothing\}\) be an explicitly listed family of \(k\) menus. The input gives binary rational shares \(p_{xB}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_{x\in B}p_{xB}=1\) for every \(B\in\mathcal B\); let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A type \(t\) is a strict ranking of \(X\), and \(\operatorname{top}_t(B)\) is its unique highest-ranked alternative in \(B\). A society is represented by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational, with total mass \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not \(m!\).
The society is unknown and its support is unrestricted. Decide whether there exists such a society \(\mu\) satisfying \(p_{xB}=\sum_{t:\operatorname{top}_t(B)=x}\mu_t\) for every observed \(B\) and \(x\in B\). Decision running time is measured in \(m,k,L\); any running time involving an explicitly represented society also includes \(\tau\) and its encoding length.

Is this stochastic rationalizability problem in \(\mathsf P\), or is it \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard?

Deterministic choice rationalizable by a weak order admits a universal, recursively enumerable axiomatization relative to nonempty choice data ([Chambers, Echenique, and Shmaya](https://doi.org/10.3982/TE1924)).
For the continuous problem, testing rationalizability by a distribution over strict rankings is already claimed \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete when the observed menus are all pairs ([Smeulders et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6224134/)). Thus the stated complexity target is already settled in the literature, rather than open.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Population shares are nonnegative binary rationals normalized to sum to one separately on each menu; total society mass is one.
  • The alternative set is nonempty; menus are explicitly listed nonempty subsets, without a requirement to observe every subset.
  • The support convention applies to the unknown rationalizing society, not to an additional supplied input; no support bound is imposed and tau is not an input parameter.
  • Positive masses in a society representation are binary rationals; the graded question's unspecified mass domain is made explicit.
  • Strict rankings and unique choices are retained as stipulated in the graded question; no tie-breaking rule is needed.

Conclusion

The problem is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete, even when the observed menus are exactly all pairs. Consequently, a polynomial-time algorithm exists if and only if \(\mathsf P=\mathsf{NP}\).

This agrees with the claim in [Smeulders et al., *Testing Probabilistic Models of Choice using Column Generation*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6224134/). Below, I give a direct hardness argument using established polyhedral results, and prove membership in \(\mathsf{NP}\) with the required rational encoding bounds.

1. Rationalizability as convex-hull membership

Put \(D=\sum_{B\in\mathcal B}|B|\). For each strict ranking \(t\), define its choice vector \(a^t\in\{0,1\}^{D}\) by

\(a^t_{xB}=\mathbf 1\{\operatorname{top}_t(B)=x\}.\)

Let \(p\in\mathbb Q^D\) be the vector of observed shares. The required equations are precisely

\(p=\sum_t\mu_t a^t,\qquad \mu_t\geq 0,\qquad \sum_t\mu_t=1.\)

Thus rationalizability implies membership in \(\operatorname{conv}\{a^t:t\text{ is a strict ranking}\}\). Section 3 proves the converse with polynomially encoded rational masses, even if convex-hull membership initially supplies real coefficients.

2. Hardness already for all pair menus

Established results used

Define the correlation polytope

\(\operatorname{COR}(n)=\operatorname{conv}\{(z_i z_j)_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}:z\in\{0,1\}^{n}\}.\)

Pitowsky’s membership theorem: deciding whether a binary-encoded rational vector belongs to \(\operatorname{COR}(n)\), with \(n\) variable, is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-complete. This is the exact membership problem, not an optimization problem. See [Pitowsky, *Correlation polytopes: Their geometry and complexity*](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01594946).

For a strict ranking \(t\) of \(\{1,\ldots,r\}\), let \(v^t_{ab}=1\) when \(a\) ranks above \(b\), for \(a<b\). Define

\(\operatorname{LO}(r)=\operatorname{conv}\{v^t:t\text{ is a strict ranking}\}.\)

Maksimenko’s face theorem: the following injective linear map \(T\), from coordinates \(q_{ij}\) to coordinates \(y_{ab}\), identifies \(\operatorname{COR}(n)\) with a face of \(\operatorname{LO}(2n)\):

\(y_{2i-1,2i}=q_{ii}\quad(1\leq i\leq n),\)

and, for every \(i<j\),

\(y_{2i,2j-1}=0,\qquad y_{2i,2j}=q_{jj}-q_{ij},\)

\(y_{2i-1,2j}=q_{ii}+q_{jj}-q_{ij},\qquad y_{2i-1,2j-1}=q_{ii}-q_{ij}.\)

More precisely,

\(T(\operatorname{COR}(n))=\operatorname{LO}(2n)\cap T(\mathbb R^{n(n+1)/2}).\)

These formulas and the face identification are given in [Maksimenko, *Boolean quadric polytopes are faces of linear ordering polytopes*, Theorem 1 and its proof](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.06170). Injectivity also follows directly: recover \(q_{ii}=y_{2i-1,2i}\) and \(q_{ij}=q_{jj}-y_{2i,2j}\).

Construction of the reduction

Given a correlation-polytope membership instance \(q\), compute \(y=T(q)\).

If some coordinate of \(y\) lies outside \([0,1]\), then \(y\notin\operatorname{LO}(2n)\), because every vertex of that polytope has coordinates in \(\{0,1\}\). The face theorem therefore implies \(q\notin\operatorname{COR}(n)\). In this case, output the fixed no-instance on three alternatives with deterministic pair choices

\(1\succ 2,\qquad 2\succ 3,\qquad 3\succ 1.\)

This instance is impossible: each probability-one comparison must hold in every positive-mass type, whereas no strict ranking contains that cycle.

Otherwise, set \(X=\{1,\ldots,2n\}\), list every pair menu, and assign, for \(a<b\),

\(p_{a,\{a,b\}}=y_{ab},\qquad p_{b,\{a,b\}}=1-y_{ab}.\)

These are nonnegative rational shares summing to \(1\). A ranking chooses \(a\) from \(\{a,b\}\) exactly when \(v^t_{ab}=1\). Consequently,

\(\text{constructed shares are rationalizable}\iff y\in\operatorname{LO}(2n).\)

The reverse implication uses the rational-certificate lemma proved below. Since \(y=T(q)\) belongs to the image of \(T\), the face theorem and injectivity give

\(y\in\operatorname{LO}(2n)\iff q\in\operatorname{COR}(n).\)

The construction lists \(\binom{2n}{2}\) menus. Each share requires only a constant number of additions or subtractions of input rationals, so the output bit length and construction time are polynomial in the source input length.

This is a polynomial-time many-one reduction. Therefore stochastic rationalizability is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard even for all pair menus.

3. Polynomial certificates despite unrestricted support

Suppose \(p\) belongs to the convex hull from Section 1. Choose a representation with the smallest possible number \(s\) of positive coefficients:

\(p=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_i a^{t_i},\qquad \lambda_i>0,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_i=1.\)

Define \(c_i=(1,a^{t_i})\in\{0,1\}^{D+1}\) and \(b=(1,p)\). Then

\(\sum_{i=1}^{s}\lambda_i c_i=b.\)

Support bound

The columns \(c_i\) are linearly independent. Otherwise, there would be a nonzero vector \(z\) satisfying \(\sum_i z_i c_i=0\). Its first coordinate gives \(\sum_i z_i=0\), so \(z\) has both positive and negative entries.

Set \(\theta=\min_{z_i>0}\lambda_i/z_i\). Replacing \(\lambda_i\) by \(\lambda_i-\theta z_i\) preserves every equation, leaves all coefficients nonnegative, and makes at least one coefficient zero. This contradicts minimality.

Hence \(s\leq D+1\).

Rationality and bit length

Because these columns are independent, select \(s\) rows giving an invertible square matrix \(C\in\{0,1\}^{s\times s}\). Let \(b'\) consist of the corresponding coordinates of \(b\). The coefficients uniquely satisfy

\(C\lambda=b'.\)

Let \(Q\) be the product of the positive denominators of the observed shares. Its binary length is \(O(L)\), and \(Qb'\) is integral with entries between \(0\) and \(Q\).

By Cramer’s rule,

\(\lambda_i=\dfrac{\det C_i(Qb')}{Q\det C},\)

where \(C_i(Qb')\) replaces column \(i\) of \(C\) by \(Qb'\). Hadamard’s determinant inequality gives

\(|\det C|\leq s^{s/2},\qquad |\det C_i(Qb')|\leq Qs^{s/2}.\)

Indeed, the original columns have Euclidean norm at most \(\sqrt{s}\), and the replacement column has norm at most \(Q\sqrt{s}\).

Therefore every positive mass is rational and has encoding length

\(O\bigl(L+s\log(s+1)\bigr).\)

This proves both that real feasibility implies rational feasibility and that a polynomial-size certificate exists.

Verification

A certificate lists these at most \(D+1\) rankings and their rational masses. Each ranking requires \(O(m\log(m+1))\) bits. Since \(D\leq mk\), the complete certificate has size polynomial in \(m,k,L\).

A verifier checks that each ranking is a permutation of \(X\), that the masses are positive and sum to \(1\), and that every observed share equals the corresponding sum of masses. Exact rational arithmetic takes polynomial time in the combined input and certificate length.

Thus the problem belongs to \(\mathsf{NP}\).

4. Interpretation of the result

Combining Sections 2 and 3 proves \(\mathsf{NP}\)-completeness under polynomial-time many-one reductions.

The support parameter \(\tau\) creates no ambiguity: verification of an explicitly represented society is polynomial in its encoding length, while every rationalizable instance has some society with \(\tau\leq D+1\) and polynomially encoded masses. Finding such a society remains computationally hard.

A recursively enumerable deterministic axiomatization does not supply a polynomial-time algorithm for this stochastic membership problem. Likewise, the factorial-size linear-program formulation alone proves neither polynomial-time solvability nor hardness; the reduction above establishes hardness directly.
