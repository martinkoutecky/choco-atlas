The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given finite-support rational buyer and seller mass measures, find a generalized crossing price for \(D(p)=\sum_{v\ge p}\nu_B(v)\) and \(S(p)=\sum_{w\le p}\nu_S(w)\). At a jump, trade the smaller eligible mass and uniformly ration the excess side, allowing fractional mass within types. Compute this mechanism’s exact gain from trade and its ratio to the optimum fractional matching value \(\max_{\pi}\sum_{v,w}(v-w)\pi_{vw}\), subject to the type-mass capacities and \(\pi_{vw}=0\) when \(v<w\); determine when the ratio is one.

There is one homogeneous good (\(m=1\)); buyers have unit demand and sellers unit supply. Buyer and seller types have nonnegative rational valuations \(v,w\) and positive rational masses \(\nu_B(v),\nu_S(w)\). Both sides are nonempty; their total masses may differ and need not sum to one.
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational. Here types include their buyer/seller role; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Define \(D(p)=\sum_{v\ge p}\nu_B(v)\), \(S(p)=\sum_{w\le p}\nu_S(w)\), and \(D_>(p)=\sum_{v>p}\nu_B(v)\). Choose the generalized crossing price \(p=\min\{x\ge0:D_>(x)\le S(x)\}\).
At this price all weakly eligible types participate. Trade mass \(t=\min\{D(p),S(p)\}\); if \(t>0\), each eligible buyer type trades fraction \(t/D(p)\) of its mass and each eligible seller type fraction \(t/S(p)\). Pair these traded masses arbitrarily, with payment \(p\) per unit; if \(t=0\), no trade occurs. Let \(G\) be the resulting total gain from trade.
The benchmark is \(\mathrm{OPT}=\max_{\pi\ge0}\sum_{v,w}(v-w)\pi_{vw}\), subject to \(\sum_w\pi_{vw}\le\nu_B(v)\), \(\sum_v\pi_{vw}\le\nu_S(w)\), and \(\pi_{vw}=0\) for \(v<w\). Write \(R=G/\mathrm{OPT}\) when \(\mathrm{OPT}>0\), and set \(R=1\) otherwise.

Result

The sharp worst-case approximation ratio is \(0\). Every individual instance with \(\mathrm{OPT}>0\) has \(R>0\), so the infimum is not attained.

There is also an exact characterization of all instances with \(R=1\), including those covered by the convention for \(\mathrm{OPT}=0\). The crossing price, gain, optimum, and ratio can all be computed exactly in polynomial time.

1. Crossing inequalities

Define the strictly eligible masses and the masses at the crossing price by

\(a=\sum_{v>p}\nu_B(v),\qquad b=\sum_{v=p}\nu_B(v),\qquad c=\sum_{w<p}\nu_S(w),\qquad e=\sum_{w=p}\nu_S(w).\)

Write \(d=D(p)=a+b\) and \(s=S(p)=c+e\), so \(t=\min\{d,s\}\).

First, the minimum defining \(p\) exists. Indeed, \(D_>(x)=0\) whenever \(x\) is at least the largest buyer valuation. Moreover, \(D_>(x)-S(x)\) is a nonincreasing step function whose changes occur only at supported valuations. Consequently, its first nonpositive point belongs to the finite set consisting of \(0\) and all supported valuations.

By the defining crossing condition,

\(a\le s.\)

We also have

\(c\le d.\)

For \(p=0\), this follows from \(c=0\), since valuations are nonnegative. For \(p>0\), choose \(y<p\) sufficiently close to \(p\) that there is no supported valuation in \([y,p)\). Then \(D_>(y)=D(p)=d\) and \(S(y)=c\). Minimality of \(p\) gives \(D_>(y)>S(y)\), hence \(d>c\).

Since also \(a\le d\) and \(c\le s\), these inequalities imply

\(\max\{a,c\}\le t\le\min\{a+b,c+e\}.\)

Thus trade mass \(t\) can accommodate every strictly eligible buyer and every strictly eligible seller, using agents at the price to balance the two sides.

2. An exact formula for the optimum

Define the two nonnegative surplus quantities

\(X=\sum_{v>p}(v-p)\nu_B(v),\qquad Y=\sum_{w<p}(p-w)\nu_S(w).\)

Claim. For every instance, \(\mathrm{OPT}=X+Y\).

Upper bound. For a real number \(z\), write \(z_+=\max\{z,0\}\). For every buyer and seller valuation,

\(v-w\le(v-p)_++(p-w)_+.\)

Therefore any feasible allocation \(\pi\) satisfies

\(\sum_{v,w}(v-w)\pi_{vw}\le\sum_v(v-p)_+\sum_w\pi_{vw}+\sum_w(p-w)_+\sum_v\pi_{vw}\le X+Y.\)

The final inequality uses the capacity constraints and the nonnegativity of the coefficients.

Attainment. Suppose first that \(t>0\). Select buyer masses \(x_v\) by taking every buyer with \(v>p\), together with mass \(t-a\) of buyers at \(p\). This is possible because \(0\le t-a\le b\). Similarly, select every seller with \(w<p\), together with mass \(t-c\) of sellers at \(p\), obtaining seller masses \(y_w\).

Both selected sides have total mass \(t\). Define

\(\pi_{vw}=x_vy_w/t.\)

Its row sums are \(x_v\), its column sums are \(y_w\), and every pair with positive mass satisfies \(v\ge p\ge w\). Hence it is feasible. Its gain equals

\(\sum_{v,w}(v-w)\pi_{vw}=\sum_v(v-p)x_v+\sum_w(p-w)y_w=X+Y.\)

If \(t=0\), the crossing inequalities imply \(a=c=0\), so \(X=Y=0\). The upper bound and the feasible zero allocation then give \(\mathrm{OPT}=0\). This proves the claim.

3. Gain and exact efficiency characterization

When \(t>0\), uniform rationing selects buyer mass \((t/d)\nu_B(v)\) for each \(v\ge p\), and seller mass \((t/s)\nu_S(w)\) for each \(w\le p\). Because both selected sides have mass \(t\), any pairing has gain

\(G=\dfrac{t}{d}X+\dfrac{t}{s}Y.\)

Thus pairing does not affect total gain. When \(\mathrm{OPT}>0\),

\(R=\dfrac{(t/d)X+(t/s)Y}{X+Y}.\)

In particular,

\(\mathrm{OPT}-G=\left(1-\dfrac{t}{d}\right)X+\left(1-\dfrac{t}{s}\right)Y.\)

Both summands are nonnegative. If \(d=s\), both vanish. If \(d>s\), only the buyer coefficient is positive, so equality holds exactly when \(X=0\). If \(s>d\), equality holds exactly when \(Y=0\).

Because every supported mass is positive, \(X=0\) is equivalent to \(a=0\), and \(Y=0\) is equivalent to \(c=0\). Consequently, the following characterization holds for every instance:

\(R=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (d=s)\ \text{or}\ (d>s\ \text{and}\ a=0)\ \text{or}\ (s>d\ \text{and}\ c=0).\)

Equivalently:

  • The weakly eligible buyer and seller masses are equal; or
  • Buyers are rationed, and every eligible buyer has valuation exactly \(p\); or
  • Sellers are rationed, and every eligible seller has valuation exactly \(p\).

To check the zero-optimum convention explicitly: \(\mathrm{OPT}=0\) implies \(X=Y=0\), hence \(a=c=0\), so one of these three conditions holds. If \(t=0\), the same conclusion follows from the crossing inequalities. Thus the characterization includes all degenerate cases.

4. The sharp worst-case ratio

The gain formula gives \(G\ge0\), so \(R\ge0\). Moreover, if \(\mathrm{OPT}>0\), then \(X+Y>0\). A positive \(X\) implies \(a>0\), and a positive \(Y\) implies \(c>0\). Since \(t\ge\max\{a,c\}\), it follows that \(t>0\); both rationing fractions are positive. Hence \(G>0\) and \(R>0\).

Nevertheless, ratios approach zero even with strictly positive valuations and equal total masses. For each positive integer \(N\), consider:

| Role | Valuation | Mass |
|---|---:|---:|
| Buyer | \(2\) | \(N\) |
| Buyer | \(N+2\) | \(1\) |
| Seller | \(1\) | \(1\) |
| Seller | \(N+3\) | \(N\) |

For \(0\le x<1\), we have \(D_>(x)=N+1>S(x)=0\). For \(1\le x<2\), we have \(D_>(x)=N+1>S(x)=1\). At \(x=2\), both quantities equal \(1\). Therefore \(p=2\).

At this price,

\(d=N+1,\qquad s=t=1,\qquad X=N,\qquad Y=1.\)

The proved formulas give

\(\mathrm{OPT}=N+1,\qquad G=\dfrac{N}{N+1}+1,\qquad R=\dfrac{2N+1}{(N+1)^2}.\)

Since \(R\le 2/(N+1)\), these ratios tend to zero. Thus

\(\inf_{\mathrm{OPT}>0}R=0.\)

Each side has total mass \(N+1\). Scaling all masses by \(1/(N+1)\) gives total mass \(1\) on each side and preserves the crossing price and ratio. The example therefore also establishes the zero infimum under separate normalization of the two sides.

5. Exact computation and bit complexity

For the promised case \(m=1\), use the following algorithm:

  • Sort the supported valuations, grouping equal values.
  • Test \(0\), then scan supported valuations in increasing order. Maintain buyer mass strictly above the current candidate and seller mass weakly below it. The first candidate satisfying \(D_>(x)\le S(x)\) is \(p\).
  • Compute \(d,s,t,X,Y\).
  • Set \(\mathrm{OPT}=X+Y\). If \(t=0\), set \(G=0\); otherwise use the gain formula above. Return \(R=1\) if \(\mathrm{OPT}=0\), and \(R=G/\mathrm{OPT}\) otherwise.

This uses \(O(\tau\log(\tau+1))\) comparisons and arithmetic operations.

For an explicit bit bound, let \(Q\) be the product of all input denominators, including valuation and mass denominators. Its bit length is \(O(L)\). Represent each input rational as an integer divided by \(Q\). All scaled integers, mass sums, and surplus numerators have \(O(L)\) bits: summation adds at most \(O(\log\tau)\) bits, and \(\tau\le L\). Surpluses have common denominator \(Q^2\), and the gain and ratio formulas require only a constant number of further arithmetic operations on \(O(L)\)-bit integers.

Using schoolbook integer arithmetic, a valid exact running-time bound is

\(O\!\left(\tau\log(\tau+1)L^2\right)\)

bit operations for \(m=1\). Thus computation is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) on the stated domain; this makes no claim for multiple goods.

6. Relation to the published guarantee

Definition 3 and Theorem 4 of the [cited paper](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/people/paul.goldberg/papers/WINE2017-procs-paper.pdf) concern a price balancing expected eligible counts in an independent sampling model, with a probability guarantee relative to expected optimal gain.

The generalized crossing here permits unequal weakly eligible masses at atoms. The proved gain formula identifies exactly how that imbalance reduces deterministic gain; the four-type family demonstrates that the reduction can be arbitrarily severe.
