The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given goods \(G=[m]\), finitely many additive types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), values \(v_{tj}\), per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and fixed nonnegative rational prices \(p_j\), does there exist a mass allocation \(y_{t,S}\ge 0\) over integral bundles \(S\subseteq G\) such that \(\sum_{S\subseteq G}y_{t,S}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\); every bundle with \(y_{t,S}>0\) spends exactly the unit budget and maximizes \(\sum_{j\in S}v_{tj}\) among bundles with price at most \(1\); and total use of each good is at most \(q_j\), with equality whenever \(p_j>0\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(G=\{1,\ldots,m\}\), with \(m\ge1\). A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\ge1\) additive types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The input also specifies binary rational values \(v_{tj}\ge0\), per-capita supplies \(q_j\ge0\), and fixed prices \(p_j\ge0\). Each individual has budget \(1\) and may consume any integral bundle \(S\subseteq G\), containing at most one unit of each good. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
A mass allocation consists of real numbers \(y_{t,S}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{S\subseteq G}y_{t,S}=\mu_t\) for every type.
Whenever \(y_{t,S}>0\), require \(\sum_{j\in S}p_j=1\) and \(S\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{R\subseteq G:\,\sum_{j\in R}p_j\le1}\sum_{j\in R}v_{tj}\). All utility ties are permitted; alternatives are evaluated without aggregate supply restrictions.
For every good require \(\sum_t\sum_{S\ni j}y_{t,S}\le q_j\), with equality whenever \(p_j>0\).

Is deciding whether such a mass allocation exists in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For a finite list of buyers and one unit of each good, existence of an equilibrium allocation at fixed prices is \(\mathrm{coNP}\)-hard, even with two additive buyers ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1503.06855v2)).
With prices also unspecified, discrete additive equilibrium computation is strongly \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard ([source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1501.06627)). No result settling the exact mass-allocation problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Values were unspecified in sign and encoding; use nonnegative binary rationals, consistent with the nonnegative utility domain.
  • Supplies were unspecified in domain; use arbitrary nonnegative binary rational per-capita supplies.
  • Use only positive-mass types in the support, omitting zero-mass entries; assume at least one good.
  • Allocation masses may be real and nonnegative; input numbers remain binary rationals.
  • Permit every utility-maximizing tie, and compare against every affordable integral bundle independently of aggregate supplies.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions, even when \(\tau=1\), all prices, values, and supplies are strictly positive dyadic rationals, and an optimal bundle costing exactly \(1\) always exists.

The proof directly addresses mass allocations, including arbitrary splitting of type mass.

1. Source problem and padding

We reduce from the \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem PARTITION: given positive binary integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\), decide whether some \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) satisfies
\[ 2\sum_{i\in I}a_i=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i. \]
This is a standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem from [Karp’s reduction theorem](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Write \(A=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\), and let \(D\) be the smallest power of two strictly larger than \(2A\). Define \(n+2\) positive integers by
\[ b_i=2a_i\quad(1\le i\le n),\qquad b_{n+1}=b_{n+2}=D-A. \]
Their sum is \(2D\).

Padding lemma. There is a subset \(T\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n+2\}\) with \(\sum_{j\in T}b_j=D\) if and only if the original PARTITION instance is a yes-instance.

To prove this, observe that the first \(n\) integers sum to \(2A<D\), while each padding integer \(D-A\) exceeds \(D/2\). A subset summing to \(D\) must therefore contain exactly one padding integer. Its remaining indices form a set \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\) satisfying
\[ 2\sum_{i\in I}a_i=D-(D-A)=A. \]
Conversely, any such \(I\), together with one padding index, gives a subset summing to \(D\).

2. Construction of the society

There is one type, with mass \(\mu_1=1\). Set \(m=n+3\). The first \(n+2\) goods correspond to the padded integers; the last good, denoted \(h\), is an additional good.

For every padded good \(j\), set
\[ p_j=v_{1j}=\frac{b_j}{D},\qquad q_j=\frac14. \]
For the additional good, set
\[ p_h=v_{1h}=1,\qquad q_h=\frac12. \]

Every price, value, and supply is strictly positive. Because \(D\) is a power of two, every number used is dyadic. Moreover,
\[ \sum_{j=1}^{n+2}p_j=2,\qquad \sum_{j\in G}p_jq_j=\frac14\cdot2+\frac12=1. \]
Thus the supplies satisfy the necessary aggregate expenditure identity.

For every bundle \(R\), utility equals expenditure:
\[ \sum_{j\in R}v_{1j}=\sum_{j\in R}p_j. \]
Every affordable bundle consequently has utility at most \(1\), and the bundle \(\{h\}\) attains utility \(1\). Hence the optimal utility is always \(1\), and a bundle is optimal exactly when its price is \(1\).

Since every other price is strictly positive, the only affordable bundle containing \(h\) is \(\{h\}\).

3. A partition gives a mass allocation

Suppose the original PARTITION instance is a yes-instance. By the padding lemma, choose \(T\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n+2\}\) with price \(1\).

Let \(T^c=\{1,\ldots,n+2\}\setminus T\). Since the padded goods have total price \(2\), the complement \(T^c\) also has price \(1\).

Define the allocation by
\[ y_{1,\{h\}}=\frac12,\qquad y_{1,T}=\frac14,\qquad y_{1,T^c}=\frac14, \]
and assign zero mass to every other bundle.

These masses sum to \(1\). All three bundles cost exactly \(1\) and attain optimal utility \(1\).

The consumption of \(h\) is \(1/2=q_h\). Each padded good lies in exactly one of \(T\) and \(T^c\), so its consumption is \(1/4=q_j\). Every supply constraint therefore holds with equality, as required because all prices are positive.

4. A mass allocation gives a partition

Conversely, suppose a feasible mass allocation exists.

Because \(p_h>0\), its supply constraint requires
\[ \sum_{S\ni h}y_{1,S}=\frac12. \]
Any bundle assigned positive mass must cost exactly \(1\). The only such bundle containing \(h\) is \(\{h\}\), so
\[ y_{1,\{h\}}=\frac12. \]
The type has total mass \(1\); therefore the total mass assigned to bundles avoiding \(h\) is \(1/2\).

There are finitely many bundles. Consequently, at least one bundle \(T\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n+2\}\) avoiding \(h\) receives positive mass. The budget-exhaustion requirement implies
\[ \sum_{j\in T}p_j=1, \]
or equivalently \(\sum_{j\in T}b_j=D\). The padding lemma now gives a partition of the original integers.

This argument permits arbitrary real allocation masses; no integrality assumption on those masses is used.

5. Encoding size and running times

If the PARTITION input has bit length \(N\), then \(\log D=O(N)\). The constructed instance has \(m=n+3\), \(\tau=1\), and total bit length
\[ L=O\bigl(m(1+\log A)\bigr)=O(N^2). \]
Computing and writing the construction takes polynomial time, with a conservative bound \(O(mL)\) bit operations.

For completeness, the general problem admits an exact algorithm in
\[ 2^{O(m)}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L) \]
time. Enumerate all \(2^m\) bundles, compute each type’s optimal affordable utility using exact rational arithmetic, and retain precisely its optimal bundles costing \(1\). Feasibility is then a rational linear program with at most \(\tau2^m\) variables and \(\tau+m\) type-mass and supply constraints, together with nonnegativity. Its coefficients have polynomial bit length, so polynomial-time rational linear programming gives the stated bound.

The reduction proves the requested \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. In particular, a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\), even restricted to \(\tau=1\), would imply \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).
