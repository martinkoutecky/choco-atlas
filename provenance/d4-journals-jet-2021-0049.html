The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given private submodular utility types \(u_L,u_R:2^{[m]}\to\mathbb{Q}\), each of mass \(\frac12\), and an atomless population of independent cells with one integral copy of every item per cell, output a mass vector \(x=(x_A)_{A\subseteq[m]}\) with \(x_A\ge0\) and \(\sum_A x_A=1\). Define \(U_L(x)=\sum_Ax_Au_L(A)\), \(U_R(x)=\sum_Ax_Au_R([m]\setminus A)\), and require \(U_L(x)\ge\sum_Ax_Au_L([m]\setminus A)\) and \(U_R(x)\ge\sum_Ax_Au_R(A)\), with no feasible \(y\) Pareto-improving both type-average utilities. Determine the worst-case communication needed for the two types to agree on \(x\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Here \(\tau=2\), with types \(L,R\) each of mass \(\frac12\).
Each type privately knows its submodular utility \(u_i:2^{[m]}\to\mathbb Q\), with each value encoded in at most \(L\) bits, where \(L\) is polynomially bounded in \(m\). Values may have either sign; monotonicity and normalization are not required.
The atomless population consists of cells of total measure one, each containing one representative of each type and one integral copy of each item. Items cannot move between cells. Every partition \((A,[m]\setminus A)\) is allowed.
An allocation is \(x_A\ge0\) with \(\sum_{A\subseteq[m]}x_A=1\), where \(x_A\) is the measure of cells assigning \(A\) to type \(L\). Define \(U_L(x)=\sum_Ax_Au_L(A)\) and \(U_R(x)=\sum_Ax_Au_R([m]\setminus A)\).
Require \(U_L(x)\ge\sum_Ax_Au_L([m]\setminus A)\) and \(U_R(x)\ge\sum_Ax_Au_R(A)\). Also require that no allocation \(y\), whether envy-free or not, satisfies \(U_i(y)\ge U_i(x)\) for both types with at least one strict inequality.
Two parties, each knowing only its own utility, must agree on an exact rational allocation, represented by its nonzero coordinates. Use deterministic protocols with unrestricted local computation; measure communicated bits in \(m,\tau,L\), and state any running times in these parameters.

Does this allocation problem admit communication polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or does it require exponentially many communicated bits in \(m\) even when \(\tau=2\)?

Known results: Cole and Tao establish existence and an exponential communication lower bound for two-player envy-free, Pareto-efficient mixed allocations with submodular utilities, together with an exponential communication upper bound ([original source](https://arxiv.org/html/1906.07257)). The formulation above has exactly the same feasible vectors, utility expressions, and private inputs as that mixed-allocation problem; consequently this target is already settled, rather than open.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted Pareto improvement as weak improvement for both types and strict improvement for at least one, rather than requiring both to improve strictly; competitors need not be envy-free.
  • Specified a per-value binary encoding bound L polynomial in m, matching the communication setting rather than measuring complexity against the entire exponential utility table.
  • Allowed signed rational utilities without imposing monotonicity or normalization, since the discrete utility domain imposes neither restriction.
  • Specified deterministic exact protocols and rational output represented by nonzero coordinates to resolve the unspecified protocol and output models.
  • Made each cell contain both type representatives, normalized total cell measure to one, and prohibited transfers between cells, preserving the stated complementary-bundle allocation model.

Result

The problem requires exponentially many communicated bits in \(m\), even when \(\tau=2\) and \(L=O(\log m)\). An exponential communication upper bound also holds, with exact rational output.

Applying the known lower bound

Cole and Tao’s Theorem 3.2 gives an exponential communication lower bound for finding an envy-free, Pareto-efficient mixed allocation between two players with submodular utilities. The paper states a bound of \(\Omega(2^{m/2})\); its hard instances have integer utility values between \(0\) and \(3m/2\), requiring only \(O(\log m)\) bits per value. Theorem 3.1 establishes existence for finite allocation sets closed under swapping players’ bundles. [Cole–Tao, *On the Existence of Pareto Efficient and Envy-Free Allocations*, Sections 3 and 5](https://arxiv.org/html/1906.07257)

Here is the precise identification of models. Write \(A^c=[m]\setminus A\). Identify a pure two-player allocation with the partition \((A,A^c)\), and identify its mixing probability with \(x_A\). Then:

  • The probability simplex is exactly the feasible set \(x_A\geq0\), \(\sum_Ax_A=1\).
  • Expected own-bundle utilities are exactly \(U_L(x)\) and \(U_R(x)\).
  • Expected utilities after exchanging bundles are exactly \(\sum_Ax_Au_L(A^c)\) and \(\sum_Ax_Au_R(A)\). Thus the two envy-free inequalities coincide.
  • Both models test Pareto efficiency against every feasible mixed allocation, including allocations that are not envy-free.
  • Each party has precisely its own utility function as private input.

The cell interpretation introduces no additional restriction: an atomless space can be partitioned into finitely many measurable pieces of prescribed measures \(x_A\), with partition \((A,A^c)\) implemented on the corresponding piece.

Consequently, any deterministic protocol for the stated problem would also solve the cited mixed-allocation problem with identical communication. Requiring exact rational output restricts acceptable outputs and cannot invalidate that lower bound.

Since the hard family has \(\tau=2\) and \(L=O(\log m)\), communication polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) is impossible.

Exact rational solutions with small support

For completeness, the exact-output requirement causes no existence obstruction. I prove that a suitable allocation always exists with at most three nonzero coordinates, each having \(O(L)\)-bit rational probability.

Define
\[ a_A=u_L(A),\qquad b_A=u_R(A^c), \]
and
\[ d_{L,A}=u_L(A)-u_L(A^c),\qquad d_{R,A}=u_R(A^c)-u_R(A). \]

Let \(E\) be the polytope given by the probability-simplex constraints and
\[ \sum_A d_{L,A}x_A\geq0,\qquad \sum_A d_{R,A}x_A\geq0. \]

The pure allocation set is finite and closed under swapping, because swapping replaces \(A\) by \(A^c\). The existence theorem therefore supplies \(x^*\in E\) that is Pareto efficient. Its applicability does not require nonnegative, normalized, or monotone utilities.

A positive supporting objective

Put \(p_L=U_L(x^*)\) and \(p_R=U_R(x^*)\). Consider the linear program
\[ \max_y\ \sum_A(a_A+b_A)y_A \]
subject to
\[ y_A\geq0,\qquad \sum_Ay_A=1,\qquad \sum_Aa_Ay_A\geq p_L,\qquad \sum_Ab_Ay_A\geq p_R. \]

It is feasible and bounded. Pareto efficiency implies that its optimum is \(p_L+p_R\): any larger objective value would give a Pareto improvement.

Its dual is
\[ \min_{t,\alpha,\beta}\ t-\alpha p_L-\beta p_R \]
subject to
\[ \alpha,\beta\geq0,\qquad t\geq(1+\alpha)a_A+(1+\beta)b_A \quad\text{for every }A. \]

By linear-programming strong duality, an optimal dual solution exists. Set \(w_L=1+\alpha>0\) and \(w_R=1+\beta>0\). Equality of the primal and dual optima gives
\[ t=w_Lp_L+w_Rp_R. \]
Thus \(x^*\) maximizes \(w_LU_L+w_RU_R\) over the entire allocation simplex.

Let \(I\) contain precisely the partitions maximizing \(w_La_A+w_Rb_A\). Every allocation supported on \(I\) maximizes this positive weighted objective and is therefore Pareto efficient: a Pareto improvement would strictly increase that objective.

A rational vertex

The polytope
\[ Q=\{x\in E:x_A=0\text{ whenever }A\notin I\} \]
is nonempty because it contains \(x^*\). Its defining coefficients are rational, even if the supporting weights are irrational. Choose a vertex \(z\) of \(Q\).

This is also a vertex of \(E\). Indeed, if \(z\) were a nontrivial convex combination of two points of \(E\), nonnegativity and its zero coordinates outside \(I\) would force both points to lie in \(Q\), contradicting its extremality there.

Let \(s\) be the number of positive coordinates of \(z\), and let \(r\leq2\) be the number of active envy inequalities. If \(s>1+r\), there is a nonzero perturbation on those \(s\) coordinates preserving normalization and all active envy equalities. Sufficiently small perturbations in both directions preserve positivity and the inactive inequalities. This contradicts extremality. Hence
\[ s\leq1+r\leq3. \]

The positive coordinates are determined by an invertible subsystem consisting of normalization and at most two active envy equalities. Its coefficients have \(O(L)\)-bit rational encodings. Cramer’s rule for a matrix of order at most three gives \(O(L)\)-bit rational coordinates.

Thus \(z\) is an exact rational, envy-free, Pareto-efficient allocation whose representation uses \(O(m+L)\) bits.

An exponential upper bound and local running time

The parties exchange their complete utility tables in a public subset order. There are \(2^m\) values per party, so this costs \(O(2^mL)\) communicated bits. Both then perform the same deterministic computation:

  • Enumerate supports of size at most three and choices of active envy equalities.
  • Solve the resulting constant-size rational systems and retain feasible candidates.
  • Test each candidate \(x\) for Pareto efficiency using the linear program above, with thresholds \(U_L(x),U_R(x)\).
  • Output the first candidate passing the test in a fixed enumeration order.

The Pareto test is exact: its optimum exceeds \(U_L(x)+U_R(x)\) exactly when a Pareto improvement exists. The preceding argument guarantees that some enumerated candidate passes.

There are \(O(2^{3m})\) candidate systems. Exact rational linear programming takes time polynomial in its explicitly encoded input size, so the local computation after tabulation takes \(2^{O(m)}\operatorname{poly}(L)\) bit operations. This excludes any unspecified cost of evaluating the private utility functions to obtain their tables.

Therefore, for \(\tau=2\), the worst-case communication is exponential: a lower bound of \(\Omega(2^{m/2})\) on the cited hard family and an upper bound of \(O(2^mL)\). The lower bound persists despite the existence of succinct exact outputs.
