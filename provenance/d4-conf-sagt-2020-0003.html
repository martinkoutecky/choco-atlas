The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), positions \(P\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over approval sets \(A_t\subseteq C\times P\), where \(S(c,p)=\sum_{t:(c,p)\in A_t}\mu_t\), can one compute an injective line-up with no reasonably dissatisfied pair under \(S\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m\) candidates and \(P\) contain \(q\) positions, with \(1\leq q\leq m\).
A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) distinct approval types \(A_t\subseteq C\times P\) with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The support size \(\tau\) counts listed types, not possible types; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Define \(S(c,p)=\sum_{t:(c,p)\in A_t}\mu_t\). A line-up is an injective map \(\pi:P\to C\); write \(\pi_p=\pi(p)\), and let \(\pi(c)\) denote the assigned position of an assigned candidate.
A pair \((c,p)\in C\times P\) is reasonably dissatisfied precisely when \(S(c,p)>S(\pi_p,p)\) and either \(c\notin\pi(P)\), or \(c\in\pi(P)\) and \(S(c,p)>S(c,\pi(c))\). Equal scores do not cause dissatisfaction; any qualifying line-up may be returned.

Is computing a line-up without reasonably dissatisfied pairs solvable in polynomial time in \(m,q,\tau,L\), or is this search problem NP-hard?

For explicitly given rational score matrices, a reasonably satisfying line-up always exists and is computable in polynomial time ([verified source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.04960)).
That result already covers the aggregated rational scores defined here; consequently, the stated continuous question is covered by the known result rather than being an open target.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Require at least one position and at least as many candidates as positions, preserving the intended full injective assignment domain.
  • Interpret distribution as total mass one, with positive binary rational masses on explicitly listed distinct approval types; empty approval sets are allowed.
  • Use the formal reasonable-satisfaction axiom to resolve the prose's erroneous restriction to assigned pairs: potential dissatisfaction is tested over all candidate-position pairs, including unassigned candidates.
  • Use strict score comparisons, so ties do not constitute dissatisfaction and no prescribed tie-breaking is required.
  • Keep candidates and positions indivisible; only the voter society is represented by masses.

Result

The search problem is solvable in polynomial time in \(m,q,\tau,L\). A greedy algorithm always produces a line-up without reasonably dissatisfied pairs.

This is the max-first rule in the [cited paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.04960), Sections 4–5. The following proof establishes correctness directly for the stated support representation, including its bit complexity.

1. Compute exact integer scores

Write each mass as \(\mu_t=a_t/b_t\), where \(a_t,b_t\) are positive integers given in binary. Define \(D=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}b_t\) and \(n_t=a_t(D/b_t)\). Then \(n_t=D\mu_t\) is an integer.

For each pair \((c,p)\), compute
\(W(c,p)=\sum_{t:(c,p)\in A_t}n_t\).
Thus \(W(c,p)=D S(c,p)\). Since \(D\) is positive, every strict comparison or equality between scores is preserved.

The binary length of \(D\) is at most \(1+\sum_t\log_2 b_t\), hence is \(O(L+1)\). Moreover, positivity and normalization give \(\sum_t n_t=D\), so \(0\leq W(c,p)\leq D\). Consequently, all these integers have \(O(L+1)\) bits.

Their computation uses polynomial-time integer arithmetic and a scan of the explicitly listed approval types. No expansion of the masses into individual voters is needed.

2. Greedy construction

Initially, every candidate and every position is available. Repeat the following operation until all positions are filled:

  • Among all pairs consisting of an available candidate and an available position, choose a pair \((c,p)\) maximizing \(W(c,p)\). Break ties arbitrarily.
  • Assign \(c\) to \(p\), then remove both from availability.

Before iteration \(k\), exactly \(k-1\) candidates and positions have been removed. There are therefore \(m-k+1\) available candidates and \(q-k+1\) available positions. For \(1\leq k\leq q\), both quantities are positive because \(m\geq q\).

Thus every iteration is possible, and after exactly \(q\) iterations the assignments define an injective map \(\pi:P\to C\).

3. Proof of reasonable satisfaction

Fix any pair \((c,p)\). Consider the iteration in which position \(p\) receives its final candidate \(\pi_p\).

Case 1: Candidate \(c\) was available immediately before that iteration.

The pair \((c,p)\) was eligible for selection. By maximality of the selected pair,
\(W(\pi_p,p)\geq W(c,p)\).
Therefore \(S(\pi_p,p)\geq S(c,p)\), and the first strict inequality required for dissatisfaction fails.

Case 2: Candidate \(c\) was already unavailable.

Then \(c\) was assigned in an earlier iteration to its final position \(\pi(c)\). Position \(p\) was still available at that earlier iteration, because it was assigned later. Hence \((c,p)\) was eligible when the algorithm selected \((c,\pi(c))\). Maximality gives
\(W(c,\pi(c))\geq W(c,p)\).
Therefore \(S(c,\pi(c))\geq S(c,p)\), and the second strict inequality required for dissatisfaction fails.

These cases exhaust all possibilities. In particular, a candidate unassigned in the final line-up is always covered by Case 1. Thus no pair is reasonably dissatisfied. The proof permits arbitrary tie-breaking.

4. Running time

Once the integer score table is constructed, each iteration scans at most \(mq\) pairs. The construction therefore uses at most \(mq^2\) comparisons of integers with \(O(L+1)\) bits.

Score aggregation and greedy selection both take polynomial time in \(m,q,\tau,L\). This proves existence and polynomial-time computability for every input satisfying the premises.
