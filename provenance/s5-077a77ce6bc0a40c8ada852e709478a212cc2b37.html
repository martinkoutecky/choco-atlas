Theorem 4 — class: algorithmic

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m\) candidates, a rational positional scoring vector \(s\), a polynomial-time backup tie-break \(f\), and a society \(\mu\) supported on at most \(\tau\) complete rankings with binary-rational masses summing to \(1\), compute the winner selected by the MFP refinement of the score rule. Determine whether this can be done in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(A=\{1,\ldots,m\}\), with \(m\ge2\), and let \(\mathcal R\) be the complete strict rankings of \(A\). A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau\) distinct types with positive binary-rational masses summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not \(m!\).
The scoring vector \(s\in\mathbb Q^m\) satisfies \(s_1\ge\cdots\ge s_m\) and \(s_1>s_m\); negative entries are permitted. Let \(D\subseteq A\) be the positional-scoring co-winners using mass-weighted scores.
Fix a deterministic backup mechanism \(f\) that, given the support representation of \(\mu\) and any nonempty \(B\subseteq A\), returns \(f(\mu,B)\in B\) in polynomial time. Let \(L\) be the total binary input length, including rankings, masses, and scores.
Candidate permutations act by relabeling rankings, with \((\sigma\mu)(R)=\mu(\sigma^{-1}R)\). Define \(G_\mu=\{\sigma\in S_A:\sigma\mu=\mu\}\) and \(F_\mu=\{a\in A:\sigma(a)=a\text{ for every }\sigma\in G_\mu\}\).
Fix candidate priority \(1\succ\cdots\succ m\). Order rankings lexicographically by this priority, and compare mass vectors in that coordinate order, favoring the larger mass at the first differing coordinate; absent rankings have mass \(0\).
Define \(\operatorname{MFP}(\mu)=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\sigma\in S_A}^{\mathrm{lex}}\sigma\mu\). For any \(\sigma^*\in\operatorname{MFP}(\mu)\), the MFP refinement outputs \(\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{a\in F_\mu\cap D}\sigma^*(a)\) when \(F_\mu\cap D\ne\varnothing\), and outputs \(f(\mu,D)\) otherwise. The former choice is independent of the selected maximizing permutation.

Can this winner be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\), or is this winner-computation problem NP-hard?

Known results: For explicitly listed finite profiles of complete rankings, MFP tie-breaking is computable in polynomial time with any polynomial-time backup mechanism. That result states a bound for finite profiles, without a classification for the sparse binary-rational mass representation above.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Restored the scoring-vector conditions: weakly decreasing rational entries with strictly different first and last entries; no sign restriction is imposed.
  • Restored the candidate-domain condition m ≥ 2.
  • Fixed candidate priority to 1 through m and specified its lexicographic extensions, including zero coordinates for unsupported rankings.
  • Interpreted the backup mechanism as a fixed polynomial-time algorithm on support-encoded societies and nonempty candidate sets, since an ordered agent profile is unavailable.
  • Interpreted positional scores as mass-weighted scores and included rankings, masses, and scoring-vector entries in the input bit length.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The winner is computable in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\), for every backup mechanism satisfying the stated hypothesis. The proof works directly with the sparse support and exact rational masses.

1. A complete ranking determines a relabeling uniquely.

Write the support as \(\{R_1,\ldots,R_\tau\}\), with masses \(w_i=\mu(R_i)>0\). Represent each ranking as a tuple \(R_i=(r_{i,1},\ldots,r_{i,m})\), listed from most to least preferred.

For any two complete rankings \(R=(r_1,\ldots,r_m)\) and \(T=(t_1,\ldots,t_m)\), there is exactly one candidate permutation \(\pi\) satisfying \(\pi R=T\): it is defined by \(\pi(r_j)=t_j\) for every \(j\). This defines a permutation because both tuples contain every candidate exactly once.

This observation makes both required permutation searches polynomial.

2. Computing an MFP permutation.

Let \(R_0=(1,\ldots,m)\), the first ranking in the prescribed lexicographic coordinate order, and let \(w_{\max}=\max_i w_i\).

For every candidate permutation \(\pi\), the first coordinate of its transformed mass vector is

\(\displaystyle (\pi\mu)(R_0)=\mu(\pi^{-1}R_0)\le w_{\max}.\)

For each support ranking \(R_i\) having mass \(w_{\max}\), define its normalization permutation \(\nu_i\) by

\(\displaystyle \nu_i(r_{i,j})=j\qquad\text{for every }j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}.\)

Then \(\nu_iR_i=R_0\), so \((\nu_i\mu)(R_0)=w_{\max}\). Consequently, every lexicographically maximizing permutation must have first-coordinate mass \(w_{\max}\): a permutation with a smaller first coordinate loses to any such \(\nu_i\), regardless of subsequent coordinates.

If \(\pi\) is a maximizer, therefore, \(\pi^{-1}R_0\) is some support ranking \(R_i\) of mass \(w_{\max}\). Since \(\pi R_i=R_0\), uniqueness from Section 1 gives \(\pi=\nu_i\).

Thus every MFP permutation belongs to the explicitly computable set

\(\displaystyle C=\{\nu_i:w_i=w_{\max}\},\qquad |C|\le\tau.\)

Taking the lexicographically greatest transformed society among these candidates produces a global maximizer. Indeed, every permutation outside \(C\) has a strictly smaller first coordinate.

It remains to compare transformed societies without constructing their mass vectors of length \(m!\). Each transformed society has exactly \(\tau\) supported rankings. Sort these rankings lexicographically, retaining their masses. To compare two societies, merge their sorted support lists:

  • At a ranking appearing in both lists, compare its two masses.
  • At a ranking appearing in only one list, compare its positive mass with zero.
  • Continue until the first unequal coordinate, or until both lists are exhausted.

Every ranking outside the union of the two supports has mass zero in both societies. Therefore, this procedure finds exactly the first differing coordinate in the full mass vectors and implements the required comparison.

3. Computing the stabilizer and its fixed candidates.

Fix the support ranking \(R_1\). For each \(i\) with \(w_i=w_1\), define \(\gamma_i\) as the unique permutation sending \(R_1\) to \(R_i\).

Test whether \(\gamma_i\mu=\mu\) by relabeling all supported rankings, sorting the resulting list, and comparing rankings and masses with the original sorted support. Retain precisely the permutations passing this test.

The retained permutations are exactly \(G_\mu\). Passing the test is its defining condition. Conversely, if \(g\in G_\mu\), then

\(\displaystyle \mu(gR_1)=(g\mu)(gR_1)=\mu(R_1)=w_1>0.\)

Hence \(gR_1=R_i\) for some support ranking of mass \(w_1\). By uniqueness, \(g=\gamma_i\), so the enumeration includes every stabilizer element.

In particular, \(|G_\mu|\le\tau\). Once these permutations are listed, compute

\(\displaystyle F_\mu=\{a\in A:g(a)=a\text{ for every retained permutation }g\}\)

by checking each candidate against the list.

4. Computing the scoring co-winners and refined winner.

Let \(\operatorname{pos}_i(a)\) denote the position of candidate \(a\) in \(R_i\). Compute every score exactly:

\(\displaystyle S(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}w_i\,s_{\operatorname{pos}_i(a)}.\)

Then form the nonempty set

\(\displaystyle D=\{a\in A:S(a)=\max_{b\in A}S(b)\}.\)

Choose the maximizing permutation \(\sigma^*\) found in Section 2.

If \(F_\mu\cap D\) is nonempty, return its unique candidate minimizing \(\sigma^*(a)\). Uniqueness follows because \(\sigma^*\) is a permutation. Otherwise, return \(f(\mu,D)\), which is defined because \(D\) is nonempty.

For completeness, the first branch is independent of which maximizing permutation is selected. If \(\alpha,\beta\in\operatorname{MFP}(\mu)\), their transformed mass vectors equal the same lexicographic maximum, so \(\alpha\mu=\beta\mu\). Applying \(\beta^{-1}\) gives \((\beta^{-1}\alpha)\mu=\mu\), hence \(\beta^{-1}\alpha\in G_\mu\). For every \(a\in F_\mu\), this implies

\(\displaystyle \beta^{-1}\alpha(a)=a,\qquad\text{and therefore }\alpha(a)=\beta(a).\)

Thus all maximizing permutations assign the same labels to candidates in \(F_\mu\).

5. Polynomial bit complexity.

The MFP computation examines at most \(\tau\) transformed supports, each containing \(\tau\) rankings. The stabilizer computation likewise examines at most \(\tau\) transformed supports. Sorting and comparing them requires polynomially many ranking and rational comparisons; a ranking comparison examines at most \(m\) candidate labels. Fixed-candidate checks require at most \(m\tau\) permutation evaluations.

Mass comparisons use exact cross multiplication of binary integers whose lengths are bounded by \(L\), so each comparison has polynomial bit cost.

To verify the same property for scores, write \(w_i=p_i/q_i\) and \(s_j=u_j/v_j\), with positive denominators and possibly negative \(u_j\). Define the common denominator

\(\displaystyle Q=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}q_i\right)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}v_j\right).\)

Its binary length is \(O(L)\), since the input explicitly contains all these denominators. Moreover, every \(q_i v_j\) divides \(Q\). Therefore scores can be compared through the exact integers

\(\displaystyle Q S(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}p_i u_{\operatorname{pos}_i(a)} \frac{Q}{q_i v_{\operatorname{pos}_i(a)}}.\)

Each summand has \(O(L)\) bits, and each sum has \(O(L+\log(\tau+1))\) bits. All required arithmetic consequently has polynomial bit complexity. Negative scoring entries cause no difficulty.

Finally, the backup mechanism receives the original sparse society and \(D\), an input of polynomial size, and runs in polynomial time by hypothesis. This proves the claimed classification without expanding rational masses into voters.

Completeness and strictness of the rankings are essential here, and both are supplied by the task.

2. Restricting the MFP search

The first ranking in the prescribed coordinate order is indeed \(R_0=(1,\ldots,m)\). For every permutation \(\pi\), the action definition gives
\[ (\pi\mu)(R_0)=\mu(\pi^{-1}R_0). \]
This value is either a supported mass or zero, so it is at most \(w_{\max}\).

The support is nonempty because its positive masses sum to one. Thus \(w_{\max}>0\), and normalizing any maximum-mass support ranking attains first-coordinate mass \(w_{\max}\).

Every maximizing permutation must attain that value. Its inverse image of \(R_0\) must therefore be a maximum-mass supported ranking. The uniqueness established in Section 1 then identifies the permutation with that ranking’s normalization.

Consequently, the set \(C\) contains every maximizer and has at most \(\tau\) elements. The stronger statement that every permutation outside \(C\) has a strictly smaller first coordinate is also justified: attaining \(w_{\max}\) already forces membership in \(C\).

3. Comparing sparse transformed societies

The proposed comparison correctly implements the full lexicographic comparison.

Relabeling by a permutation preserves distinctness of rankings and preserves their positive masses, so each transformed support still contains exactly \(\tau\) entries. Merging two sorted support lists visits their union in the prescribed coordinate order. Rankings outside this union have zero mass in both societies and cannot affect the comparison.

At each visited ranking, comparing the listed masses, with zero for an absent entry, therefore detects precisely the first differing coordinate. Exhausting both lists without a difference means the full mass vectors are equal.

No enumeration of the \(m!\) coordinates is required.

4. Enumerating the stabilizer and fixed candidates

The stabilizer enumeration is exhaustive and sound.

Every retained permutation belongs to \(G_\mu\) by the explicit equality test. Conversely, for \(g\in G_\mu\), the displayed identity
\[ \mu(gR_1)=(g\mu)(gR_1)=\mu(R_1)=w_1 \]
follows directly from the action definition and stabilizer membership. Therefore \(gR_1\) is a supported ranking with mass \(w_1\). Uniqueness of the relabeling forces \(g\) to equal the corresponding enumerated \(\gamma_i\).

This establishes both the completeness of the enumeration and the bound \(|G_\mu|\le\tau\). Checking every candidate against every retained permutation computes exactly \(F_\mu\). The identity permutation is included and passes the test, so the procedure also handles the trivial stabilizer correctly.

5. Scores, output, and independence of the maximizer

The scoring formula agrees with the task’s mass-weighted positional scores. Since \(A\) is finite and nonempty, a maximum score exists and \(D\) is nonempty. Negative score entries do not invalidate either statement.

The independence argument is also correct. Lexicographic order on the finite collection of transformed mass vectors has a unique maximum vector. Thus any two maximizing permutations \(\alpha,\beta\) satisfy \(\alpha\mu=\beta\mu\), implying \(\beta^{-1}\alpha\in G_\mu\). Every candidate in \(F_\mu\) is fixed by this permutation, which yields \(\alpha(a)=\beta(a)\). Hence the selected candidate is independent of the maximizing permutation.

Finally, the backup input has polynomial size, and its runtime is polynomial by the stated hypothesis.
