The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite task set \(Q\), type masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), per-unit resource vectors \(b_{tk}\), eligibility sets \(I_t\), per-capita task demands \(d_{qk}\), and rewards \(r_q\), choose \(y_q\in\{0,1\}\) and resource allocations \(x_{tqk}\ge 0\) to maximize \(\sum_{q\in Q}r_qy_q\), subject to \(x_{tqk}=0\) when \(q\notin I_t\), \(\sum_qx_{tqk}\le\mu_tb_{tk}\), \(\sum_tx_{tqk}=d_{qk}y_q\), and \(x_{tqk}\le\mu_t\min\{b_{tk},d_{qk}\}y_q\). Tasks remain indivisible; allocations may split type mass across tasks. Determine the complexity of deciding whether a feasible allocation has reward at least \(K\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(Q\) be a task set of size \(m\ge1\), with \(h\ge1\) resource coordinates. A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\ge1\) types with positive masses \(\mu_t\), each encoded as a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
Each type has per-unit resource holdings \(b_{tk}\ge0\) and an eligibility set \(I_t\subseteq Q\), with \(\bigcup_t I_t=Q\). Each task has per-capita demands \(d_{qk}\ge0\) and reward \(r_q\ge0\). These numbers and the threshold \(K\ge0\) are binary rationals; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Tasks are indivisible and may be completed at most once; resources are divisible, types may contribute to multiple tasks, and unused resources and idle mass are allowed. Choose \(y_q\in\{0,1\}\), with \(\sum_q y_q\ge1\), and real allocations \(x_{tqk}\ge0\) satisfying, for every applicable index,
\(x_{tqk}=0\) if \(q\notin I_t\), \(\sum_q x_{tqk}\le\mu_t b_{tk}\), \(\sum_t x_{tqk}=d_{qk}y_q\), and \(x_{tqk}\le\mu_t\min\{b_{tk},d_{qk}\}y_q\).
The decision problem asks whether such an allocation exists with \(\sum_q r_qy_q\ge K\). State running times in \(m,\tau,h,L\).

Is this continuous task-selection decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

The finite-agent version with integer resource contributions is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, including the restriction to one resource coordinate and universal eligibility ([Zhang et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1613/jair.1.17138)).
No verified result for exactly the normalized mass-allocation problem above was identified.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Specified nonnegative binary-rational holdings, demands, rewards, and threshold; zero resource coordinates are allowed, consistently with the discrete model's examples and experimental domains.
  • Required positive task-set size, support size, and resource dimension, and carried over the discrete requirement that the eligibility sets collectively cover every task.
  • Required at least one completed task to retain the intended requirement of at least one feasible non-idle coalition; the all-idle allocation alone does not qualify.
  • Allowed unused resources and idle mass, corresponding to the discrete idle coalition, while requiring every selected task to meet its full demand.
  • Interpreted allocations as real resource amounts and retained the graded question's normalization and per-type contribution caps exactly.

Result

The decision problem is $\mathrm{NP}$-complete, already for one resource coordinate and universal eligibility. Hardness holds both for $\tau=1$ and for $\tau=2$ with distinct types and masses $\mu_1=\mu_2=1/2$.

The key structural fact is that the normalized caps force every feasible allocation to be proportional to the masses.

1. The continuous allocations are uniquely determined

Lemma. Every feasible solution satisfies
$$ x_{tqk}=\mu_t d_{qk}y_q $$
for every type, task, and resource coordinate.

Proof. The per-task cap gives
$$ 0\le x_{tqk} \le \mu_t\min\{b_{tk},d_{qk}\}y_q \le \mu_t d_{qk}y_q. $$
For fixed $q,k$, define the nonnegative deficits
$$ \delta_t=\mu_t d_{qk}y_q-x_{tqk}. $$
By mass normalization and the demand equality,
$$ \sum_t\delta_t =d_{qk}y_q\sum_t\mu_t-\sum_t x_{tqk} =d_{qk}y_q-d_{qk}y_q =0. $$
A finite sum of nonnegative numbers equals zero only when every summand equals zero. Thus $\delta_t=0$ for every $t$, proving the lemma. $\square$

Two consequences follow.

First, if a selected task $q$ has $d_{qk}>0$ for some $k$, then
$$ x_{tqk}=\mu_t d_{qk}>0 $$
for every type, because every $\mu_t$ is positive. Eligibility therefore requires $q\in I_t$ for every $t$. Tasks whose demands are all zero require no contributions and need not have universal eligibility.

Second, each type’s capacity constraint becomes
$$ \mu_t\sum_q d_{qk}y_q\le \mu_t b_{tk}. $$
Dividing by the positive mass gives
$$ \sum_q d_{qk}y_q\le b_{tk}. $$

2. Exact equivalence to task knapsack

Define
$$ B_k=\min_{1\le t\le\tau}b_{tk} $$
and let
$$ A= \left\{ q\in Q: \bigl(d_{qk}=0\text{ for every }k\bigr) \ \text{or}\ \bigl(q\in I_t\text{ for every }t\bigr) \right\}. $$

Proposition. The original instance is a yes-instance if and only if there exists a nonempty subset $S\subseteq A$ satisfying
$$ \sum_{q\in S}d_{qk}\le B_k \quad\text{for every }k, \qquad \sum_{q\in S}r_q\ge K. $$

Proof of necessity. Let $S=\{q:y_q=1\}$ for a feasible solution. Nonemptiness and the reward inequality are original constraints. The lemma and its eligibility consequence give $S\subseteq A$. Its capacity consequence gives
$$ \sum_{q\in S}d_{qk}\le b_{tk} $$
for every $t,k$, hence the bound by $B_k$.

Proof of sufficiency. Suppose such an $S$ exists. Set $y_q=1$ exactly on $S$ and define
$$ x_{tqk}=\mu_t d_{qk}y_q. $$
These allocations are nonnegative. They obey eligibility: every selected task with a positive demand belongs to every eligibility set, and every task with zero demands receives zero allocation.

Mass normalization gives the required demand equalities:
$$ \sum_t x_{tqk} =d_{qk}y_q\sum_t\mu_t =d_{qk}y_q. $$
The capacity inequalities hold because
$$ \sum_q x_{tqk} =\mu_t\sum_{q\in S}d_{qk} \le\mu_t B_k \le\mu_t b_{tk}. $$

It remains to verify the per-task caps. If $q\in S$, nonnegativity of demands gives
$$ d_{qk}\le\sum_{p\in S}d_{pk}\le B_k\le b_{tk}. $$
Consequently $\min\{b_{tk},d_{qk}\}=d_{qk}$, and the cap holds with equality. If $q\notin S$, both sides of the cap are zero. Thus all original constraints hold. $\square$

This is exactly a multidimensional binary knapsack decision problem on $A$, with the additional requirement that the selected subset be nonempty.

3. NP-hardness with one resource coordinate

We reduce from the classical NP-hard SUBSET SUM problem: given positive binary integers $a_1,\ldots,a_m$ and a positive target $B$, decide whether some subset sums exactly to $B$. This positive-integer formulation and its hardness are stated in the introduction of [Polak, Rohwedder, and Węgrzycki, *Knapsack and Subset Sum with Small Items*](https://arxiv.org/html/2105.04035).

Construct an instance with
$$ Q=\{1,\ldots,m\},\qquad h=1,\qquad \tau=2, $$
and set
$$ \mu_1=\mu_2=\frac12,\qquad b_{11}=B,\qquad b_{21}=B+1,\qquad I_1=I_2=Q. $$
For every task $q$, set
$$ d_{q1}=a_q,\qquad r_q=a_q, \qquad K=B. $$

This satisfies every input hypothesis. The two types are distinct, both masses are positive, and their sum is one. All numerical data are binary rationals. The construction has output bit length linear in the SUBSET SUM input bit length and takes linear time.

Here $A=Q$ and $B_1=B$. By the proposition, the constructed instance is feasible exactly when some nonempty $S\subseteq Q$ satisfies
$$ \sum_{q\in S}a_q\le B \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{q\in S}a_q\ge B. $$
These inequalities are equivalent to
$$ \sum_{q\in S}a_q=B. $$
Since $B>0$, any subset attaining this equality is nonempty. Thus the reduction preserves yes- and no-instances.

Therefore the problem is NP-hard even with $h=1$, fixed support size $\tau=2$, fixed masses, and universal eligibility. The same reduction works with $\tau=1$, $\mu_1=1$, and $b_{11}=B$.

4. NP membership and running times

A certificate consists solely of the $m$ selection bits. The proposition lets a verifier check eligibility, nonemptiness, the $h$ demand inequalities, and the reward threshold using exact rational arithmetic. No real-valued allocation certificate is needed.

For explicit bit-complexity bounds, clear denominators using the product $D$ of all input rational denominators. Its bit length is $O(L)$, because the sum of their encoded lengths is at most $L$. Every scaled quantity and every subset sum has $O(L)$ bits: summation adds at most $O(\log m)$ bits, and $m\le L$ for an explicit task list.

Using ordinary integer arithmetic, a conservative bound for preprocessing and certificate verification is
$$ O(m\tau hL^2) $$
bit operations. This proves membership in $\mathrm{NP}$.

A deterministic exact algorithm preprocesses $A$ and the capacities, then enumerates all nonempty subsets of $A$. Its running time is bounded by
$$ O\!\left(\bigl(m\tau h+2^m mh\bigr)L^2\right), $$
with polynomial space.

Together with the reduction, NP membership proves $\mathrm{NP}$-completeness. In particular, a polynomial-time algorithm in $m,\tau,h,L$ exists if and only if $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$.
