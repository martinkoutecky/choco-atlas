The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(C\), \(k\), and a hidden rational finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over approval ballots \(B\subseteq C\), a learner proposes committees \(W\in\binom{C}{k}\). A rejection returns only an omitted candidate \(c\); it is valid if some \(\ell\in[k]\) has mass at least \(\ell/k\) on ballots approving \(c\) and fewer than \(\ell\) members of \(W\). What is the minimum worst-case number of rejected proposals a deterministic learner needs before acceptance, over candidate sets, hidden distributions, and truthful adaptive oracles?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a finite candidate set, \(m=|C|\), and \(1\le k\le m\). A society is represented by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct approval ballots \(B\subseteq C\) with positive binary rational masses \(\mu(B)\) summing to \(1\). Empty ballots are allowed; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible ballots, and \(L\) is the total encoding bit length.
The distribution is fixed throughout the interaction and hidden. A deterministic learner knows only \(C,k\), and chooses each proposal \(W\in\binom{C}{k}\) from the preceding proposal–response history.
An adaptive truthful oracle rejects \(W\) by returning only \(c\in C\setminus W\) for which some \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) satisfies \(\sum_{B:\,c\in B,\,|B\cap W|<\ell}\mu(B)\ge\ell/k\). It accepts exactly when no such candidate exists; neither the level nor the witnessing ballots are disclosed. Acceptance defines EJR+ in this model.
Let \(R(\mathcal L,\mu,\mathcal O)\) count rejections before first acceptance, with value \(\infty\) if acceptance never occurs, and define \(Q(k)=\sup_{m\ge k}\inf_{\mathcal L}\sup_{\mu}\sup_{\mathcal O}R(\mathcal L,\mu,\mathcal O)\), where the last supremum ranges over truthful adaptive oracles for the fixed distribution.
Learners have unrestricted computation. Any running times stated should be expressed in \(m,\tau,L,k\).

What is the asymptotic order of \(Q(k)\)? In particular, is \(Q(k)\) bounded by a polynomial in \(k\), or is no polynomial rejection bound possible?

For finite electorates, deterministic candidate-only EJR+ learning has an asymptotic lower bound of order \(k^{3/2}\) with refined leading constant approximately \(1.1496\), and an upper bound \(2^{O(k^2(\log k)^2)}\); whether a polynomial rejection bound exists remains open. 2. A sparse realization lemma for rejection histories

A rejection transcript of length $r$ is a sequence
$$ (W_1,c_1),\ldots,(W_r,c_r), $$
where $W_t\in\binom{C}{k}$ and $c_t\in C\setminus W_t$. It is feasible if one fixed distribution makes every prescribed response truthful.

Lemma

Let $r\ge 1$. If a rejection transcript is feasible, even under real-valued masses, then it has a realizing rational distribution $\nu$ satisfying all of the following:

  • Its support size is at most $r$.
  • Every support ballot is contained in

$$ U=\bigcup_{t=1}^r\bigl(W_t\cup\{c_t\}\bigr). $$

  • For some positive integer $n$,

$$ n\le k r^{r/2}, $$
every support mass is a positive integer multiple of $1/n$.

This single distribution realizes the entire transcript.

Proof

For each $t$, choose a witnessing level $\ell_t\in\{1,\ldots,k\}$ from a distribution realizing the transcript. These levels are used only in the proof; they need not be available to the learner.

Intersecting every ballot with $U$, and aggregating identical resulting ballots, preserves each relevant approval and intersection count. Hence we may restrict attention to ballots $B\subseteq U$.

Introduce a nonnegative variable $x_B$ for every such ballot. Define
$$ A_{t,B} = \begin{cases} 1,&c_t\in B\text{ and }|B\cap W_t|<\ell_t,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}, \end{cases} \qquad b_t=\frac{\ell_t}{k}. $$
Thus $A$ is a matrix with entries in $\{0,1\}$.

Consider the linear program
$$ \text{minimize }\sum_{B\subseteq U}x_B \quad\text{subject to}\quad Ax\ge b,\qquad x\ge 0. $$
The projected realizing distribution is feasible and has total mass $1$. Consequently the optimum is at most $1$.

The optimum is attained: it suffices to minimize over the nonempty compact set of feasible vectors with total mass at most $1$. The optimal face is compact and therefore has an extreme point. Such a point is also a vertex of the original feasible polyhedron: any decomposition into feasible points would, by optimality and linearity of the objective, be a decomposition into optimal points.

Choose an optimal vertex $x$, and let $p$ be its number of positive coordinates. Since every $b_t$ is positive, $p\ge 1$.

The active covering constraints, restricted to those $p$ coordinates, have rank $p$. Otherwise there would be a nonzero vector supported on those coordinates and annihilated by all active covering rows. Sufficiently small perturbations in both directions would preserve positivity and every inactive inequality, contradicting that $x$ is a vertex. In particular,
$$ p\le r. $$

Choose $p$ linearly independent active covering rows. Their restrictions give a nonsingular matrix $M\in\{0,1\}^{p\times p}$, and the positive coordinates satisfy
$$ Mx_{\mathrm{pos}} = \frac{1}{k} \begin{pmatrix} \ell_{i_1}\\ \vdots\\ \ell_{i_p} \end{pmatrix}. $$
Cramer’s rule shows that all positive coordinates are integer multiples of
$$ \frac{1}{n}, \qquad n=k|\det M|. $$
Each row of $M$ has Euclidean norm at most $\sqrt p$. Hadamard’s determinant inequality therefore gives
$$ n \le k p^{p/2} \le k r^{r/2}. $$

Write $s=\sum_B x_B$, so $0<s\le 1$. Increase any one positive coordinate by $1-s$. Because all entries of $A$ are nonnegative, this preserves every covering inequality. It preserves the support and makes the total mass exactly $1$. Since $s$ is also an integer multiple of $1/n$, the resulting masses retain the same common denominator.

Remove zero coordinates. The resulting distribution $\nu$ has positive masses, at most $r$ distinct support ballots, and makes every prescribed response truthful. $\square$

Encoding consequence

Using ballot bit vectors and binary integer encodings, the realizing distribution can be encoded with
$$ L = O\!\left( rm+r\log k+r^2\log(r+1) \right). $$
Indeed, each of at most $r$ ballots uses $m$ bits, and each mass needs
$$ O\!\left(\log k+r\log(r+1)\right) $$
bits.

Thus any feasible polynomial-length rejection history already has a realizing instance whose support size and encoding length are polynomial in $m,k$.

The lemma preserves the prescribed rejections. It does not assert that all responses on other committees are preserved.

3. One electorate size captures every finite rejection horizon

Let $q_{\mathbb Q}(m,k)$ be the minimax rejection value for rational distributions on a fixed candidate set of size $m$. Let $q_n(m,k)$ be the analogous value for a finite electorate of size $n$, where the deterministic candidate-only learner knows $n$.

For $r\ge 1$, define
$$ D_r=\left\lfloor r^{r/2}\right\rfloor, \qquad n_r=k\,\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,D_r). $$

Proposition

For every $m\ge k$ and every $r\ge 1$,
$$ q_{\mathbb Q}(m,k)\ge r \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q_{n_r}(m,k)\ge r. $$

In particular, $n_r$ is independent of $m$.

Proof

First, a distribution with masses that are integer multiples of $1/n$ is represented exactly by $n$ voters: assign $n\mu(B)$ voters ballot $B$. For every committee, candidate, and level,
$$ \left|\left\{i: c\in A_i,\ |A_i\cap W|<\ell \right\}\right| = n\sum_{B:\,c\in B,\ |B\cap W|<\ell}\mu(B). $$
Therefore its qualifying mass reaches $\ell/k$ exactly when the qualifying voter count reaches $\ell n/k$. This is also equivalent to the existence of a witness group of that size: the entire qualifying set is such a group whenever its size reaches the threshold.

Conversely, compressing a finite electorate into distinct ballots and relative frequencies gives an allowed rational distribution.

Fix any deterministic history-to-committee map. Suppose it admits a truthful prefix of $r$ rejections under some rational distribution. By the lemma, that prefix has a realizing distribution with common denominator
$$ n=k|\det M|, \qquad 1\le|\det M|\le D_r. $$
Hence $n$ divides $n_r$. Represent the distribution by $n$ voters and replicate every voter $n_r/n$ times. Replication preserves all violation inequalities.

The resulting fixed electorate of size $n_r$ realizes the prescribed prefix. Determinism ensures that the same responses reproduce the same committees. Complete the oracle on other histories by accepting precisely when no violation exists and otherwise choosing any available candidate.

The converse follows by compressing an electorate of size $n_r$. Thus, for every deterministic map, the possibility of $r$ consecutive rejections is identical in the two models.

At fixed $n_r$, knowing that number merely selects a history-to-committee map; the class of possible maps remains the same. Taking the minimax quantifiers proves the equivalence. $\square$

Corollary: exact minimax equivalence

For every fixed $m,k$,
$$ q_{\mathbb Q}(m,k)=\sup_{n\ge 1}q_n(m,k), $$
and consequently
$$ Q(k)=\sup_{\substack{m\ge k\\n\ge 1}}q_n(m,k). $$

To justify the first equality, compression gives
$$ q_n(m,k)\le q_{\mathbb Q}(m,k) $$
for every $n$. Conversely, every integer lower-bound horizon attained by $q_{\mathbb Q}(m,k)$ is attained at $n_r$, by the proposition. This proves equality, including the possibility of an infinite value.

The same argument shows that allowing arbitrary real masses would leave the minimax value unchanged: the sparse realization lemma applies to every finite real-feasible rejection prefix.

5. Exact rejection complexity for laminar supports

A ballot family is laminar if, for any two ballots $B,D$, either
$$ B\subseteq D,\qquad D\subseteq B, \qquad\text{or}\qquad B\cap D=\varnothing. $$

Theorem

If the supremum over distributions is restricted to laminar support families, the resulting minimax rejection complexity is exactly $k$.

Upper bound

Use permanent retention: maintain the set $S$ of returned candidates and always propose a committee containing $S$, filling unused seats by a fixed ordering.

We prove that this strategy incurs at most $k$ rejections.

Suppose a prefix of $T$ proposals is rejected. Write its responses as $c_t$ and choose witnessing levels $\ell_t$. Let
$$ G_t= \left\{ B: c_t\in B,\ |B\cap W_t|<\ell_t \right\}, $$
where $B$ ranges over support ballots. Truthfulness gives
$$ \mu(G_t)\ge\frac{\ell_t}{k}. $$

All ballots in $G_t$ contain $c_t$. Laminarity therefore makes them a chain under inclusion. Since the support is finite and $G_t$ is nonempty, it has a largest ballot $F_t$. Moreover,
$$ |F_t\cap W_t|<\ell_t. $$

For $s<t$, suppose $G_s\cap G_t$ is nonempty. A ballot in that intersection approves $c_s$ and is contained in $F_t$, so $c_s\in F_t$. Retention also gives $c_s\in W_t$.

Returned candidates are pairwise distinct, because every later committee retains every earlier response. Consequently,
$$ \left|\{s<t:G_s\cap G_t\ne\varnothing\}\right| \le |F_t\cap W_t| \le\ell_t-1. $$

Now repeatedly select the largest-index remaining group $G_t$, and remove it together with all remaining groups that intersect it. Each step removes at most $\ell_t$ groups. The selected groups are pairwise disjoint as subsets of the ballot support.

If $I$ is the set of selected indices, then
$$ T \le\sum_{t\in I}\ell_t \le k\sum_{t\in I}\mu(G_t) \le k. $$
The middle inequality uses the witness quotas; the last uses disjointness and total mass $1$.

Thus retention remains feasible through $k$ rejections. Once its retained set fills the committee, another rejection would yield $T=k+1$, contradicting the proved bound. Acceptance occurs after at most $k$ rejections.

Lower bound

Fix any deterministic learner with $m=k+1$. Simulate its first $k$ proposals by responding in round $t$ with the unique candidate
$$ c_t\in C\setminus W_t. $$
After the simulation, choose a $k$-element set $K$ containing all returned candidates. This is possible because at most $k$ distinct candidates have been returned.

Put all mass on the single ballot $K$. This support is laminar. For every simulated round,
$$ c_t\in K\setminus W_t, \qquad |K\cap W_t|<k. $$
Hence $c_t$ is truthful at level $\ell=k$: its qualifying mass is $1=k/k$.

Under this one fixed distribution, an oracle prescribing those responses on the simulated prefix reproduces all $k$ rejections. Extend it truthfully elsewhere. The distribution is fixed throughout the actual interaction; constructing it after the simulation is permitted because the learner is deterministic.

This proves the lower bound $k$, and hence the theorem. $\square$
