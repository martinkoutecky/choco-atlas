The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(n=2^k\) candidates \(V\), a target \(K\), and a distribution \(\mu\) with binary-rational masses over \(\tau\) strict rankings of \(V\), orient each pair by its strict majority under \(\mu\). Let \(A\) be the candidates that beat \(K\), and \(B\) those that beat \(K\)'s opponents but lose to \(K\). If \(K\) is a king and \(B=H\mathbin{\dot\cup}I\mathbin{\dot\cup}J\) with \(|H|<|A|\), \(\mathrm{in}_A(i)\ge\log n\) for every \(i\in I\), and \(\mathrm{out}(j)\le|A|\) for every \(j\in J\), find a balanced seeding in which \(K\) wins. What is the running time in \(n,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the mass bit length?

Conclusion

Finding a winning seeding is solvable in deterministic polynomial time in \(n,\tau,L\). The distribution input can be converted exactly into an explicit pairwise tournament in polynomial time, after which the cited constructive theorem applies.

For \(n=1\), the strict condition \(|H|<|A|\) is impossible because both sets are empty. Thus there are no promised inputs of that size; the single-candidate bracket would otherwise be trivial. Below, consider \(n\ge 2\).

1. Computing the tournament exactly

Write the supplied rankings as \(\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_\tau\), with respective masses \(w_1,\ldots,w_\tau\). For distinct candidates \(u,v\), define

\(s(u,v)=\sum_{\{t:\pi_t\text{ ranks }u\text{ above }v\}}w_t\).

By definition, \(u\succ v\) precisely when \(s(u,v)>1/2\).

We use the ordinary binary encoding of rational numbers: write \(w_t=p_t/q_t\), where the positive integers \(p_t,q_t\) are encoded in binary. A finite binary expansion of a dyadic rational can also be converted to this representation with only a linear increase in encoding length.

Define the positive integer common denominator and integer weights by

\(D=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}q_t,\qquad W_t=p_t(D/q_t)\).

These quantities are computable by exact integer arithmetic. Their bit lengths are polynomially bounded:

  • The bit length of \(D\) is at most \(1+\sum_t\operatorname{bitlength}(q_t)\), hence \(O(L+1)\).
  • Since the masses are positive and sum to \(1\), each mass is at most \(1\). Consequently \(0<W_t\le D\).
  • The normalization gives \(\sum_t W_t=D\), so every partial sum of the integer weights is at most \(D\).

For each unordered candidate pair, compute

\(S(u,v)=\sum_{\{t:\pi_t\text{ ranks }u\text{ above }v\}}W_t\).

Then the exact comparison is

\(u\succ v\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2S(u,v)>D\).

All integers used in this comparison have \(O(L+1)\) bits. Thus even a majority margin that is extremely small causes no precision problem.

Precompute the position of every candidate in every supplied ranking. Each pair then requires at most \(\tau\) position comparisons and integer additions. There are \(\binom n2\) pairs, so constructing the entire adjacency matrix takes polynomial time in \(n,\tau,L\), including the cost of integer arithmetic.

Because each ranking is strict and the masses sum to \(1\), we have \(S(u,v)+S(v,u)=D\). The no-tie promise therefore ensures that exactly one direction is selected for each pair. The resulting directed graph \(T=(V,E)\), with \((u,v)\in E\) exactly when \(u\succ v\), is an explicit tournament.

2. Applying the constructive tournament theorem

Theorem 2.1 of Kim, Suksompong, and Vassilevska Williams states that, in a tournament on a power-of-two number of vertices, a king \(K\) has a polynomial-time computable winning balanced seeding whenever its inneighborhood admits the supplied three-part partition satisfying the inequalities in this question. The paper assumes balanced brackets and power-of-two sizes throughout. [Theorem 2.1 and its constructive proof](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~mpkim/pubs/siam2017-tournaments.pdf).

Every hypothesis applies to our constructed tournament:

  • Its vertex set has size \(n=2^k\).
  • Its edges agree exactly with the relation \(\succ\), so the promised king property holds in \(T\).
  • Its outneighborhood of \(K\) is exactly \(A\), and its inneighborhood is exactly \(B\).
  • The supplied disjoint partition \(B=H\mathbin{\dot\cup}I\mathbin{\dot\cup}J\) therefore remains a partition of that inneighborhood.
  • The quantities \(\mathrm{in}_A(i)\) and \(\mathrm{out}(j)\) count precisely the corresponding edges of \(T\). Hence all three promised inequalities hold unchanged, with \(\log_2 n=k\).

The theorem consequently supplies a balanced winning seeding for \(K\), computable in polynomial time from the explicit tournament.

3. Correctness and total running time

Run the theorem’s algorithm on \(T\) and return its seeding.

For any fixed seeding, the winner under \(T\) equals the winner under the original society. Indeed, each first-round match has the same two candidates and the same outcome by construction of \(E\). Inductively, every later match receives the same winners from its two child matches and again has the same outcome. Thus the seeding returned by the theorem makes \(K\) win under the original distribution.

Tournament construction takes polynomial time in \(n,\tau,L\), and the subsequent seeding algorithm takes polynomial time in \(n\). Their composition is therefore polynomial in the requested parameters \(m=n,\tau,L\).

The explicit support representation is sufficient: the algorithm processes only the \(\tau\) supplied rankings and never enumerates all possible rankings. No separate structural theorem for binary-rational distributions is needed.
