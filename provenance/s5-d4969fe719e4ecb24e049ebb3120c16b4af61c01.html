The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given colleges, resources and their regions, rational per-capita seat and resource caps, and a finite set of student types with rational masses, complete preferences over college-resource bundles and unmatched status, and a priority tier at each college, compute a feasible allocation \(x_{t,c,r}\) and unmatched mass. Require that no positive mass can improve to a desired contract by displacing lower-priority mass at that college while preserving feasibility when the desired resource is the incumbent’s resource or the empty resource; and require every feasible positive-mass improvement without displacement to be dominated, meaning that carrying it out creates a direct-envy block that was absent before.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty set of \(m\) colleges and \(R\) a set of \(k\) nonempty resources; put \(R_0=R\cup\{r_0\}\), where \(r_0\) is the empty resource. Each \(r\in R\) has a nonempty region \(C_r\subseteq C\); regions may overlap. Seat caps \(q_c\) and resource caps \(q_r\) are positive binary rationals; the empty resource is unrestricted.
A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(p_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t p_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type has strict complete preferences over \((C\times R_0)\cup\{\varnothing\}\) and a priority tier at each college. Only strictly higher tiers justify displacement; equal tiers do not. Whenever \((c,r)\succ_t\varnothing\) with \(r\in R\), require \((c,r)\succ_t(c,r_0)\).
An allocation consists of nonnegative masses \(x_{t,c,r}\) and unmatched masses \(x_{t,\varnothing}\), satisfying \(x_{t,\varnothing}+\sum_{c,r}x_{t,c,r}=p_t\), \(\sum_{t,r}x_{t,c,r}\le q_c\), and \(\sum_{t,c}x_{t,c,r}\le q_r\) for \(r\in R\). Set \(x_{t,c,r}=0\) for \(c\notin C_r\) or \(\varnothing\succ_t(c,r)\).
Write \(x_{t,a}\) for mass at a bundle or unmatched status \(a\). A waste improvement \((t,a;c,r;\varepsilon)\) transfers \(0<\varepsilon\le x_{t,a}\) from \(a\) to \((c,r)\succ_t a\), without displacement, yielding a feasible allocation \(y\); this includes resource changes within a college.
A label \((t,a;c,r)\) direct-envy-blocks \(x\) if some positive transfer from \(a\) to \((c,r)\succ_t a\) is feasible after moving an equal positive mass of a strictly lower-priority type from \((c,s)\) to unmatched status, with \(r\in\{s,r_0\}\). It waste-blocks \(x\) if some waste improvement with that label is feasible.
A waste improvement yielding \(y\) is dominated if there is a label \((u,b;c,s)\), at the same destination college and with \(x_{u,b}>0\) and \(y_{u,b}>0\), that neither waste-blocks nor direct-envy-blocks \(x\), but direct-envy-blocks \(y\).
Call \(x\) direct-envy stable if no label direct-envy-blocks it and every feasible waste improvement of every positive magnitude is dominated.

Is computing a rational direct-envy stable allocation, or correctly reporting that none exists, solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,k,L\), or is this problem NP-hard?

For finite students with strict college priorities, three cutoff mechanisms always compute a direct-envy stable matching in \(O(N^3)\) time, where \(N=\max\{n,m,k+1\}\). [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.14690v2#S6.SS1)
No result settling this precise divisible-mass problem was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized total student mass to one, interpreting the supplied per-capita caps relative to this normalization.
  • Carried over positive caps, nonempty resource regions, individual rationality, strict student preferences, and the preference restriction for acceptable nonempty resources; allowed overlapping regions and unrestricted empty-resource allocation.
  • Interpreted priority tiers as permitting displacement only across strictly ordered tiers, with no tie-breaking within a tier.
  • Defined improvement relative to a positive source subpopulation, allowing arbitrary splitting, releasing its previous bundle, and moving displaced incumbent mass to unmatched status.
  • Included resource changes within the same college among waste improvements.
  • Restored the dominance requirements that the witnessing desired contract is at the same destination college and was neither waste-blocking nor direct-envy-blocking before the move.
  • Quantified dominance over every feasible positive transfer magnitude and required the witnessing source subpopulation to remain present before and after the move.
  • Requested exact rational output and allowed reporting nonexistence, since continuous existence is not supplied by the discrete result.

Resolution

The problem is polynomial-time solvable in $m,\tau,k,L$. Every permitted instance admits a rational direct-envy stable allocation.

The algorithm uses at most $\tau m(k+1)+1$ linear-program stages. It permits conflicting college priorities and equal priority tiers.

1. Admissible alternatives and demanding mass

For each type $t$, let $\mathcal A_t$ consist of unmatched status and all bundles that are regionally available and preferred by $t$ to unmatched status. We retain the given preference order on $\mathcal A_t$.

All allocation coordinates outside these sets are zero. A label with an inadmissible destination cannot block, because its proposed transfer cannot be feasible.

Write $u\triangleright_c t$ when $u$ has a strictly higher priority tier than $t$ at college $c$.

For an admissible bundle $d\in\mathcal A_t$, define the demanding mass

$$ D_{t,d}(x)=\sum_{\substack{a\in\mathcal A_t\\d\succ_t a}}x_{t,a}. $$

For a bundle $d\notin\mathcal A_t$, define $D_{t,d}(x)=0$. Thus $D_{t,d}(x)>0$ exactly when some positive source mass of type $t$ wants the admissible destination $d$.

Lemma 1: Direct envy depends only on positive coordinates

A label $(t,a;c,r)$ direct-envy-blocks a feasible allocation $x$ if and only if its source is positive, its destination is admissible and preferred to its source, and there exist $v,s$ satisfying

$$ t\triangleright_c v,\qquad x_{v,c,s}>0,\qquad r\in\{s,r_0\}. $$

Proof. Necessity follows from the definition.

For sufficiency, choose

$$ 0<\varepsilon\leq\min\{x_{t,a},x_{v,c,s}\}. $$

Move this mass of $t$ to $(c,r)$ and the same mass of $v$ from $(c,s)$ to unmatched status. Strict priority implies $t\neq v$, so these coordinate changes preserve nonnegativity and each type’s total mass.

Addition and displacement cancel at college $c$. If $r=s$ is nonempty, they also cancel the destination-resource load. If $r=r_0$, the destination adds no constrained resource load. Removing the original source only decreases other loads. The resulting allocation is feasible. $\square$

2. Safe destinations

For a feasible allocation $x$, define $\mathcal E(x)$ to be the set of admissible type-bundle coordinates $(t,c,r)$ satisfying the following conditions:

  • If $r=r_0$, then every $u\triangleright_c t$ satisfies

$$ D_{u,(c,r_0)}(x)=0. $$

  • If $r\in R$, then every $u\triangleright_c t$ satisfies

$$ D_{u,(c,r)}(x)=D_{u,(c,r_0)}(x)=0. $$

Call these the safe destinations at $x$. Unmatched coordinates remain available separately.

By Lemma 1, a positive holder at $(t,c,r)$ can be directly envied precisely through one of the demands excluded by these conditions. Consequently,

$$ x\text{ is direct-envy-free} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_{t,c,r}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ (t,c,r)\in\mathcal E(x). $$

We will also use the following monotonicity property. If feasible allocations $x,z$ satisfy

$$ D_{t,d}(z)\leq D_{t,d}(x) $$

for every type and admissible bundle, then

$$ \mathcal E(x)\subseteq\mathcal E(z). $$

Indeed, every demanding mass that is zero at $x$ remains zero at $z$, since demanding masses are nonnegative.

Waste improvements decrease demanding masses

Suppose a waste improvement transfers mass $\varepsilon$ of type $t$ from $a$ to $d\succ_t a$, producing $y$. For every admissible bundle $e$,

$$ D_{t,e}(y)-D_{t,e}(x) = \varepsilon \left( \mathbf 1\{e\succ_t d\} - \mathbf 1\{e\succ_t a\} \right) \leq 0. $$

The inequality holds because $e\succ_t d\succ_t a$ implies $e\succ_t a$. Other types’ demanding masses are unchanged.

Thus waste improvements never increase any demanding mass.

3. Domination and the local stability criterion

We first prove an exact domination test, including its independence of the improvement magnitude.

Lemma 2: Exact domination test

Let $x$ be direct-envy-free. Fix any feasible waste improvement $(t,a;c,r;\varepsilon)$, producing $y$.

This improvement is dominated if and only if there exist $u,b,s$ such that

$$ u\triangleright_c t,\qquad s\in\{r,r_0\}, $$

$$ x_{u,b}>0,\qquad (c,s)\in\mathcal A_u,\qquad (c,s)\succ_u b, $$

and the label $(u,b;c,s)$ does not waste-block $x$.

Proof. Suppose a label qualifies as a dominating label. Its source is positive in both $x$ and $y$.

The improvement increases only the coordinate $(t,c,r)$. Every other coordinate stays unchanged or decreases. If the dominating label could directly displace a holder at any other coordinate in $y$, that holder was already positive in $x$. Together with the label’s positive source in $x$, Lemma 1 would give direct envy at $x$, a contradiction.

The newly envied holder must therefore be type $t$ at $(c,r)$. Lemma 1 gives $u\triangleright_c t$ and $s\in\{r,r_0\}$. The other stated conditions follow from the definition of domination.

Conversely, suppose the stated conditions hold. Because $u\triangleright_c t$, the types differ, so the improvement leaves $x_{u,b}$ unchanged and positive. Also $y_{t,c,r}>0$. Lemma 1 therefore makes $(u,b;c,s)$ direct-envy-blocking at $y$.

This label does not waste-block $x$ by assumption, and it does not direct-envy-block $x$ because $x$ is direct-envy-free. Hence it dominates the improvement.

The criterion contains no reference to $\varepsilon$, and the argument applies to every feasible positive magnitude. $\square$

Proposition 3: Stability is absence of waste to safe destinations

A feasible allocation $x$ is direct-envy stable if and only if:

  • $x$ is direct-envy-free; and
  • no label with destination coordinate in $\mathcal E(x)$ waste-blocks $x$.

Proof. First suppose $x$ is stable. A waste improvement to a destination $(t,c,r)\in\mathcal E(x)$ has no possible witness in Lemma 2: safety excludes every strictly higher-priority demanding source at the compatible destinations $(c,r)$ and $(c,r_0)$. Such an improvement would be undominated, contradicting stability.

Conversely, suppose both stated conditions hold. Consider an arbitrary feasible waste improvement with label $(t,a;c,r)$.

Set

$$ K_r= \begin{cases} \{r_0\},&r=r_0,\\ \{r,r_0\},&r\in R, \end{cases} $$

and consider all positive demanding sources at these compatible destinations:

$$ \mathcal Q= \left\{ (u,b,s): s\in K_r,\ (c,s)\in\mathcal A_u,\ x_{u,b}>0,\ (c,s)\succ_u b \right\}. $$

This finite set is nonempty because it contains $(t,a,r)$. Choose its highest represented priority tier at college $c$.

If $t$ belongs to that highest tier, no strictly higher-priority type demands either compatible destination. Thus $(t,c,r)\in\mathcal E(x)$, contrary to condition 2 and the chosen waste improvement.

The highest represented tier must therefore be strictly above $t$. Choose $(u,b,s)\in\mathcal Q$ in that tier.

Its destination coordinate $(u,c,s)$ is safe:

  • If $s=r_0$, any strictly higher-priority demanding source for $(c,r_0)$ would contradict maximality of the chosen tier.
  • If $s$ is nonempty, then $s=r$. Any strictly higher-priority demanding source for either $(c,r)$ or $(c,r_0)$ would likewise contradict maximality.

Condition 2 consequently says that $(u,b;c,s)$ does not waste-block $x$. Lemma 2 shows that it dominates the original improvement, for every feasible positive magnitude.

Every feasible waste improvement is therefore dominated, and $x$ is stable. $\square$

Equal tiers cause no difficulty: only a tier strictly above the improving type can supply the dominating witness.

4. One linear-program stage

Choose integer values representing each type’s preference order:

$$ v_t(a)= \left| \left\{ b\in\mathcal A_t:a\succ_t b \right\} \right|. $$

Thus

$$ d\succ_t a\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_t(d)>v_t(a). $$

Define the linear objective

$$ \Phi(z)=\sum_t\sum_{a\in\mathcal A_t}v_t(a)z_{t,a}. $$

Given a feasible, direct-envy-free allocation $x$, let $\mathcal P(x)$ consist of allocations $z$ satisfying:

  • All original allocation feasibility constraints.
  • The support restrictions

$$ z_{t,c,r}=0 \qquad\text{whenever }(t,c,r)\notin\mathcal E(x). $$

  • The preference-tail inequalities

$$ D_{t,d}(z)\leq D_{t,d}(x) $$

for every type $t$ and admissible bundle $d$.

These are linear constraints. Moreover, $x\in\mathcal P(x)$: its positive bundle coordinates are safe, and its demanding masses satisfy the inequalities with equality.

The polytope is bounded, because nonnegativity and the type-mass equations give

$$ 0\leq z_{t,a}\leq p_t\leq 1. $$

Compute an optimal vertex $z$ of

$$ \max_{z\in\mathcal P(x)}\Phi(z). $$

Lemma 4: Properties of the stage output

The resulting allocation $z$ satisfies:

  • $\mathcal E(x)\subseteq\mathcal E(z)$.
  • $z$ is direct-envy-free.
  • No label with destination in $\mathcal E(x)$ waste-blocks $z$.

Proof. The first property follows from the demanding-mass inequalities and the monotonicity established in Section 2.

The support restrictions place every positive bundle coordinate of $z$ in $\mathcal E(x)$, hence in $\mathcal E(z)$. The direct-envy characterization in Section 2 proves the second property.

For the third property, suppose a feasible waste improvement from $z$ has destination in $\mathcal E(x)$ and produces $y$. Its destination is allowed by the support restrictions, and removing source mass cannot violate them.

Section 2 gives, for every demanding-mass constraint,

$$ D_{t,d}(y)\leq D_{t,d}(z)\leq D_{t,d}(x). $$

Thus $y\in\mathcal P(x)$. But if the improvement moves type $u$ from $a$ to $e\succ_u a$ with magnitude $\varepsilon$, then

$$ \Phi(y)-\Phi(z) = \varepsilon\bigl(v_u(e)-v_u(a)\bigr)>0. $$

This contradicts optimality of $z$. $\square$

5. Algorithm and termination

Start with the all-unmatched allocation

$$ x^0_{t,\varnothing}=p_t, \qquad x^0_{t,c,r}=0. $$

It is feasible and direct-envy-free.

At stage $j$:

  • Compute $\mathcal E(x^j)$.
  • Compute an optimal vertex $z$ of the linear program from Section 4.
  • Compute $\mathcal E(z)$.
  • If $\mathcal E(z)=\mathcal E(x^j)$, output $z$. Otherwise set $x^{j+1}=z$ and repeat.

Lemma 4 preserves feasibility and absence of direct envy at every stage. It also gives

$$ \mathcal E(x^j)\subseteq\mathcal E(z). $$

Every nonterminal stage therefore strictly enlarges the safe-destination set.

There are at most $\tau m(k+1)$ admissible type-bundle coordinates. Hence the algorithm terminates after at most

$$ \tau m(k+1)+1 $$

linear-program stages.

At a terminal stage, Lemma 4 says that no waste improvement at $z$ targets $\mathcal E(x^j)$. Since the stopping condition gives

$$ \mathcal E(z)=\mathcal E(x^j), $$

no waste improvement targets a safe destination at $z$. Proposition 3 proves that the output is direct-envy stable.

This establishes existence for every permitted instance.

6. Rational encoding lengths and polynomial time

It remains to control the encoding lengths across successive linear programs.

Let

$$ B=m(k+1)+1, $$

and let $n$ be the number of retained allocation coordinates, including unmatched coordinates. Then

$$ n\leq\tau B. $$

Each stage has $n$ variables and polynomially many constraints. Every constraint-matrix entry belongs to

$$ \{-1,0,1\}. $$

Indeed, the constraints are nonnegativity, type-mass equations, capacity inequalities, coordinate-zero restrictions, and sums defining demanding masses. The objective coefficients are integers between $0$ and $B-1$.

Let $H_0$ be the product of the denominators of all input masses and capacities. Its binary encoding length is $O(L)$, and the initial allocation has coordinates that are integer multiples of $1/H_0$.

Suppose the current allocation’s coordinates are integer multiples of $1/H_j$, where $H_0$ divides $H_j$. Every right-hand side of the stage linear program is then an integer multiple of $1/H_j$: this includes the input masses and capacities and the sums $D_{t,d}(x^j)$.

At an optimal vertex, choose $n$ linearly independent active constraints. Their coefficient matrix $A_j$ is an integer matrix with entries of absolute value at most $1$. Cramer’s rule shows that every output coordinate is an integer multiple of

$$ \frac{1}{H_j|\det A_j|}. $$

Set

$$ H_{j+1}=H_j|\det A_j|. $$

Hadamard’s inequality gives

$$ 1\leq|\det A_j|\leq n^{n/2}. $$

There are at most $n+1$ stages. Consequently, throughout the algorithm,

$$ \log_2 H_j = O\!\left(L+n^2\log(n+1)\right). $$

Every allocation coordinate lies between $0$ and $1$, so its numerator and denominator have polynomial encoding length. The demanding-mass right-hand sides are sums of these coordinates and have the same common-denominator bound. Input capacity right-hand sides also retain polynomial encoding length.

We use the standard polynomial-time theorem for rational linear programming: a nonempty bounded rational linear program can be optimized exactly, with an optimal rational vertex computed in time polynomial in its encoding length. Its hypotheses hold at every stage by Section 4, and the preceding bounds show that every stage’s encoding length is polynomial in $m,\tau,k,L$.

Computing demanding masses, safe-destination sets, and the stopping comparison uses exact rational arithmetic on polynomial-length numbers. Since there are polynomially many stages, the entire algorithm runs in polynomial time.

7. Conclusion

The algorithm computes a rational direct-envy stable allocation for every permitted instance in polynomial time in $m,\tau,k,L$. Nonexistence never occurs under the stated hypotheses.
