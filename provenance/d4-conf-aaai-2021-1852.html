The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\), preferred candidate \(p\), fixed \(q\ge3\), and rational masses \(\mu_r\) on a support \(R\) of \(\tau\) rankings, choose \(y_{r,S}\ge0\), the mass of ranking type \(r\) assigned to final top-\(q\) set \(S\in\binom{C}{q}\), to minimize \(\sum_{r\in R,S\in\binom{C}{q}}\kappa_r(S)y_{r,S}\), subject to \(\sum_{S\in\binom{C}{q}}y_{r,S}=\mu_r\) for every \(r\), and \(\sum_{r,S:p\in S}y_{r,S}\ge\sum_{r,S:c\in S}y_{r,S}\) for every \(c\ne p\). Here \(\kappa_r(S)=|\{c\notin S:\operatorname{rank}_r(c)<\max_{s\in S}\operatorname{rank}_r(s)\}|\), the minimum number of demotion operations per unit of mass needed to make \(S\) the approved set. Each unit of mass receives an ordinary integral ranking after its demotions, and \(p\) must be a co-winner.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Fix an integer \(q\ge3\). Let \(C\) contain \(m>q\) candidates, with preferred candidate \(p\in C\), and use \(q\)-approval with the co-winner convention.
A society is given by its support: a list \(R\) of \(\tau\) strict rankings of \(C\), each with positive mass \(\mu_r\) encoded as a binary rational, where \(\sum_{r\in R}\mu_r=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings.
A demotion moves any candidate down a positive integer number of positions in the current ranking, preserving the relative order of the other candidates; operations are sequential and each costs one per unit of affected mass, independently of distance. Mass may be split, but every portion receives an ordinary integral ranking.
For \(S\in\binom{C}{q}\), define \(\kappa_r(S)=|\{c\in C\setminus S:\operatorname{rank}_r(c)<\max_{s\in S}\operatorname{rank}_r(s)\}|\), the minimum operation count needed to make \(S\) the final approved set; ranks start at \(1\).
Choose real masses \(y_{r,S}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{S\in\binom{C}{q}}y_{r,S}=\mu_r\), and define \(s(c)=\sum_{r\in R}\sum_{S\in\binom{C}{q}:c\in S}y_{r,S}\). Require \(s(p)\ge s(c)\) for every \(c\ne p\); the cost is \(\sum_{r\in R}\sum_{S\in\binom{C}{q}}\kappa_r(S)y_{r,S}\).
Given a nonnegative binary-rational budget \(B\), decide whether such an assignment has cost at most \(B\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length, including rankings, masses, and budget; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\), with \(q\) fixed.

Is this continuous budget decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard, for each fixed \(q\ge3\)?

For a finite list of indivisible voters, the corresponding unit-cost budget problem is NP-hard for every fixed \(q\ge3\). [Zagoury et al., 2021](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/download/16723/16530)
The discrete optimization problem also admits a polynomial-time \(\min(q,m-q)\)-factor approximation; no result settling the exact mass problem above was identified in the literature search. [Zagoury et al., 2026, full text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/408611892_Efficiently_Negative_Complexity_and_Approximations_of_Targeted_Negative_Campaigning)

Result

For every fixed \(q\ge 3\), the continuous budget decision problem belongs to \(\mathrm{P}\).

The decisive fact is that each support ranking has only \(\binom{m}{q}=O(m^q)\) possible final approved sets. Thus the stated mass formulation is an explicitly constructible rational linear program of polynomial size. Below, we verify both its operational meaning and its bit complexity.

1. The cost of realizing an approved set

Fix a ranking \(r\) and a set \(S\in\binom{C}{q}\). Let \(s_*\) be the member of \(S\) ranked last in \(r\), and put \(a=\operatorname{rank}_r(s_*)\).

Exactly \(q\) members of \(S\) occur in the first \(a\) positions. Consequently,

\(\kappa_r(S)=a-q.\)

We prove that this is precisely the minimum number of demotions needed to make \(S\) the approved set.

Lower bound. Consider any candidate \(c\notin S\) initially preceding \(s_*\). In the final ranking, every member of \(S\) must precede every candidate outside \(S\), so \(s_*\) must precede \(c\).

A demotion of a third candidate preserves the relative order of \(c\) and \(s_*\). Demoting \(s_*\) cannot change their order from \(c\) preceding \(s_*\) to \(s_*\) preceding \(c\). Therefore \(c\) itself must be demoted at least once. There are \(\kappa_r(S)\) such candidates, and each operation demotes only one candidate. At least \(\kappa_r(S)\) operations are necessary.

Upper bound. Demote each candidate outside \(S\) that initially precedes \(s_*\) to the bottom, once each. Until such a candidate is demoted, it still precedes \(s_*\), because operations on other candidates preserve their relative order. Every prescribed demotion therefore moves its candidate a positive distance.

After these operations, precisely the members of \(S\) precede or equal \(s_*\). All other candidates follow \(s_*\), so the first \(q\) positions contain exactly \(S\). This uses \(\kappa_r(S)\) operations.

Hence the minimum is \(\kappa_r(S)\), and

\(0\le \kappa_r(S)\le m-q.\)

2. An exact linear feasibility formulation

Let \(\mathcal S=\binom{C}{q}\). Introduce one real variable \(y_{r,S}\) for every \(r\in R\) and \(S\in\mathcal S\). Impose the following constraints:

  • Nonnegativity: \(y_{r,S}\ge 0\) for every \(r,S\).
  • Conservation of each support mass: \(\sum_{S\in\mathcal S}y_{r,S}=\mu_r\) for every \(r\in R\).
  • Co-winner inequalities: \(\sum_{r\in R}\sum_{S\in\mathcal S}\bigl(\mathbf 1_{\{c\in S\}}-\mathbf 1_{\{p\in S\}}\bigr)y_{r,S}\le 0\) for every \(c\ne p\).
  • Budget: \(\sum_{r\in R}\sum_{S\in\mathcal S}\kappa_r(S)y_{r,S}\le B\).

Here \(\mathbf 1_{\{c\in S\}}\) equals \(1\) if \(c\in S\), and \(0\) otherwise. The co-winner inequalities are exactly \(s(c)\le s(p)\).

This system is equivalent to the allowed manipulation problem.

Indeed, any campaign induces \(y_{r,S}\) by aggregating the portions originating from \(r\) whose final approved set is \(S\). Section 1 shows that its actual cost is at least the budget expression above. Thus every successful campaign within budget yields a feasible assignment.

Conversely, given a feasible assignment, split the mass \(\mu_r\) into the finitely many portions \(y_{r,S}\). On each positive portion, perform the demotion sequence from Section 1. Every portion receives an ordinary strict ranking, and its cost is exactly \(\kappa_r(S)y_{r,S}\). The resulting scores satisfy the co-winner inequalities and the total cost is at most \(B\).

No integrality condition on the variables is needed, because mass splitting is explicitly permitted.

3. Size and exact running time

Write \(H=\binom{m}{q}\) and \(N=\tau H\). The system has:

  • \(N\) nonnegative variables;
  • \(\tau\) conservation equalities;
  • \(m-1\) co-winner inequalities;
  • one budget inequality.

Replacing each equality by two inequalities gives \(2\tau+m\) rows, apart from variable nonnegativity.

All matrix coefficients are integers. Conservation and score coefficients belong to \(\{-1,0,1\}\), while budget coefficients lie between \(0\) and \(m-q\). Thus each matrix coefficient requires \(O(\log(m+1))\) bits. The rational right-hand sides are the input masses, the budget, their negatives where needed, and zeros.

A dense encoding therefore has bit length at most

\(K=O\!\left(L+(\tau+m)\tau\binom{m}{q}\log(m+1)\right).\)

It can be constructed in polynomial time: compute inverse ranks for each input ranking, enumerate all \(q\)-element subsets, and use \(\kappa_r(S)=\max_{s\in S}\operatorname{rank}_r(s)-q\).

We apply the standard polynomial-time rational linear feasibility theorem: feasibility over the reals of an explicitly given finite system of non-strict rational linear inequalities can be decided in time polynomial in its binary encoding length. This is the polynomial-time linear programming theorem originating with [Khachiyan](https://www.mathnet.ru/eng/dan42319).

Its hypotheses hold here: the system is finite and explicit, all coefficients and right-hand sides are rational, and every inequality is non-strict. In particular, it decides feasibility exactly, including instances where the minimum cost equals \(B\); numerical approximation is unnecessary.

The resulting bit running time is

\(\left[L+(\tau+m)\tau\binom{m}{q}\log(m+1)\right]^{O(1)},\)

and hence, for fixed \(q\),

\(\left[L+(\tau+m)\tau m^q\log(m+1)\right]^{O(1)}.\)

Because the rankings and support are explicitly listed, \(m\) and \(\tau\) are bounded by the input length. This running time is therefore polynomial in \(L\).

4. Why discrete hardness does not transfer

For an indivisible voter, exactly one final approved set must be selected. Encoding that restriction requires discrete choices. In the present problem, a support mass may be divided among multiple final approved sets, and every such division has the exact operational realization proved above.

Thus the continuous formulation itself is a polynomial-size linear program for fixed \(q\). Its polynomial-time solvability does not require an integrality theorem or contradict the stated hardness result for indivisible voters.
