The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(k\) public projects, budget \(B\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) over a finite type set \(T\), and non-satiating scalar-separable utilities \(U_t(x)=\sum_j u_{tj}f_j(x_j)\), compute a feasible allocation \(x\) satisfying \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\frac{u_{tj}f'_j(x_j)}{\sum_\ell u_{t\ell}x_\ell f'_\ell(x_\ell)}\le 1/B\) for every project \(j\), with equality whenever \(x_j>0\), and output the resulting allocation as a core outcome against all blocking submasses.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(T\) be a finite set of types with rational masses \(\mu_t\geq0\) summing to \(1\). An allocation is \(x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^k\) with \(\sum_jx_j\leq B\), and \(U_t(x)=\sum_j u_{tj}f_j(x_j)\), where \(u_{tj}\geq0\) and every \(f_j\) is differentiable, strictly increasing, concave, and has \(x f_j'(x)\) nondecreasing with value \(0\) at \(x=0\).
A blocking submass is \(s\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}^T\) with \(s_t\leq\mu_t\): writing \(a=\sum_ts_t\), it blocks \(x\) if some \(y\geq0\) with \(\sum_jy_j\leq aB\) satisfies \(U_t(y)>U_t(x)\) for every \(t\) with \(s_t>0\). A continuous core outcome admits no blocking submass.

Is computing an allocation \(x\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t\frac{u_{tj}f_j'(x_j)}{\sum_\ell u_{t\ell}x_\ell f_\ell'(x_\ell)}\leq1/B\) for every \(j\), with equality when \(x_j>0\), and which is a continuous core outcome, in \(\mathrm{P}\) or NP-hard?

For a finite list of agents with the same non-satiating scalar-separable utilities, the discrete Lindahl equilibrium, hence a core allocation, is computable by convex programming.

Prover output (gpt-6-astra / medium, Bolzano prover prompt)

The distribution-over-types formulation admits a convex program whose exact optimizers yield the displayed conditions and exclude every positive-mass blocking submass. Rational masses enter directly as weights; expanding them into individual agents is unnecessary.

This establishes a structural extension of the finite-agent convex-programming result. It does not, under the input specification given, establish an unconditional \(\mathrm{P}\) classification: representations of the functions and of exact real outputs are unspecified.

Necessary conventions. Literally, the stated definition allows \(s=0\), which blocks every allocation: choose \(y=0\), and the improvement condition is vacuous. Below, blocking therefore requires \(a>0\).

Also assume \(B>0\), discard zero-mass types from the displayed sums, and assume \(\sum_j u_{tj}>0\) for every remaining type. The last assumption expresses non-satiation and is necessary for the denominators: an all-zero utility vector gives an undefined quotient at every allocation.

Under these explicit conventions, the following result is fully proved.

Proposition. There is a convex minimization problem with \(k\) variables and one weighted logarithmic term per type such that:

  • An optimizer exists.
  • Every optimizer yields an allocation satisfying all the displayed inequalities and equalities.
  • Every allocation satisfying those conditions is a continuous core outcome, where blocking submasses may have arbitrary real coordinates.

The proof also handles flat intervals of \(x f_j'(x)\).

Proof: construction of the scalar convex costs. For each coordinate, replace \(f_j\) beyond \(B\) by its tangent extension:

\( \widetilde f_j(z)=f_j(z)\quad\text{for }0\leq z\leq B,\qquad \widetilde f_j(z)=f_j(B)+f_j'(B)(z-B)\quad\text{for }z>B. \)

This preserves differentiability, concavity and strict increase. It also preserves the assumption that \(z\widetilde f_j'(z)\) is nondecreasing: beyond \(B\), that expression is \(z f_j'(B)\).

The derivative \(f_j'(z)\) is positive at every finite \(z\). Indeed, monotonicity gives a nonnegative derivative; if it vanished anywhere, concavity would force the function to be constant thereafter, contradicting strict increase. Thus, in particular, \(f_j'(B)>0\).

Write \(h_j(z)=z\widetilde f_j'(z)\). This function is continuous and nondecreasing, satisfies \(h_j(0)=0\), is positive for \(z>0\), and tends to infinity. Continuity follows from continuity of the derivative of a differentiable concave function; at the origin, the derivative is interpreted as the finite right derivative.

For \(q>0\), the level set \(h_j^{-1}(\{q\})\) is therefore a nonempty compact interval. Denote its endpoints by

\( \alpha_j(q)=\min\{z:h_j(z)=q\},\qquad \beta_j(q)=\max\{z:h_j(z)=q\}. \)

Define

\( C_j(q)=\int_0^q\frac{\alpha_j(v)}{v}\,dv\quad(q\geq0). \)

The integrand has the finite right limit \(1/f_j'(0)\) at zero. To see this, use

\( \frac{\alpha_j(v)}{v} =\frac{1}{\widetilde f_j'(\alpha_j(v))}, \)

and observe that \(\alpha_j(v)\to0\) as \(v\downarrow0\).

The function \(\alpha_j\) is nondecreasing, while \(\widetilde f_j'\) is nonincreasing. Consequently, the integrand is nondecreasing, so \(C_j\) is convex. Moreover,

\( \frac{q}{f_j'(0)}\leq C_j(q)\leq\frac{q}{f_j'(B)}. \)

We will use its one-sided derivatives:

\( C'_{j,-}(q)=\frac{\alpha_j(q)}q,\qquad C'_{j,+}(q)=\frac{\beta_j(q)}q\quad(q>0),\qquad C'_{j,+}(0)=\frac1{f_j'(0)}. \)

Here is why flat intervals cause precisely this derivative interval. As \(v\uparrow q\), continuity and monotonicity of \(h_j\) imply \(\alpha_j(v)\to\alpha_j(q)\). As \(v\downarrow q\) from above, they imply \(\alpha_j(v)\to\beta_j(q)\). For example, in the second limit, every \(\alpha_j(v)\) exceeds \(\beta_j(q)\); its limiting value lies in the level set at \(q\), so it must equal that level set’s largest element. Taking the corresponding one-sided limits of the integrand gives the asserted derivatives.

Proof: the weighted convex program and existence. For \(q\geq0\), put \(D_t(q)=\sum_j u_{tj}q_j\). Consider

\( \displaystyle \operatorname{maximize}_{q\geq0}\; F(q)=\sum_t\mu_t\log D_t(q)-\frac1B\sum_j C_j(q), \)

with \(F(q)=-\infty\) when some \(D_t(q)=0\). Equivalently, minimize the convex function \(-F\).

All logarithmic arguments are positive when every coordinate of \(q\) is positive, because every type has some positive coefficient. Hence the objective is finite somewhere.

An optimizer exists. To verify boundedness of maximizing sequences, let

\( R=\sum_jq_j,\qquad M=\max_{t,j}u_{tj}>0,\qquad c=\min_j\frac1{f_j'(0)}>0. \)

Whenever \(F(q)\) is finite, \(D_t(q)\leq MR\), and therefore

\( F(q)\leq\log(MR)-\frac cB R. \)

This tends to \(-\infty\) as \(R\to\infty\). Thus a maximizing sequence can be chosen bounded. It has a convergent subsequence. No \(D_t\) can tend to zero along that subsequence: its positive-weight logarithm would tend to \(-\infty\), while all other terms are bounded above, contradicting the finite lower bound on the sequence’s objective values. The limit consequently has all \(D_t>0\), and continuity there proves attainment.

Fix an optimizer \(q^*\), and abbreviate

\( D_t=D_t(q^*),\qquad g_j=\sum_t\mu_t\frac{u_{tj}}{D_t}. \)

These are finite. Coordinatewise optimality and the one-sided derivative formulas imply

\( \frac{\alpha_j(q_j^*)}{q_j^*}\leq B g_j \leq\frac{\beta_j(q_j^*)}{q_j^*}\quad\text{if }q_j^*>0, \)

and

\( B g_j\leq\frac1{f_j'(0)}\quad\text{if }q_j^*=0. \)

For completeness, at a positive coordinate, the derivative of \(F\) from the left must be nonnegative and its derivative from the right nonpositive. At a zero coordinate, its right derivative must be nonpositive. Substitution gives exactly these inequalities.

Proof: recovering the allocation. Define

\( x_j=Bq_j^*g_j. \)

If \(q_j^*>0\), the preceding inequalities give

\( \alpha_j(q_j^*)\leq x_j\leq\beta_j(q_j^*), \)

so \(h_j(x_j)=q_j^*\). If \(q_j^*=0\), then \(x_j=0\), and the same identity holds.

The recovered allocation spends exactly the available budget:

\( \displaystyle \sum_jx_j =B\sum_jq_j^*\sum_t\mu_t\frac{u_{tj}}{D_t} =B\sum_t\mu_t\frac{\sum_j u_{tj}q_j^*}{D_t} =B. \)

In particular, every \(x_j\leq B\), so the tangent extensions agree with the original functions at all recovered coordinates. Consequently,

\( q_j^*=x_jf_j'(x_j),\qquad D_t=\sum_j u_{tj}x_jf_j'(x_j)>0. \)

For \(x_j>0\), both \(q_j^*>0\) and \(x_j=Bq_j^*g_j\), hence

\( f_j'(x_j)g_j=\frac{q_j^*}{x_j}g_j=\frac1B. \)

For \(x_j=0\), necessarily \(q_j^*=0\), and coordinatewise optimality gives \(f_j'(0)g_j\leq1/B\).

These are exactly the required conditions:

\( \displaystyle \sum_t\mu_t \frac{u_{tj}f_j'(x_j)} {\sum_\ell u_{t\ell}x_\ell f_\ell'(x_\ell)} \leq\frac1B, \)

with equality whenever \(x_j>0\).

Proof: exclusion of arbitrary blocking submasses. This part applies to any feasible allocation satisfying the displayed conditions with positive denominators, independently of how it was obtained.

Define a nonnegative price vector for each type by

\( p_{tj}=\frac{B u_{tj}f_j'(x_j)} {\sum_\ell u_{t\ell}x_\ell f_\ell'(x_\ell)}. \)

Then

\( \sum_jp_{tj}x_j=B\quad\text{for every }t,\qquad \sum_t\mu_tp_{tj}\leq1\quad\text{for every }j. \)

Suppose a positive-mass submass \(s\) blocks through \(y\). Concavity gives, for every participating type,

\( 0<U_t(y)-U_t(x) \leq\sum_j u_{tj}f_j'(x_j)(y_j-x_j). \)

Multiplying by the positive normalization defining \(p_t\), we obtain \(p_t\cdot y>p_t\cdot x=B\). Since \(a=\sum_ts_t>0\),

\( \displaystyle aB <\sum_ts_t(p_t\cdot y) =\sum_jy_j\sum_ts_tp_{tj} \leq\sum_jy_j\sum_t\mu_tp_{tj} \leq\sum_jy_j \leq aB. \)

The first weak inequality uses \(s_t\leq\mu_t\) and nonnegative prices. The contradiction proves the continuous-core assertion. No rationality assumption on the blocking submass was used. This completes the proposition.

What this establishes computationally. The program has \(k\) variables, \(|T|\) logarithmic terms, and \(k\) scalar convex costs. The masses appear only as the coefficients \(\mu_t\). Thus their denominators do not force population expansion. The distribution-over-types issue is resolved at the level of an exact convex formulation and continuous-core certification.

This is consistent with the finite-agent convex-programming result described by [Fain, Goel and Munagala](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.03474); the weighted formulation and the blocking-submass argument above are proved independently here.

A formal polynomial-time claim still needs more than convexity. The question permits arbitrary differentiable functions and arbitrary real utility coefficients without specifying their finite encoding or oracle access. Nor does it specify an output representation or approximation criterion.

Even simple formulas can require irrational exact allocations. For example, take one type of mass \(1\), coefficients \(u_{11}=u_{12}=1\), budget \(B=4\), and

\( f_1(z)=\log(1+z),\qquad f_2(z)=\sqrt{1+z}. \)

Both satisfy the assumptions, including monotonicity of \(z f_j'(z)\). Any allocation satisfying the requested conditions spends the whole budget: multiply each coordinate condition by \(x_j\) and sum to obtain \(\sum_jx_j/B=1\). Neither boundary allocation satisfies the conditions, because its unused coordinate has the larger marginal utility. Therefore both coordinates are positive and their marginal utilities are equal:

\( \frac1{1+x_1}=\frac1{2\sqrt{1+x_2}},\qquad x_1+x_2=4. \)

Solving gives

\( x_1=-3+2\sqrt7,\qquad x_2=7-2\sqrt7. \)

Thus even rational formula parameters do not make exact rational output possible. This example does not prove hardness; it demonstrates why the computational model must be specified.

The remaining issue is therefore a bit-complexity theorem for a specified representation and accuracy requirement. The weighted convex formulation and its protection against all positive-mass blocking submasses are established.
