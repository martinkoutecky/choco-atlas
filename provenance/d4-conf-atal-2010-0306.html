The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), rational costs \(c_{tj}\), per-agent workload bounds, per-capita job-supply bounds, and rational \(\alpha,\epsilon>0\), let each type \(t\) distribute its mass among feasible integral bundles \(B\), with \(x_{tB}\ge 0\) and \(\sum_B x_{tB}=\mu_t\). Each bundle has cost \(c_t(B)=\sum_j c_{tj}B_j\), and the resulting cost distribution is \(\nu_x=\sum_{t,B}x_{tB}\delta_{c_t(B)}\). With \(\widehat\Phi(\nu_x)=\frac{\pi}{2}\int_0^1\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}(1-u)\right)Q^\downarrow_{\nu_x}(u)\,du\), distinguish the promised cases \(\operatorname{OPT}\le\alpha\) and \(\operatorname{OPT}\ge\alpha+\epsilon\), where \(\operatorname{OPT}=\min_x\widehat\Phi(\nu_x)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge1\) jobs. Type \(t\) has nonnegative rational costs \(c_{tj}\) and integer workload bounds \(0\le\ell_t\le u_t\le m\). Job \(j\) has rational per-capita supply bounds \(0\le a_j\le b_j\le1\).
The feasible bundles for type \(t\) are \(\mathcal B_t=\{B\in\{0,1\}^m:\ell_t\le\sum_jB_j\le u_t\}\), specified implicitly by these bounds. Each agent receives at most one copy of each job.
An allocation consists of real masses \(x_{tB}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{B\in\mathcal B_t}x_{tB}=\mu_t\) and \(a_j\le\sum_t\sum_{B\in\mathcal B_t}x_{tB}B_j\le b_j\) for every job. Assume a feasible allocation exists.
Set \(c_t(B)=\sum_jc_{tj}B_j\) and \(\nu_x=\sum_t\sum_{B\in\mathcal B_t}x_{tB}\delta_{c_t(B)}\). Let \(Q^\downarrow_{\nu_x}\) be its decreasing quantile function; choices at atom boundaries do not affect the integral.
Define \(\widehat\Phi(\nu_x)=\frac{\pi}{2}\int_0^1\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}(1-u)\right)Q^\downarrow_{\nu_x}(u)\,du\) and \(\operatorname{OPT}=\min_x\widehat\Phi(\nu_x)\). The input includes binary-rational \(\alpha,\epsilon>0\), and \(L\) is its total bit length.

Is distinguishing the promised cases \(\operatorname{OPT}\le\alpha\) and \(\operatorname{OPT}\ge\alpha+\epsilon\) solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this promise problem NP-hard?

For finite agents with the corresponding binary assignments and count bounds, deciding whether \(\sum_{k=1}^n\sin\!\left(\frac{(n+1-k)\pi}{2n+1}\right)y_{(k)}\le\alpha\), where individual costs \(y_{(k)}\) are sorted decreasingly, is reported NP-complete by [Golden and Perny](https://borisgolden.com/research/AAMAS10.pdf).
No result settling the exact continuous promise problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive type masses to sum to one, as required by the quantile integral over the unit interval.
  • Made integral bundles binary job-incidence vectors, preserving the discrete restriction of at most one copy of each job per agent.
  • Specified integer workload bounds between zero and the number of jobs, and nonnegative rational additive costs.
  • Interpreted per-capita job-supply bounds as lower and upper bounds on the total mass assigned each job, with both bounds in the unit interval.
  • Specified bundles implicitly through workload bounds rather than including an explicit bundle list in the input.
  • Allowed real allocation masses and promised feasibility, so the minimum is defined.
  • Treated the positive rational threshold and gap as binary-encoded input and included them in the total bit length.

Result

The promise problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\text{-hard}\), even with one supported type, integer costs, no workload restrictions, every job’s supply fixed to \(1/2\), and the fixed promise gap \(\epsilon=1/4\).

The proof applies directly to allocations with arbitrary real masses.

1. A quantitative bound for the objective

Let \(Y\) be a nonnegative random variable with finite support, distribution \(\nu\), and survival function \(S(z)=\Pr(Y>z)\). Write \(\kappa=\pi/2\). Since \(\sin(\kappa(1-u))=\cos(\kappa u)\), the objective is

\(\displaystyle \widehat\Phi(\nu)=\int_0^1\kappa\cos(\kappa u)Q^\downarrow_\nu(u)\,du.\)

For each \(z\), the set where \(Q^\downarrow_\nu(u)>z\) is, up to endpoints, the interval of length \(S(z)\) beginning at zero. Applying Tonelli’s theorem to the nonnegative integrand gives

\(\displaystyle \widehat\Phi(\nu)=\int_0^\infty\int_0^{S(z)}\kappa\cos(\kappa u)\,du\,dz=\int_0^\infty\sin(\kappa S(z))\,dz.\)

The usual tail formula also gives \(\mathbb EY=\int_0^\infty S(z)\,dz\). Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \widehat\Phi(\nu)-\mathbb EY=\int_0^\infty h(S(z))\,dz,\qquad h(p)=\sin(\pi p/2)-p.\)

The function \(h\) is concave on \([0,1]\), because

\(\displaystyle h''(p)=-\frac{\pi^2}{4}\sin(\pi p/2)\le0.\)

Furthermore, \(h(0)=h(1)=0\) and \(h(1/2)=(\sqrt2-1)/2\). Concavity places \(h\) above the chords connecting these three points, so

\(\displaystyle h(p)\ge(\sqrt2-1)\min\{p,1-p\}\qquad(0\le p\le1).\)

In particular, \(\widehat\Phi(\nu)\ge\mathbb EY\).

Choose a median \(q\) belonging to the support of \(Y\). Such a median exists: take the smallest support value whose cumulative probability is at least \(1/2\). It satisfies both \(\Pr(Y<q)\le1/2\) and \(\Pr(Y>q)\le1/2\). Thus \(S(z)\ge1/2\) for \(z<q\), and \(S(z)\le1/2\) for \(z\ge q\). Therefore,

\(\displaystyle \int_0^\infty\min\{S(z),1-S(z)\}\,dz =\int_0^q(1-S(z))\,dz+\int_q^\infty S(z)\,dz =\mathbb E|Y-q|.\)

The last equality follows by integrating the probabilities defining \((q-Y)_+\) and \((Y-q)_+\); atom boundaries have no effect. We have proved

\(\displaystyle \boxed{\widehat\Phi(\nu)-\mathbb EY\ge(\sqrt2-1)\mathbb E|Y-q|.}\)

In particular, suppose \(Y\) is integer-valued, \(\mathbb EY=T\) is an integer, and \(T\) does not belong to its support. The chosen median \(q\) is then an integer different from \(T\). By the triangle inequality for expectations,

\(\displaystyle \mathbb E|Y-q|\ge|\mathbb E(Y-q)|=|T-q|\ge1.\)

Hence

\(\displaystyle \boxed{\widehat\Phi(\nu)\ge T+\sqrt2-1.}\)

This bound holds for arbitrary real probabilities on the support.

2. Construction from PARTITION

We reduce from PARTITION on positive integers encoded in binary, a classical \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete problem. See [Karp, *Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems*](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/Karp.pdf).

Let the source instance be positive integers \(d_1,\ldots,d_m\), and define \(T=\sum_{j=1}^m d_j\). Its question is whether there exists a subset \(I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\) with

\(\displaystyle 2\sum_{j\in I}d_j=T.\)

Construct the society as follows:

  • One supported type, with \(\mu_1=1\).
  • Costs \(c_{1j}=2d_j\).
  • Workload bounds \(\ell_1=0\) and \(u_1=m\).
  • Supply bounds \(a_j=b_j=1/2\) for every job.
  • Threshold \(\alpha=T\) and promise gap \(\epsilon=1/4\).

All these data satisfy the required hypotheses. In particular, feasibility is guaranteed: assign mass \(1/2\) to the empty bundle and mass \(1/2\) to the full bundle.

For any feasible allocation \(x\), let \(Y\) denote its random bundle cost. Since the sole type has mass one, the allocation masses are probabilities. The exact supply constraints imply

\(\displaystyle \mathbb EY =\sum_Bx_{1B}\sum_j2d_jB_j =\sum_j2d_j\sum_Bx_{1B}B_j =\sum_j2d_j\cdot\frac12 =T.\)

Thus every feasible allocation has the same mean cost.

3. Correctness of the reduction

A partition gives the low promised case

Suppose \(I\) satisfies \(2\sum_{j\in I}d_j=T\). Both its bundle and the complementary bundle have cost \(T\).

Assign mass \(1/2\) to each of these two bundles. Every job appears in exactly one of them, so every supply constraint is satisfied. The resulting cost distribution is \(\delta_T\), and therefore

\(\displaystyle \widehat\Phi(\delta_T) =T\int_0^1\kappa\cos(\kappa u)\,du=T.\)

Every feasible allocation has objective at least its mean \(T\), by Section 1. Consequently,

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{OPT}=T=\alpha.\)

Absence of a partition gives the high promised case

Suppose the PARTITION instance has no solution. A bundle \(B\) has cost

\(\displaystyle c_1(B)=2\sum_jd_jB_j.\)

This is an integer, and it equals \(T\) precisely when the selected jobs form a solution of the source instance. Hence every bundle cost differs from \(T\).

For every feasible allocation, \(Y\) is therefore integer-valued, has mean \(T\), and has no support value equal to \(T\). The quantitative bound in Section 1 yields

\(\displaystyle \widehat\Phi(\nu_x)\ge T+\sqrt2-1.\)

Since \(\sqrt2-1>1/4\), it follows that

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{OPT}\ge T+\sqrt2-1>T+\frac14=\alpha+\epsilon.\)

The argument covers every feasible allocation, including those using arbitrary real masses.

4. Encoding and complexity

The construction uses \(m\) jobs and support size \(\tau=1\). Doubling the source integers increases their bit lengths by at most one, and the bit length of \(T\) is polynomial in the source input length. All other numerical data have constant bit length. The reduction is therefore computable in polynomial time and produces an instance of polynomial total bit length \(L\).

Every source instance maps into one of the two promised cases, with the correct answer. Thus an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) for the stated promise problem would decide PARTITION in polynomial time.

This proves the requested \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness, even for the fixed gap \(\epsilon=1/4\).
