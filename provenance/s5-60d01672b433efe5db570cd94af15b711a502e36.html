The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given an arborescence hypergraph \(H=(T,E)\) on \(\tau\) types, strict preferences \(\succ_t\) over incident contracts, and positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), find contract masses \(x_e\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_{e\ni t}x_e\le\mu_t\) for every type \(t\), and for every contract \(e\in E\), some \(t\in e\) satisfies \(\sum_{f\ni t,\ f\succeq_t e}x_f=\mu_t\). Is there an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|E|\), and the input bit length \(L\) that finds such a stable allocation?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\ge 1\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
The input includes a rooted tree directed away from its root, whose arcs are identified with \(T\), and an explicit hypergraph \(H=(T,E)\) whose contracts are nonempty directed paths in this tree. Put \(m=|E|\); let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Each type \(t\) has a strict total order \(\succ_t\) over \(\delta(t)=\{e\in E:t\in e\}\). The singleton \(\{t\}\) belongs to \(E\) and is ranked last; \(f\succeq_t e\) means \(f\succ_t e\) or \(f=e\).
A stable allocation is \(x\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^{E}\) satisfying \(\sum_{f\in\delta(t)}x_f\le\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and, for every \(e\in E\), admitting some \(t\in e\) with \(\sum_{f\in\delta(t):f\succeq_t e}x_f=\mu_t\).

Is finding a stable allocation solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this search problem \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

With unit capacities and integral allocations, a stable matching can be found in \(O(\tau m)\) time by Scarf’s algorithm ([Chandrasekaran et al.](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ICALP.2025.45)); polynomial-time solvability also holds for unit-capacity network hypergraphs ([Chandrasekaran–Kalathur](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.24728v1)).
Scarf’s lemma guarantees existence for arbitrary positive masses ([general-capacity formulation](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol334-icalp2025/html/LIPIcs.ICALP.2025.31/LIPIcs.ICALP.2025.31.html)); no complexity classification for this exact mass problem was located.

Status and new partial results

I do not resolve the classification for unrestricted masses. I prove polynomial-time solvability for three subclasses, all allowing arbitrary positive binary rational masses:

  • The contracts form a laminar family.
  • The intersection graph of the nonsingleton contracts is a forest.
  • The type–contract incidence graph is a forest. In this case the stable allocation is unique. This includes every instance whose contracts have length at most two.

These results extend beyond the minimum-mass-at-the-first-arc condition in the notes. I also prove a complexity obstruction: a standard solution-preserving reduction from satisfiability to this total search problem would imply $\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}$. This obstruction does not establish polynomial-time solvability.

1. General structural facts and short certificates

Let $A$ be the type–contract incidence matrix:
$$ A_{t,e}= \begin{cases} 1,&t\in e,\\ 0,&t\notin e. \end{cases} $$
Write
$$ P(\mu)=\{x\in\mathbb R_{\ge 0}^{E}:Ax\le\mu\}. $$

1.1. Saturation and witnesses

Every stable allocation saturates every type. Indeed, the singleton $\{t\}$ has only the possible witness $t$, and every incident contract is weakly preferred to that singleton. Its stability condition therefore gives
$$ \sum_{f\in\delta(t)}x_f=\mu_t. $$

For any feasible allocation, $t\in e$ witnesses stability of $e$ if and only if
$$ (Ax)_t=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad x_f=0 \quad\text{whenever }f\in\delta(t)\text{ and }e\succ_t f. $$
To prove this, split the total load at $t$ into the load on contracts weakly preferred to $e$ and the load on contracts strictly worse than $e$. Both loads are nonnegative, and their sum is at most $\mu_t$. The first equals $\mu_t$ precisely when the total equals $\mu_t$ and the second is zero.

We will repeatedly use the following consequence.

Witness preservation. Suppose $y$ is stable for a family $F$, and $x$ is feasible after adding contracts to that family. If $x_f\ge y_f$ for every $f\in F$, then every stability witness for $y$ remains a witness for $x$.

For a witnessed contract, the preferred-contract sum cannot decrease. It previously equalled the relevant capacity, and feasibility prevents it from exceeding that capacity. Hence equality persists.

1.2. Total unimodularity

Lemma. The incidence matrix $A$ is totally unimodular.

Proof. The Ghouila–Houri criterion states that a matrix with entries in $\{0,1,-1\}$ is totally unimodular if every subset of its rows can be signed so that each signed column sum belongs to $\{-1,0,1\}$.

Fix a subset $R$ of tree arcs. For $t\in R$, let $k_R(t)$ count the selected arcs on the root-to-$t$ path, including $t$. Sign $t$ positively when $k_R(t)$ is odd and negatively when it is even.

A directed contract is a contiguous segment of a root-to-vertex path. Its selected arcs consequently form a consecutive segment of the selected-arc sequence on that root path. Their signs alternate, so their sum belongs to $\{-1,0,1\}$. This verifies the criterion. $\square$

1.3. Existence of a short stable certificate

The general-capacity form of Scarf’s lemma used here is the following:

If $A$ is nonnegative, every column is nonzero, $b$ is nonnegative, and each row strictly orders its positive columns, then $\{x\ge 0:Ax\le b\}$ has an extreme point that dominates every column. Domination of column $e$ in row $t$ means $A_{t,e}>0$, $(Ax)_t=b_t$, and every positively allocated column incident to $t$ is weakly preferred to $e$.

This is [Lemma 1 of Biró–Csáji–Schlotter](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol334-icalp2025/html/LIPIcs.ICALP.2025.31/LIPIcs.ICALP.2025.31.html). Its hypotheses hold: $A$ is nonnegative, contracts are nonempty, masses are positive, and the required strict orders are supplied. By the witness characterization above, its dominating extreme point is stable.

Write $\mu_t=p_t/q_t$ with positive integers $p_t,q_t$, and let
$$ D=\operatorname{lcm}_{t\in T}q_t. $$
Then
$$ \log_2D\le\sum_{t\in T}\log_2q_t\le L. $$

Proposition. There exists a stable allocation $x^\star$ satisfying
$$ |\{e:x^\star_e>0\}|\le\tau, \qquad Dx^\star\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{E}. $$

Proof. Choose the stable extreme point furnished by Scarf’s lemma. Its positive-support columns are linearly independent: otherwise a nonzero vector $z$ supported on its positive coordinates would satisfy $Az=0$. For sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, both $x^\star+\varepsilon z$ and $x^\star-\varepsilon z$ would be nonnegative and feasible, contradicting extremality.

Thus its support has size at most $\tau$. Singleton columns give $A$ row rank $\tau$, so extend those support columns to a nonsingular square matrix $A_B$ of order $\tau$. Saturation gives
$$ x^\star_B=A_B^{-1}\mu, \qquad x^\star_{E\setminus B}=0. $$
Total unimodularity implies $\det A_B\in\{-1,1\}$; the cofactor formula therefore makes $A_B^{-1}$ integral. Hence $Dx^\star$ is integral.

Finally, feasibility and nonemptiness of contracts give $0\le x^\star_e\le 1$. Every coordinate has a representation $a_e/D$ with $0\le a_e\le D$, using polynomially many bits. $\square$

Consequently, stable certificates can be verified in polynomial time using exact arithmetic. Enumerating all size-$\tau$ column sets, solving the nonsingular systems above, and checking stability also gives an exact algorithm with running time
$$ \binom m\tau\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). $$
This remains exponential when $\tau$ varies.

2. Arbitrary masses on laminar contract families

A family is laminar if any two intersecting members are comparable by inclusion.

Theorem. If $E$ is laminar, a stable allocation can be found in time polynomial in $m,\tau,L$, for arbitrary allowed masses.

The recursion below accepts any subfamily $F\subseteq E$. Capacities remain $\mu_t$, and preferences are restricted to surviving contracts. Some singletons may be absent; the induction requires stability only for contracts in $F$.

If $F$ contains only singletons, allocate $\mu_t$ to every singleton $\{t\}\in F$.

Otherwise:

  • Choose an inclusion-minimal nonsingleton $f\in F$.
  • Choose $r\in f$ with minimum mass:

$$ \mu_r=\min_{t\in f}\mu_t. $$

  • Remove

$$ R= \{g\in F:r\in g,\ f\succeq_r g\} \ \cup\ \{\{t\}\in F:t\in f\}. $$

  • Recursively obtain $y$ on $F'=F\setminus R$, and extend it by zero to $F$.
  • Put

$$ h=\sum_{\substack{g\in F'\\r\in g}}y_g, \qquad a=\mu_r-h. $$
Add $a$ to contract $f$. For each removed singleton $\{t\}$ with $t\in f$, allocate an additional $\mu_t-\mu_r$ to that singleton.

2.1. Feasibility

Every contract in $F'$ intersecting $f$ contains all of $f$. Indeed, a singleton intersecting $f$ was removed. Any remaining nonsingleton intersecting $f$ is, by laminarity, either a subset or a superset of $f$. A proper nonsingleton subset contradicts the choice of $f$, and $f$ itself was removed.

It follows that the recursive load is the same at every member of $f$:
$$ \sum_{\substack{g\in F'\\t\in g}}y_g=h \qquad(t\in f). $$
Feasibility of $y$ gives $h\le\mu_r$, so $a\ge 0$. Adding $a$ to $f$ makes every load on $f$ equal to $\mu_r$, which is at most every $\mu_t$ there.

For a removed singleton $\{t\}$, the subsequent addition $\mu_t-\mu_r$ makes its type’s load exactly $\mu_t$. Types outside $f$ are unchanged. Thus the resulting allocation is feasible.

2.2. Stability

Contracts in $F'$ retain their recursive witnesses by witness preservation.

Every removed nonsingleton $e$ satisfies $r\in e$ and $f\succeq_r e$. Every surviving contract incident to $r$ is strictly preferred to $f$, by the definition of $R$. At $r$, the added allocation to its singleton is zero, and
$$ h+a=\mu_r. $$
All positive allocation at $r$ therefore goes to contracts weakly preferred to $e$. Hence $r$ witnesses $e$.

Every removed singleton is stable because its type was saturated. The base case is feasible and stable, completing the induction.

2.3. Complexity

Each nonbase call removes at least $f$, so there are at most $m$ calls. Straightforward scans use $O(m^2\tau)$ rational arithmetic operations.

All numbers remain multiples of $1/D$. Feasibility bounds loads and coordinates by $1$, and the displayed additions and subtractions have polynomial bit length. Thus the bit complexity is polynomial. $\square$

This theorem permits masses that decrease, increase, or oscillate along a tree path.

3. Arbitrary masses when nonsingleton intersections form a forest

Let
$$ N=\{e\in E:|e|>1\}. $$
Its intersection graph has vertex set $N$, with $e$ adjacent to $f$ when $e\cap f\ne\varnothing$.

Theorem. If this intersection graph is a forest, a stable allocation can be found in polynomial time, for arbitrary allowed masses.

This condition allows adjacent contracts to share several types whose preferences disagree.

3.1. Exact reduction to scalar constraints

No type belongs to three nonsingleton contracts: such contracts would form a triangle in the intersection graph.

For $e\in N$, define
$$ u_e=\min_{t\in e}\mu_t. $$
For adjacent $e,f\in N$, define
$$ c_{ef}=\min_{t\in e\cap f}\mu_t. $$
Introduce an arc $e\to f$ precisely when some minimum-mass type in their intersection prefers $f$:
$$ e\to f \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists t\in e\cap f: \mu_t=c_{ef}\ \text{ and }\ f\succ_t e. $$
Every intersection edge has at least one direction; it may have both.

For proposed nonsingleton coordinates $z$, define singleton coordinates by
$$ x_{\{t\}} = \mu_t-\sum_{\substack{e\in N\\t\in e}}z_e. $$
The resulting allocation is feasible exactly when
$$ 0\le z_e\le u_e \qquad(e\in N) $$
and
$$ z_e+z_f\le c_{ef} \qquad(e,f\text{ adjacent}). $$

To verify this equivalence, a type meets at most two nonsingleton contracts. With one incident contract, its capacity is enforced by the corresponding upper bound. With two, it is enforced by the corresponding pair constraint. Conversely, feasibility gives each upper bound and each pair constraint by taking the minimum over the relevant types.

The singleton coordinates saturate every type, so all singleton contracts are stable.

Lemma. A nonsingleton contract $e$ is stable exactly when
$$ z_e=u_e \quad\text{or}\quad \exists f\text{ adjacent to }e: e\to f\ \text{ and }\ z_e+z_f=c_{ef}. $$

Proof. At a witness $t\in e$, the preferred-contract sum is either $z_e$, or $z_e+z_f$ for the unique other nonsingleton $f$ incident to $t$. Singletons are ranked below $e$.

In the first case, $z_e=\mu_t$. Since $z_e\le u_e\le\mu_t$, equality forces $z_e=u_e$.

In the second case, $f\succ_t e$ and $z_e+z_f=\mu_t$. Since the pair constraint gives
$$ z_e+z_f\le c_{ef}\le\mu_t, $$
we obtain $\mu_t=c_{ef}$ and $e\to f$.

Conversely, if $z_e=u_e$, choose $t\in e$ with $\mu_t=u_e$. Contract $e$ alone fills its capacity, so all other allocations there are zero; this type witnesses $e$. If the second condition holds, a minimum-mass common type defining $e\to f$ witnesses $e$. $\square$

3.2. An auxiliary recursive problem

We solve a slightly more general scalar problem on any induced subforest. Give its vertices upper bounds $U_e$ satisfying
$$ 0\le U_e\le u_e, $$
and retain the original pair capacities and arc directions. Require feasibility and, at each vertex $e$,
$$ z_e=U_e \quad\text{or}\quad \exists f: e\to f,\quad z_e+z_f=c_{ef}. $$
At the initial call $U_e=u_e$, this is exactly the preceding stability criterion.

There is always a vertex $f$ such that
$$ e\to f \qquad\text{for every neighbor }e\text{ of }f. $$
To see this, retain only arcs whose reverse arc is absent. This directed graph is acyclic: a directed cycle cannot have length two, and a longer directed cycle would give an undirected cycle in the forest. Choose a sink. Every incident edge is either directed toward it or has both directions.

Set
$$ z_f=U_f. $$
Delete $f$, and update each neighboring upper bound to
$$ U'_e=\min\{U_e,c_{ef}-U_f\}. $$
Other upper bounds remain unchanged. These new bounds are nonnegative because
$$ U_f\le u_f\le c_{ef}. $$
Recursively solve the remaining induced forest.

3.3. Correctness of the recursion

Induct on the number of vertices.

Feasibility among remaining vertices follows from recursion. For a neighbor $e$ of $f$,
$$ z_e+z_f\le U'_e+U_f\le c_{ef}. $$
Vertex $f$ satisfies its stability condition by $z_f=U_f$.

Consider a remaining vertex $e$. If its recursive stability uses a neighboring vertex that remains, the same equality and arc remain valid.

Otherwise $z_e=U'_e$. If $U'_e=U_e$, this is its original upper-bound condition. If $U'_e<U_e$, then $e$ neighbors $f$ and
$$ z_e+z_f=(c_{ef}-U_f)+U_f=c_{ef}. $$
The choice of $f$ guarantees $e\to f$, so this equality witnesses the required condition at $e$.

Thus recursion solves the auxiliary problem. Starting with $U=u$, the exact reduction above produces a stable allocation of the original society.

There are at most $|N|$ recursive steps. Intersection capacities and directions can be computed by polynomially many scans. All arithmetic consists of minima and subtraction on the input denominator grid, with values in $[0,1]$. The algorithm therefore has polynomial bit complexity. $\square$

3.4. A readily checkable consequence for tree paths

Corollary. The task is polynomial-time solvable whenever each type belongs to at most two nonsingleton contracts.

Proof. It suffices to prove that their intersection graph is a forest.

In any nonempty subfamily, choose $f$ whose first arc $r$ has maximum depth. Every other contract $g$ intersecting $f$ contains $r$: their first arcs lie on the root-to-intersection path, and the first arc of $g$ is at or above $r$.

Because $r$ belongs to at most two nonsingleton contracts, $f$ has at most one neighbor in this subfamily’s intersection graph. Thus every nonempty induced subgraph has a vertex of degree at most one. A graph containing a cycle would have an induced subgraph on that cycle’s vertices with minimum degree at least two. Hence the graph is a forest. Apply the theorem. $\square$

4. Incidence forests: a unique stable allocation

The incidence graph is the bipartite graph with type vertices $T$, contract vertices $E$, and an edge between $t$ and $e$ exactly when $t\in e$.

Theorem. If the incidence graph is a forest, the stable allocation is unique and can be computed in polynomial time for arbitrary allowed masses.

This theorem and the preceding intersection-forest theorem cover different subclasses.

4.1. Existence of a unanimously best contract

In a residual instance, call $e$ unanimously best if it is the highest-ranked surviving contract at every $t\in e$.

Such a contract always exists in an incidence forest, provided every remaining type has at least one incident contract.

Suppose otherwise. Give every type an outgoing arc to its best contract. Give every contract an outgoing arc to a member at which it is not best. Every vertex now has an outgoing arc, so following outgoing arcs in this finite graph produces a directed cycle.

A directed cycle of length two is impossible: a contract points only to a type that points to a different contract. A longer directed cycle gives an undirected cycle in the incidence graph, also impossible. This contradiction proves existence.

4.2. A forced allocation and exact residual reduction

Let $e$ be unanimously best, and put
$$ a=\min_{t\in e}\mu_t, \qquad Z=\{t\in e:\mu_t=a\}. $$

In every stable allocation, stability of $e$ forces $x_e=a$. Indeed, at each member its preferred-contract sum is just $x_e$. Some witness therefore has $x_e=\mu_t$, while feasibility gives $x_e\le a\le\mu_t$.

At every $t\in Z$, contract $e$ exhausts capacity. All other contracts incident to such a type consequently have allocation zero.

Delete the types in $Z$ and all contracts meeting $Z$. For a surviving type, set its residual capacity to
$$ \mu'_t= \begin{cases} \mu_t-a,&t\in e\setminus Z,\\ \mu_t,&t\notin e. \end{cases} $$
Every surviving capacity is positive. Every surviving type retains its singleton, and the residual incidence graph remains a forest.

There is a bijection between stable allocations of this residual instance and stable allocations of the original instance: extend a residual allocation by assigning $a$ to $e$ and zero to the other deleted contracts.

For feasibility, subtracting the fixed allocation $a$ gives exactly the displayed residual capacities. Every deleted contract is witnessed by a type in $Z$, where unanimously best $e$ fills the original capacity.

For a surviving contract $g$ and its member $t$, if $t\in e$, then $e\succ_t g$. Hence its original preferred-contract sum is its residual preferred-contract sum plus $a$, and its original capacity is $\mu'_t+a$. The two witness equalities are therefore equivalent. If $t\notin e$, neither sum nor capacity changes.

This proves both directions of the bijection.

4.3. Algorithm, uniqueness, and the length-two case

Repeatedly find a unanimously best contract and apply the residual reduction. At least one type disappears each time, so there are at most $\tau$ iterations.

The empty instance has exactly one allocation. The bijection above therefore proves uniqueness inductively, while also proving correctness of the algorithm. Scanning incidence lists and preferences at each iteration takes polynomially many operations; residual capacities remain nonnegative multiples of $1/D$ bounded by $1$.

Finally, suppose every contract has length at most two. Each nonsingleton joins a tree arc to an immediately succeeding arc. The graph of these pairs on $T$ is a subgraph of the forest linking each nonroot-adjacent arc to its unique preceding arc. The incidence graph is obtained by subdividing those pair edges and attaching singleton leaves. It is therefore a forest, so the theorem applies. $\square$

5. What ordinary search hardness would imply

The short-certificate proposition makes this a total search problem with polynomial-time verification and polynomial-length solutions. Formally, restricting certificates to
$$ x_e=a_e/D,\qquad 0\le a_e\le D, $$
gives a total polynomially balanced relation on valid instances. Invalid encodings can be recognized in polynomial time and assigned a special certificate, giving a total relation on all encodings.

Here is a precise obstruction to one common meaning of $\mathrm{NP}$-hard search.

Proposition. Suppose there are polynomial-time maps $I$ and $d$ such that:

  • $I(\varphi)$ is a valid instance for every Boolean formula $\varphi$;
  • whenever $\varphi$ is satisfiable, every stable allocation $x$ of $I(\varphi)$ makes $d(\varphi,x)$ a satisfying assignment of $\varphi$.

Then $\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}$.

Proof. An unsatisfiability certificate consists of a short stable allocation $x$ for $I(\varphi)$. Verify stability and check that $d(\varphi,x)$ is not a satisfying assignment.

If $\varphi$ is unsatisfiable, such a certificate exists because a short stable allocation always exists and no assignment satisfies $\varphi$.

If $\varphi$ is satisfiable, the reduction’s hypothesis says every verified stable allocation decodes to a satisfying assignment, so no certificate passes.

Thus unsatisfiability belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$. Since it is $\mathrm{coNP}$-complete, this implies $\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{coNP}$. $\square$

This does not exclude hardness under other explicitly specified notions, and it does not establish a polynomial algorithm. It explains why transferring a decision or existence hardness result to this always-solvable search problem requires particular care.

6. Remaining unrestricted case

The algorithms proved here do not cover all rooted-tree path systems. For example, on a three-arc chain $a,b,c$, include the nonsingleton contracts
$$ \{a,b\},\qquad \{b,c\},\qquad \{a,b,c\}. $$
This family is not laminar, its nonsingleton intersection graph contains a triangle, and its incidence graph contains a cycle. With
$$ \mu_a=\frac12,\qquad \mu_b=\frac13,\qquad \mu_c=\frac16, $$
it also violates the first-arc minimum-mass condition from the notes. This example only demonstrates that the proved criteria leave cases uncovered; it is not a hardness example.

The unrestricted classification therefore remains open in this write-up.
