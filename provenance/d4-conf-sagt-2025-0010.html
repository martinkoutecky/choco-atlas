The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(\mathcal T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a finite state set \(\Theta\) with rational prior \(q\), and rational opinions \(a_{t\theta}\in[0,1]\), choose a finite public signaling scheme \(x_{\theta j}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{\theta j}=q_\theta\). For each signal \(j\), let \(p_j=\sum_\theta x_{\theta j}\) and, when \(p_j>0\), let \(z_{tj}=\sum_\theta a_{t\theta}x_{\theta j}/p_j\). What is the maximum expected covered population mass, \(\sum_j p_j\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t\mathbf{1}[z_{tj}\ge 1/2]\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types \(t\in\mathcal T\) with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\ge1\) states \(\Theta\), a binary rational prior \(q_\theta\ge0\) with \(\sum_\theta q_\theta=1\), and binary rational opinions \(a_{t\theta}\in[0,1]\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
The sender observes the state and commits in advance to a public signaling scheme with any finite signal set \(J\), represented by joint probabilities \(x_{\theta j}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{j\in J}x_{\theta j}=q_\theta\).
For \(p_j=\sum_\theta x_{\theta j}>0\), the opinion of type \(t\) is \(z_{tj}=\sum_\theta a_{t\theta}x_{\theta j}/p_j\); its entire mass is covered exactly when \(z_{tj}\ge\tfrac12\), including equality. Signals of probability zero contribute zero.
Maximize \(F(x)=\sum_{j:p_j>0}p_j\sum_t\mu_t\mathbf1[z_{tj}\ge\tfrac12]\), and denote the optimum by \(\mathrm{OPT}\). State running times in \(m,\tau,L\) and any approximation parameters.

For each fixed \(0<\varepsilon<1\), is computing a public signaling scheme with \(F(x)\ge\mathrm{OPT}/\tau^{1-\varepsilon}\) possible in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this approximation task NP-hard?

For discrete societies of \(n\) agents, maximizing the expected number with opinion in \([\tfrac12,1]\) is NP-hard to approximate within \(n^{1-\varepsilon}\) for every fixed \(0<\varepsilon<1\), even without social influence and with a uniform state prior; a polynomial-time \(n\)-approximation is known.   • Allow any finite public signal set with commitment, and assign zero contribution to signals of probability zero.

1. The discrete hardness premise

We use the discrete-society hardness theorem supplied in the question: approximating the maximum expected number of covered agents within \(n^{1-\varepsilon}\) is NP-hard for every fixed \(0<\varepsilon<1\), even without social influence and with a uniform state prior.

Its numerical hypotheses fit the present input model. The cited construction has \(m=n\), prior probabilities \(1/n\), and opinions drawn from \(0\), \(3/4\), and \(1/2-1/(8n)\). These are binary-encoded rationals of bit length \(O(\log n)\). The desired interval is the closed interval \([1/2,1]\), so its treatment of equality agrees with this problem. See [Theorem 5 and its construction](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.07520v1#S4.SS3).

Here and below, “binary rational” means a rational represented by a binary-encoded numerator and denominator.

2. A polynomial reduction to support representation

Consider a discrete instance with \(n\ge1\) agents. Write \(a_{i\theta}\) for agent \(i\)'s opinion in state \(\theta\).

Partition the agents into equivalence classes according to their complete opinion vectors: agents \(i\) and \(i'\) are equivalent precisely when \(a_{i\theta}=a_{i'\theta}\) for every state \(\theta\). For each class \(t\), let \(k_t\) be its cardinality. Create one type with its common opinion vector and mass \(\mu_t=k_t/n\).

All masses are positive rationals, and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The resulting support size satisfies \(1\le\tau\le n\). Keep the states and prior unchanged.

For a feasible scheme \(x\), define the discrete objective \(C(x)\) to be the expected number of covered agents. Agents in the same class have identical posterior opinions at every positive-probability signal. Consequently,

\(C(x)=\sum_{j:p_j>0}p_j\sum_t k_t\mathbf1[z_{tj}\ge1/2]=nF(x).\)

This identity holds for every feasible finite scheme, including signals that place opinions exactly at the threshold. Zero-probability signals contribute zero to both objectives. Moreover, the feasible schemes depend only on the prior, which was unchanged. Taking optimal values therefore gives

\(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{discrete}}=n\,\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{types}}.\)

The reduction is polynomial in the discrete input length \(B\), \(n\), and \(m\): exact rational comparisons identify the classes, and each new mass requires \(O(\log n)\) bits. The resulting input length satisfies \(L=O(B+n\log n)\).

Now suppose an algorithm, polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), always returned a scheme satisfying the requested guarantee. Apply it to the constructed type instance and use its joint probabilities as the discrete signaling scheme. Then

\(C(x)=nF(x)\ge \dfrac{n\,\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{types}}}{\tau^{1-\varepsilon}}=\dfrac{\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{discrete}}}{\tau^{1-\varepsilon}}\ge\dfrac{\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{discrete}}}{n^{1-\varepsilon}}.\)

The last inequality follows from \(\tau\le n\) and \(1-\varepsilon>0\). Its running time is polynomial in the original input parameters, contradicting the discrete hardness premise unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\).

Thus coincident opinion vectors cause no difficulty: merging them makes the proposed approximation guarantee at least as strong as the forbidden discrete guarantee. No separate hardness theorem for arbitrary rational masses is needed.

3. A polynomial-time \(\tau\)-approximation

For completeness, the baseline approximation also extends without expanding the masses into individual agents.

For each type \(t\), solve the rational linear program

\(\displaystyle M_t=\max\left\{\sum_\theta y_\theta:\ 0\le y_\theta\le q_\theta,\quad \sum_\theta(a_{t\theta}-1/2)y_\theta\ge0\right\}.\)

Its feasible set is nonempty and compact, so an optimum exists. Rational linear programming computes an optimal \(y^{(t)}\) in time polynomial in \(m,L\).

Let \(R_t(x)\) denote the total probability of signals covering type \(t\). For any scheme, sum its joint-probability columns over those signals. The resulting vector satisfies the LP constraints: each covered column satisfies the displayed coverage inequality, and their sum is coordinatewise at most \(q\). Hence \(R_t(x)\le M_t\).

Conversely, the two columns \(y^{(t)}\) and \(q-y^{(t)}\) form a feasible scheme. If its first column has positive probability, it covers \(t\) by the LP inequality; otherwise \(M_t=0\). Therefore this scheme achieves \(R_t(x)\ge M_t\). Together with the preceding upper bound, this proves that \(M_t\) is exactly the maximum achievable coverage probability for type \(t\).

For every scheme,

\(F(x)=\sum_t\mu_tR_t(x)\le\sum_t\mu_tM_t\le\tau\max_t\mu_tM_t.\)

Choose a type maximizing \(\mu_tM_t\), and output its two-column scheme. Its value is at least that maximum, hence at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/\tau\).

This algorithm uses \(\tau\) LPs, each with \(m\) variables and \(2m+1\) inequalities. Its total running time is \(\tau\,\operatorname{poly}(m,L)\), with at most two signals and polynomial output bit length.
