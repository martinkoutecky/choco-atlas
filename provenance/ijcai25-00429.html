The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(p\) taker types and \(q\) giver types with rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\), complete cross-rankings induced by unknown line positions \(x_i,y_j\), and \(\kappa\in(0,1]\), compute from rankings alone a coupling \(\pi\) with row masses \(\mu_i\) and column masses \(\nu_j\) minimizing \(\mathrm{SC}_{\kappa}(\pi)=\int_0^\kappa Q_{\pi}^{\downarrow}(u)\,du\), where \(Q_{\pi}^{\downarrow}\) is the decreasing quantile function of distances \(|x_i-y_j|\) weighted by \(\pi_{ij}\), for every consistent embedding and simultaneously for all \(\kappa\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

There are \(p\geq1\) taker types and \(q\geq1\) giver types, with positive binary rational masses satisfying \(\sum_i\mu_i=\sum_j\nu_j=1\).
A society is given by its support: the list of its \(\tau=p+q\) types with positive mass, each with its mass as a binary rational; \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Put \(m=p+q\), and let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Each type supplies a complete strict ranking of the opposite side. The profile is promised consistent with some pairwise distinct real positions \(x_1,\ldots,x_p,y_1,\ldots,y_q\): ranking one opposite type above another requires its distance to be weakly smaller. Equal distances may be ordered arbitrarily in the supplied rankings.
A feasible coupling is a nonnegative rational matrix \(\pi\) with \(\sum_j\pi_{ij}=\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i\pi_{ij}=\nu_j\); mass may be split.
For an embedding \(d\), let \(Q_{\pi,d}^{\downarrow}\) be the decreasing quantile function of the probability distribution assigning mass \(\pi_{ij}\) to \(|x_i-y_j|\), and define \(\mathrm{SC}_{\kappa}(\pi\mid d)=\int_0^{\kappa}Q_{\pi,d}^{\downarrow}(u)\,du\).
The required output depends only on masses and rankings: one coupling \(\pi\) satisfying \(\mathrm{SC}_{\kappa}(\pi\mid d)=\min_{\rho}\mathrm{SC}_{\kappa}(\rho\mid d)\) for every consistent embedding \(d\) and every real \(\kappa\in(0,1]\), where the minimum ranges over all feasible couplings.

Is computing this universally and simultaneously optimal coupling in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For equal finite populations with indivisible agents, a polynomial-time deterministic ordinal algorithm returns a matching simultaneously optimal for every \(k\)-centrum objective, the sum of the \(k\) largest matching distances. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00149v1)
No result settling the exact rational-mass ordinal problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized each side's total mass to one, as required by the quantile domain (0,1], and required every listed mass to be positive.
  • Carried over pairwise distinct real locations and complete strict rankings with weak distance consistency; equal-distance ties follow the supplied orders.
  • Required only the existence of a consistent embedding as an input promise; positions are not supplied.
  • Made the comparator all feasible couplings with the same marginals, allowing divisible mass and requiring a rational output matrix.
  • Interpreted simultaneous optimality as requiring one output independent of kappa, for all real kappa in (0,1], rather than a separate output for each supplied kappa.
  • Defined m=p+q and L as total input bit length to specify the computational size parameters.

Result

The problem is polynomial-time solvable. There is a deterministic ordinal algorithm returning one rational coupling that is optimal for every consistent embedding and every real \(\kappa\in(0,1]\).

The algorithm recovers the orders of the support types, aligns their orientations, and transports mass greedily along these orders. It works directly with binary rational masses.

The finite-population result appears as Theorem 4.1 of the [supplied paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00149v1). Below is a self-contained proof for the exact rational-mass problem, including arbitrary distance tie-breaking.

1. Recovering the support orders

Write \(A\) for the taker types and \(B\) for the giver types. Rankings are listed from best to worst. For any type \(v\), let \(\ell(v)\) denote its last-ranked opposite type.

If \(p=1\), feasibility uniquely forces \(\pi_{1j}=\nu_j\). Similarly, if \(q=1\), it forces \(\pi_{i1}=\mu_i\). These unique feasible couplings satisfy the requirement. Henceforth assume \(p,q\geq2\).

Define \(F_B=\{\ell(a):a\in A\}\) and \(F_A=\{\ell(b):b\in B\}\).

Lemma 1: Order recovery on one side

From the rankings, one can recover the order of \(A\) up to reversal. The same holds for \(B\).

Proof. Fix an arbitrary consistent embedding.

For any real point, a farthest member of a finite set of distinct line positions must be an endpoint of that set: every interior position has distance strictly smaller than the maximum distance to the two endpoints. Consequently, \(F_B\) contains either one or two types.

We use the elementary equivalence, valid for \(u<v\),

\[ \]
\( |z-u|\leq |z-v| \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z\leq (u+v)/2. \)

It follows by squaring both nonnegative distances and rearranging.

Case 1: \(F_B=\{b\}\). The type \(b\) is an endpoint of \(B\). Suppose it is the rightmost endpoint. Choose any other giver \(b'\). Every taker ranks \(b'\) above \(b\), so consistency gives

\( x_a\leq (y_{b'}+y_b)/2<y_b. \)

Thus all takers lie strictly left of \(b\). Their distances from \(b\) are strictly decreasing in their positions, so \(b\)'s ranking is precisely their right-to-left order.

If \(b\) is the leftmost endpoint, the symmetric argument shows that its ranking is their left-to-right order. Therefore, returning \(b\)'s ranking recovers the order up to reversal.

Case 2: \(F_B=\{b_\ell,b_r\}\). Choose either naming of these two types. By reflecting the embedding if necessary, suppose \(y_{b_\ell}<y_{b_r}\). They are the two endpoints of \(B\).

Partition the takers into

\( S=\{a:\ell(a)=b_r\},\qquad T=\{a:\ell(a)=b_\ell\}. \)

Both sets are nonempty. Put \(c=(y_{b_\ell}+y_{b_r})/2\). Consistency and the displayed equivalence give

\( x_s\leq c\quad(s\in S),\qquad x_t\geq c\quad(t\in T). \)

Because all positions are distinct, every member of \(S\) lies strictly left of every member of \(T\), even when one taker is at \(c\).

Moreover, all members of \(S\) lie strictly left of \(b_r\). Consequently, reversing \(b_r\)'s ranking restricted to \(S\) gives the left-to-right order of \(S\). All members of \(T\) lie strictly right of \(b_\ell\), so \(b_\ell\)'s ranking restricted to \(T\) gives the left-to-right order of \(T\).

Concatenating these two lists recovers the order of \(A\), oriented from \(b_\ell\) toward \(b_r\). This construction uses only rankings.

Interchanging the two sides proves the assertion for \(B\). \(\square\)

Lemma 2: Aligning the orientations

One can compute two orders that, in every consistent embedding, are either both left-to-right or both right-to-left.

Proof. First recover both orders using Lemma 1.

If \(F_B\) has two members, use the naming \(b_\ell,b_r\) chosen during recovery of \(A\). Orient the recovered order of \(B\) to start with \(b_\ell\). This is possible because \(b_\ell\) is an endpoint. Both orders now run in the direction from \(b_\ell\) toward \(b_r\).

If \(F_B\) has one member but \(F_A\) has two, apply the symmetric construction.

It remains to consider

\( F_B=\{b_*\},\qquad F_A=\{a_*\}. \)

Return the following orders:

  • For \(A\), reverse \(b_*\)'s ranking.
  • For \(B\), use \(a_*\)'s ranking.

To verify their alignment, reflect the embedding so that \(b_*\) is the rightmost giver. The proof of Lemma 1 shows that all takers lie strictly left of \(b_*\). Thus reversing its ranking orders \(A\) left-to-right. Its last-ranked taker is \(a_*\), which is therefore the leftmost taker.

Applying the singleton case of Lemma 1 to the opposite side shows that all givers lie on the same side of \(a_*\). Since \(b_*\) lies right of \(a_*\), every giver lies right of \(a_*\). Hence \(a_*\)'s ranking orders \(B\) left-to-right.

All constructions are determined by the supplied rankings, and the reasoning applies separately to every consistent embedding. \(\square\)

2. Constructing the coupling

Relabel the two aligned orders as \(a_1,\ldots,a_p\) and \(b_1,\ldots,b_q\).

Maintain their residual masses, initially \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\). Starting with the first unexhausted taker and giver:

  • Assign the smaller of their residual masses to their pair.
  • Subtract that amount from both residual masses.
  • Advance past every exhausted type.

Continue until all mass is assigned. Denote the resulting coupling by \(\pi^*\).

Every assignment preserves equality of the two total residual masses. Thus the procedure exhausts both sides and satisfies all prescribed marginals. All assigned amounts are rational.

Every assignment exhausts at least one type, and the final assignment exhausts two. Therefore \(\pi^*\) has at most \(p+q-1=\tau-1\) positive entries.

3. An uncrossing inequality

Fix an embedding in which the two orders are increasing; a common reflection handles decreasing orders.

For a threshold \(t\geq0\), define

\( h_t(z)=\max\{|z|-t,0\} \)

and, for a coupling \(\rho\),

\( H_t(\rho\mid d)=\sum_{i,j}\rho_{ij}h_t(x_i-y_j). \)

Lemma 3: Uncrossing decreases every threshold cost

If \(a\leq a'\) and \(b\leq b'\), then, for every \(t\geq0\),

\( h_t(a-b)+h_t(a'-b')\leq h_t(a-b')+h_t(a'-b). \)

Proof. The function \(h_t\) is convex because it is the maximum of the three affine functions \(z-t\), \(-z-t\), and \(0\).

Set

\( u=a-b',\quad v=a'-b,\quad r=a-b,\quad s=a'-b'. \)

These numbers satisfy

\( u\leq r,s\leq v,\qquad r+s=u+v. \)

If \(u=v\), they are all equal. Otherwise, for some \(\lambda\in[0,1]\),

\( r=\lambda u+(1-\lambda)v,\qquad s=(1-\lambda)u+\lambda v. \)

Applying convexity to both expressions and adding yields

\( h_t(r)+h_t(s)\leq h_t(u)+h_t(v), \)

which is the desired inequality. \(\square\)

Lemma 4: The greedy coupling minimizes every threshold cost

For every feasible rational coupling \(\rho\) and every \(t\geq0\),

\( H_t(\pi^*\mid d)\leq H_t(\rho\mid d). \)

Proof. We transform \(\rho\) into \(\pi^*\) by finitely many uncrossings.

Consider the first active row and column, temporarily numbered \(1\), with residual marginals \(r_1,c_1\). Their greedy assignment is \(g=\min\{r_1,c_1\}\).

If the current entry \(\rho_{11}\) is less than \(g\), both its row and its column have positive mass elsewhere. Choose \(j>1\) and \(i>1\) with \(\rho_{1j}>0\) and \(\rho_{i1}>0\). Set

\( \varepsilon=\min\{\rho_{1j},\rho_{i1},g-\rho_{11}\}>0. \)

Subtract \(\varepsilon\) from entries \((1,j)\) and \((i,1)\), and add it to entries \((1,1)\) and \((i,j)\). Nonnegativity and all marginals are preserved.

Since \(x_1\leq x_i\) and \(y_1\leq y_j\), Lemma 3 shows that this transfer does not increase \(H_t\), for any threshold \(t\).

Each transfer either reaches \(\rho_{11}=g\) or eliminates a positive entry elsewhere in the first row or first column. Such entries never increase during this stage. Hence the stage terminates after finitely many transfers.

Once \(\rho_{11}=g\), at least one of the first row and first column is exhausted. Freeze this assignment, remove exhausted types, and repeat on the residual coupling.

The process terminates and produces exactly the greedy coupling. Every transfer is rational and decreases or preserves every \(H_t\) simultaneously. \(\square\)

4. From threshold costs to every quantile objective

Lemma 5: Threshold representation

For every feasible coupling \(\rho\) and every \(\kappa\in(0,1]\),

\( \displaystyle \mathrm{SC}_{\kappa}(\rho\mid d)=\min_{t\geq0}\bigl\{\kappa t+H_t(\rho\mid d)\bigr\}. \)

Proof. Write \(Q=Q_{\rho,d}^{\downarrow}\). For every \(t\geq0\),

\( \displaystyle \int_0^\kappa Q(u)\,du\leq \kappa t+\int_0^\kappa (Q(u)-t)_+\,du\leq \kappa t+\int_0^1(Q(u)-t)_+\,du. \)

The last integral equals \(H_t(\rho\mid d)\), so every expression on the right is an upper bound.

For attainment, list the distinct distances receiving positive mass as \(c_1>\cdots>c_r\), with masses \(w_1,\ldots,w_r\). Put \(W_h=\sum_{\ell=1}^h w_\ell\) and \(W_0=0\). Choose \(h\) with \(W_{h-1}<\kappa\leq W_h\). Then

\( \displaystyle \mathrm{SC}_{\kappa}(\rho\mid d)=\sum_{\ell<h}w_\ell c_\ell+(\kappa-W_{h-1})c_h. \)

At \(t=c_h\), the threshold expression equals

\( \displaystyle \kappa c_h+\sum_{\ell<h}w_\ell(c_\ell-c_h)=\mathrm{SC}_{\kappa}(\rho\mid d). \)

Thus the minimum is attained and the identity holds. \(\square\)

Universal simultaneous optimality

Let \(d\) be any consistent embedding, \(\kappa\in(0,1]\), and \(\rho\) any feasible rational coupling. Lemma 2 makes the algorithm's orders increasing after a possible common reflection.

Choose a minimizing threshold \(t_\rho\) for \(\rho\), whose existence follows from Lemma 5. Lemmas 4 and 5 give

\( \displaystyle \mathrm{SC}_{\kappa}(\pi^*\mid d)\leq \kappa t_\rho+H_{t_\rho}(\pi^*\mid d)\leq \kappa t_\rho+H_{t_\rho}(\rho\mid d)=\mathrm{SC}_{\kappa}(\rho\mid d). \)

Since \(\pi^*\) is itself feasible, it attains the required minimum.

The embedding, threshold fraction, and competitor were arbitrary. Therefore this single ordinally computed coupling is optimal for every consistent embedding and every real \(\kappa\in(0,1]\).

5. Bit complexity

Here \(m=\tau=p+q\). Reading the explicit rankings and recovering and aligning the orders takes \(O(pq+m)\) operations on type labels.

For exact mass arithmetic, let \(D\) be the product of all input mass denominators. Its binary length is \(O(L)\). Convert the masses into integer numerators over \(D\), run the greedy procedure using integer comparisons and subtractions, and output its assignments divided by \(D\).

Using schoolbook integer arithmetic:

  • Computing \(D\) and converting the \(\tau\) masses costs \(O(\tau L^2)\) bit operations.
  • The greedy procedure performs at most \(\tau-1\) assignments on \(O(L)\)-bit integers.
  • Each positive output entry has \(O(L)\) bits.

Including label handling and dense matrix output if requested, a conservative total bound is

\( O\!\left(m^2\log m+\tau L^2\right) \)

bit operations. The sparse output has at most \(\tau-1\) positive entries.

Thus the exact rational-mass ordinal problem belongs to \(\mathrm{P}\).
