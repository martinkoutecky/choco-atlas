Theorem 3 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), a finite set \(T\) of complete pairwise-judgment types, a rational distribution \(\nu\) over \(T\), a target \(w\) in the weighted uncovered set of the induced society, and a rational threshold \(K\), let \(p(a,b)=\sum_{t\in T}\nu_t\mathbf{1}[t\text{ ranks }a\text{ above }b]\). Decide whether there is a componentwise inclusion-minimal matrix \(x\) satisfying \(0\leq x(a,b)\leq p(a,b)\) and \(x(a,b)+x(b,a)\leq1\), such that \(w\) is in the weighted uncovered set of every completion \(q\) with \(q(a,b)\geq x(a,b)\) and \(q(a,b)+q(b,a)=1\), and \(\sum_{a\ne b}x(a,b)\leq K\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty candidate set with \(m=|C|\). A type gives exactly one strict judgment for each unordered pair of distinct candidates; judgments need not be transitive.
A society is given by its support: the list \(T\) of its \(\tau\) positive-mass types, each mass \(\nu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_{t\in T}\nu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
Define \(p(a,b)=\sum_{t\in T}\nu_t\mathbf 1[t\text{ judges }a\text{ above }b]\) for distinct candidates, and \(p(a,a)=0\).
For any complete real matrix \(q\) with zero diagonal, nonnegative entries, and \(q(a,b)+q(b,a)=1\) for distinct candidates, define its weighted uncovered set \(\operatorname{wUC}(q)\) by: \(u\in\operatorname{wUC}(q)\) precisely when every \(v\ne u\) satisfies \(q(u,v)>q(v,u)\) or admits \(z\in C\setminus\{u,v\}\) with \(q(u,z)>q(v,z)\). These inequalities are strict; no tie-breaking is applied.
The input additionally contains \(w\in\operatorname{wUC}(p)\) and a binary-rational threshold \(K\). A support is a real matrix \(x\) with zero diagonal, \(0\le x(a,b)\le p(a,b)\), and \(x(a,b)+x(b,a)\le1\) for distinct candidates. Its completions are all complete real matrices \(q\ge x\) as above, without restrictions to the original types.
A support is sufficient if \(w\in\operatorname{wUC}(q)\) for every completion. It is componentwise inclusion-minimal if it is sufficient and no distinct support \(y\le x\) is sufficient. Decide whether such a minimal support exists with \(\sum_{a\ne b}x(a,b)\le K\).

Is this decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

For complete integer-weighted tournaments with \(n\ge2\), a promised weighted-uncovered winner, and an integer threshold, existence of a minimal support within the threshold is NP-complete. [Verified discrete result](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.09312v4).
No complexity result for this exact continuous decision problem was identified in the sources checked.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted complete pairwise-judgment types as strict orientations of every distinct pair, without transitivity, matching the independent-comparison setting.
  • Normalized total society mass to one and required every listed support mass to be positive.
  • Used the strict path characterization underlying the anchor to define the weighted uncovered set, resolving its inconsistency with the preliminary covering definition; intermediate candidates are distinct from both endpoints.
  • Allowed real support weights, real completions, and real componentwise decreases, with no positive minimum increment.
  • Required zero diagonals and nonnegative completion entries; completions need not use the input types or preserve their distribution.
  • Retained the nonempty candidate domain and allowed any rational threshold, as the anchor imposes no additional candidate-count or threshold-sign condition.

Result

The decision problem as stated is in \(\mathrm{P}\). More precisely, its answer is YES exactly when \(m=1\) and \(K\ge 0\).

For every \(m\ge 2\), sufficient supports exist under the winner promise, but none is componentwise inclusion-minimal. This follows from the strict inequalities and the ability to decrease real support weights arbitrarily slightly.

1. Exact characterization of sufficiency

Lemma. A support \(x\) is sufficient if and only if, for every \(v\ne w\), at least one of the following holds:

  • \(x(w,v)>\tfrac12\);
  • there exists \(z\in C\setminus\{w,v\}\) such that \(x(w,z)+x(z,v)>1\).

Proof. First suppose these conditions hold. Let \(q\) be any completion of \(x\), and fix \(v\ne w\).

If the first condition holds, then \(q(w,v)\ge x(w,v)>\tfrac12\). Since \(q(v,w)=1-q(w,v)\), this gives \(q(w,v)>q(v,w)\).

If the second condition holds, choose its witness \(z\). Completeness and \(q\ge x\) give
\(q(v,z)=1-q(z,v)\le 1-x(z,v)\). Consequently,
\(q(w,z)\ge x(w,z)>1-x(z,v)\ge q(v,z)\).

Thus every rival satisfies one of the defining conditions for \(w\in\operatorname{wUC}(q)\). Since \(q\) was arbitrary, \(x\) is sufficient.

Conversely, suppose both conditions fail for some \(v\ne w\). Then
\(x(w,v)\le\tfrac12\), and \(x(w,z)+x(z,v)\le1\) for every \(z\in C\setminus\{w,v\}\).

Construct a completion by setting

  • \(q(w,v)=x(w,v)\);
  • \(q(w,z)=x(w,z)\) for every \(z\in C\setminus\{w,v\}\);
  • \(q(v,z)=1-x(z,v)\) for every such \(z\).

These prescriptions concern distinct unordered pairs, so they are consistent. Assign the reverse entry on each prescribed pair by completeness. Every reverse entry respects its lower bound because \(x(a,b)+x(b,a)\le1\). Complete all remaining pairs in the same way: choose either orientation, set its entry to the corresponding entry of \(x\), and set the reverse entry to its complement.

The resulting matrix is a completion of \(x\). It satisfies \(q(w,v)\le q(v,w)\), and, for every eligible \(z\),
\(q(w,z)=x(w,z)\le1-x(z,v)=q(v,z)\).
Hence \(v\) defeats both alternatives in the definition of membership for \(w\), so \(w\notin\operatorname{wUC}(q)\). Therefore \(x\) is not sufficient. \(\square\)

2. Every sufficient support can be strictly decreased

Proposition. If \(m\ge2\) and \(x\) is sufficient, there exists a distinct sufficient support \(y\le x\).

Proof. For each \(v\ne w\), select one witness from the lemma. Write its inequality as \(A_v(x)>b_v\), where either

  • \(A_v(x)=x(w,v)\) and \(b_v=\tfrac12\), or
  • \(A_v(x)=x(w,z)+x(z,v)\) for a selected \(z\), and \(b_v=1\).

Each \(A_v\) is homogeneous: \(A_v(\lambda x)=\lambda A_v(x)\).

There are finitely many rivals, and each selected inequality is strict. Therefore the number
\(r=\max_{v\ne w} b_v/A_v(x)\)
is defined and satisfies \(0<r<1\).

Choose \(\lambda=(1+r)/2\), and set \(y=\lambda x\). Then \(0<\lambda<1\). In particular, \(y\) has zero diagonal, \(0\le y\le x\le p\), and
\(y(a,b)+y(b,a)=\lambda\bigl(x(a,b)+x(b,a)\bigr)\le1\).
Thus \(y\) is a support.

For every rival,
\(A_v(y)=\lambda A_v(x)>b_v\), because \(\lambda>r\ge b_v/A_v(x)\).
The lemma therefore shows that \(y\) is sufficient.

Finally, \(x\ne0\): with at least one rival, the lemma requires a positive witness value. Hence \(\lambda<1\) implies \(y\ne x\). \(\square\)

It follows immediately that no sufficient support is componentwise inclusion-minimal when \(m\ge2\).

This is not a failure of the winner promise to ensure sufficiency. Indeed, \(p\) itself is sufficient: any complete \(q\ge p\) must equal \(p\), since both entries on each unordered pair sum to one. The promise gives \(w\in\operatorname{wUC}(p)\).

3. Decision procedure and running time

If \(m=1\), the zero matrix is the only support and completion. Membership of \(w\) is vacuous, so this support is sufficient and componentwise minimal. Its total weight is zero. The answer is therefore YES exactly when \(K\ge0\).

If \(m\ge2\), the proposition gives NO for every threshold \(K\).

Thus the algorithm checks \(m\), and, only if \(m=1\), checks the sign of \(K\). Including reading the promised input, its running time is \(O(L)\). It requires no aggregation over the \(\tau\) types and no computation of the pairwise matrix.

4. Why the integer result does not transfer

The cited result uses integer weights for both partial tournaments and completions; see its definitions and [Theorem 9](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.09312v4#S4.SS2).

After normalization by the total integer weight \(n\), those supports remain restricted to a finite grid with spacing \(1/n\). Scaling a support slightly downward generally leaves that grid. In the present problem, real weights are allowed, and the scaling argument preserves all selected strict witnesses.

Already with two candidates and \(p(w,v)=1\), sufficient supports are exactly those with \(x(w,v)\in(\tfrac12,1]\). Their weights have infimum \(\tfrac12\), but no minimal support exists. The proposition proves the same absence of minimality for every \(m\ge2\), without changing the stated definitions.
