Theorem 1 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given peer-type masses \(\mu_t\), assignment-class masses \(\nu_s\), a feasible grading schedule with \(a_{st}\) reviewers of type \(t\) per assignment of class \(s\), diligent reliabilities \(p_t^1\), costs \(c_{ts}\), rewards \(r_{ts}\), and normalized checking budget \(\kappa\), determine the complexity of maximizing the paper’s average lower-bound accuracy over finite measures \(\rho_s\) on \([0,1]\). Each \(\rho_s\) has total mass \(\nu_s\), and \(x\) units of assignment mass receive checking probability \(x\); type \(t\) works diligently on class \(s\) exactly when \(xr_{ts}\ge c_{ts}\). The objective is \(\sum_s\int_0^1\left[1-(1-x)\exp\left(-\frac12\sum_t a_{st}(2p_t^{e_{ts}(x)}-1)^2\right)\right]d\rho_s(x)\), subject to \(\sum_s\int_0^1 x\,d\rho_s(x)\le\kappa\), where \(e_{ts}(x)=1\) when \(xr_{ts}\ge c_{ts}\) and \(0\) otherwise. In particular, determine exact and \(\epsilon\)-approximation complexity in the number of types and classes, input bit length, and precision.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) peer types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. There are \(m\) assignment classes with positive binary-rational masses \(\nu_s\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=\sum_s\nu_s=1\).
A fixed feasible grading schedule specifies integer reviewer counts \(a_{st}\ge0\), with \(\sum_t a_{st}=\ell\ge1\) and \(\sum_s\nu_s a_{st}=\ell\mu_t\); feasibility promises distinct reviewers, no self-review, and load \(\ell\) per peer.
Inputs also include binary rationals \(p_t^1\in(1/2,1]\), \(c_{ts},r_{ts}\in[0,1]\), and normalized checking budget \(\kappa\in[0,1]\). Set \(p_t^0=1/2\). Effort is \(e_{ts}(x)=1\) exactly when \(xr_{ts}\ge c_{ts}\), including indifference; otherwise it is \(0\).
Choose nonnegative finite Borel measures \(\rho_s\) on \([0,1]\), with \(\rho_s([0,1])=\nu_s\). Here \(\rho_s(B)\) is the assignment mass receiving checking probabilities in \(B\).
Maximize \(F(\rho)=\sum_{s=1}^m\int_0^1[1-(1-x)\exp(-\tfrac12\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}a_{st}(2p_t^{e_{ts}(x)}-1)^2)]\,d\rho_s(x)\), subject to \(\sum_s\int_0^1x\,d\rho_s(x)\le\kappa\). Write \(\mathrm{OPT}=\sup_\rho F(\rho)\).
Exact computation means deciding \(\mathrm{OPT}\ge q\) for a binary-rational input \(q\in[0,1]\), using ordinary Turing bit complexity and the mathematical exponential. Additive approximation means computing a feasible rational atomic policy with value at least \(\mathrm{OPT}-\epsilon\), for binary-rational \(0<\epsilon<1\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length.

Is this continuous exact decision problem in \(\mathrm{P}\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?
What is the complexity of additive \(\epsilon\)-approximation, with running times stated in \(m,\tau,\ell,L\), and \(\log(1/\epsilon)\) or \(1/\epsilon\)?

Finite-agent optimal spot-checking with this lower-bound objective is reported \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard by [Wang, An, and Jiang (2018)](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/11336); their [2020 extended version](https://www3.ntu.edu.sg/home/boan/papers/IEEETCSS20.pdf) retains this result.
No result settling exactly the measure-valued problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized both peer and assignment masses to one, so the objective is an average and the checking budget is a fraction.
  • Interpreted reviewer counts as nonnegative integers and carried over equal positive grading load through row sums and mass balance; schedule feasibility is a promise that includes distinct reviewers and no self-review.
  • Restored zero-effort reliability 1/2, diligent reliability in (1/2,1], and costs and rewards in [0,1]; encoded numerical inputs as binary rationals.
  • Retained diligent effort at indifference. Zero rewards are handled directly by the utility inequality, without dividing by the reward.
  • Interpreted the measures as nonnegative finite Borel measures; their mass describes how much of each assignment class receives each checking probability.
  • Specified exact complexity through rational-threshold decision in the Turing model, rather than requiring a finite exact numerical encoding of a potentially transcendental optimum.
  • Specified epsilon-approximation as additive error in normalized average accuracy, with a feasible rational atomic policy.

Rigorous partial solution

The additive approximation problem is solvable in
$$ \operatorname{poly}\!\left(m,\tau,L,\log(\ell+1),\log(1/\epsilon)\right) $$
Turing time, with an exactly feasible rational atomic output supported on at most $m+1$ class-labelled atoms. In particular, no polynomial dependence on $1/\epsilon$ is necessary.

The exact classification is not settled here: I prove decidability, but neither membership in $\mathrm{P}$ nor $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness.

Additional structural results identify the arithmetic issue more precisely:

  • The optimum, as a function of the budget, has at most $m(\min\{\tau,\ell\}+1)$ affine segments and rational budget breakpoints.
  • The zero-budget restriction is polynomial-time equivalent to comparison of positive rational sums of exponentials at nonnegative rational arguments.
  • Equality of the values of two given rational atomic policies is polynomial-time decidable. Ordering those values is a separate issue.

All reductions below use binary encodings of rationals. If “binary-rational” is interpreted more narrowly as dyadic, the arithmetic comparison problems should likewise be restricted to dyadic inputs; the constructions preserve that restriction.

1. Exact reduction to a finite polytope

Define
$$ d_t=(2p_t^1-1)^2,\qquad A_s(x)=\frac12\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}a_{st}d_t e_{ts}(x), \qquad f_s(x)=1-(1-x)e^{-A_s(x)}. $$
Because $p_t^0=1/2$, these definitions reproduce the given objective. Moreover,
$$ 0<d_t\le1,\qquad 0\le A_s(x)\le\frac{\ell}{2}. $$

For each class, let
$$ B_s=\{0,1\}\cup \left\{\frac{c_{ts}}{r_{ts}}: a_{st}>0,\ r_{ts}>0,\ 0<c_{ts}\le r_{ts} \right\}. $$
List the distinct elements as
$$ 0=b_{s,0}<b_{s,1}<\cdots<b_{s,k_s}=1. $$

The effort rule includes the following boundary cases:

  • If $c_{ts}=0$, effort is exerted everywhere, including when $r_{ts}=0$.
  • If $c_{ts}>0=r_{ts}$ or $c_{ts}>r_{ts}$, effort is never exerted.
  • Otherwise, effort begins at $c_{ts}/r_{ts}$, including that point.

Consequently, $A_s$ is constant on every interval $[b_{s,i},b_{s,i+1})$ and can only increase at its right endpoint.

For $x$ in such an interval, set
$$ \lambda(x)=\frac{b_{s,i+1}-x}{b_{s,i+1}-b_{s,i}}. $$
Then
$$ x=\lambda(x)b_{s,i}+(1-\lambda(x))b_{s,i+1} $$
and
$$ f_s(x)\le \lambda(x)f_s(b_{s,i})+ (1-\lambda(x))f_s(b_{s,i+1}). $$
Indeed, $f_s$ is affine on the interval. Its affine continuation to the right endpoint has value at most the actual endpoint value, because the exponent can only increase there.

Thus mass at $x$ may be replaced by these two endpoint masses, preserving checking cost and weakly increasing value. The replacement weights are bounded Borel functions on a finite partition, so integrating them against $\rho_s$ defines a finite atomic measure. Mass already at $1$ is left there.

It follows that the original problem has exactly the same optimum as
$$ \max_{w\in P}\sum_{s,i}w_{s,i}v_{s,i}, \qquad v_{s,i}=1-(1-b_{s,i})e^{-A_s(b_{s,i})}, $$
where
$$ P= \left\{ w\ge0: \sum_iw_{s,i}=\nu_s\ \text{for every }s,\quad \sum_{s,i}b_{s,i}w_{s,i}\le\kappa \right\}. $$
Conversely, each $w\in P$ defines the feasible policy
$$ \rho_s=\sum_iw_{s,i}\delta_{b_{s,i}}. $$

Let
$$ N=\sum_s|B_s|. $$
Since every positive integer $a_{st}$ consumes at least one of the $\ell$ reviewer positions,
$$ N\le m\bigl(\min\{\tau,\ell\}+2\bigr). $$
This bound uses the supplied support only.

Thresholds, exponents, and constraint coefficients are computable using rational arithmetic in polynomial time. Their binary lengths are polynomial in $L$: sums and products involve only polynomially many supplied rationals, and reviewer counts are used in binary.

2. Attainment and rational optimal policies

The polytope $P$ is nonempty, since assigning every class probability $0$ is feasible. It is compact, because
$$ 0\le w_{s,i}\le\nu_s. $$
Its real linear objective therefore attains a maximum at a vertex.

Every vertex is rational. To see this, select a full-rank collection of its active rational constraints; their unique solution is the vertex. Clearing denominators and applying Cramer’s rule also shows that every vertex has polynomial binary length in $L$.

Every vertex has at most $m+1$ positive coordinates. Otherwise, the columns corresponding to its positive coordinates would be linearly dependent in the matrix consisting of the $m$ class-mass rows and the budget row. A nonzero dependence gives a perturbation preserving all those quantities. Sufficiently small perturbations in both directions preserve nonnegativity, contradicting extremality.

Hence an optimal policy exists with at most $m+1$ class-labelled atoms. Since every $\nu_s$ is positive, every class uses at least one atom; at most one class uses two.

The obstacle to exact computation is therefore not the size of an optimal policy’s rational description.

3. Additive approximation with logarithmic precision dependence

3.1 Certified exponential approximations

Set
$$ b=\left\lceil\log_2(1/\epsilon)\right\rceil,\qquad \delta=2^{-b}\le\epsilon,\qquad B=b+1. $$
For every nonnegative rational $A$, we construct a rational $u(A)$ satisfying
$$ e^{-A}\le u(A)\le e^{-A}+\delta, \qquad 0<u(A)\le1. $$

If $A\ge B$, use $u(A)=\delta$. Since $e>2$,
$$ e^{-A}\le e^{-B}<2^{-B}<\delta, $$
which establishes both required inequalities.

If $A<B$, use
$$ n=8(B+b+1),\qquad S=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{A^j}{j!}, \qquad u(A)=\frac1S. $$
Taylor’s theorem gives
$$ 0\le e^A-S\le e^B\frac{A^n}{n!}. $$
The inequality
$$ \log(n!)\ge\int_1^n\log t\,dt =n\log n-n+1 $$
implies $n!\ge(n/e)^n$. Therefore, using $e<4$ and $n\ge8B$,
$$ e^B\frac{A^n}{n!} \le e^B\left(\frac{eB}{n}\right)^n \le2^{2B-n} \le2^{-b}. $$
Since $1\le S\le e^A$,
$$ 0\le\frac1S-e^{-A} =\frac{e^A-S}{Se^A} \le\delta. $$

This construction uses $O(b)$ terms. If $A=a/d$, a common denominator for $S$ is
$$ d^{\,n-1}(n-1)!. $$
Its bit length, and those of the intermediate numerators, are polynomial in the bit length of $A$ and in $b$. Thus the construction has polynomial bit complexity. The preliminary test $A\ge B$ avoids dependence polynomial in the numerical value of $\ell$.

3.2 Rational optimization and the error guarantee

Replace the objective coefficients by
$$ \widetilde v_{s,i} =1-(1-b_{s,i})u(A_s(b_{s,i})). $$
They satisfy
$$ 0\le v_{s,i}-\widetilde v_{s,i}\le\delta. $$
Every feasible vector has total mass $1$, so
$$ 0\le \sum_{s,i}w_{s,i}(v_{s,i}-\widetilde v_{s,i}) \le\delta. $$

The algorithmic theorem used here is the polynomial-time solvability of rational linear programming: a linear program with rational coefficients can be checked for feasibility and unboundedness, and, when its optimum is finite, an exact rational optimal solution and value can be computed in time polynomial in its binary encoding length. This is the classical Khachiyan theorem; its bit-complexity formulation is also stated in [Adler and Beling’s research paper](https://adler.ieor.berkeley.edu/ilans_pubs/lp_algebraic_stoc_1992.pdf).

Here all constraints and the approximated objective are rational, their total encoding length is polynomial in $L+b$, and feasibility and boundedness were proved above.

For completeness, obtaining an optimal vertex also requires only polynomial work. First compute the rational optimum and restrict to its optimal face. Successively minimize each coordinate over the current face, adding the resulting coordinate equality. Each step retains a nonempty face of $P$. After all coordinates have been fixed, the face is a singleton and hence a vertex of $P$. Coordinate minima are attained at vertices of $P$, so their bit lengths satisfy the polynomial bounds already proved. There are only $N$ additional rational LP calls.

Let $\widetilde w$ be the resulting vertex and $\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}$ its approximated objective value. Then
$$ \widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}} \le\mathrm{OPT} \le\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}+\delta, $$
and its actual value satisfies
$$ F(\widetilde\rho) \ge\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}} \ge\mathrm{OPT}-\epsilon. $$
The policy is exactly feasible because the constraints were never approximated.

The running time is
$$ \operatorname{poly}\!\left( m,\tau,L,\log(\ell+1), \left\lceil\log_2(1/\epsilon)\right\rceil \right), $$
with at most $m(\min\{\tau,\ell\}+2)$ LP variables. Since $L$ accounts for the supplied arrays and binary reviewer counts, this is also
$$ \operatorname{poly}\!\left(L+\log(1/\epsilon)\right). $$
If the accuracy input’s encoding is excluded from $L$, its encoding length must additionally be included.

The output uses at most $m+1$ atoms. Their positions and masses have polynomial bit length in the original constraint encoding, independently of the precision used for the objective.

4. The entire budget-value function has polynomially many segments

Let $V(k)$ denote the optimum when the budget is $k\in[0,1]$, with all other data fixed.

First, $f_s$ is strictly increasing. If $x<y$, then $A_s(y)\ge A_s(x)$, and
$$ \begin{aligned} f_s(y)-f_s(x) &=(y-x)e^{-A_s(x)} +(1-y)\bigl(e^{-A_s(x)}-e^{-A_s(y)}\bigr)\\ &\ge(y-x)e^{-\ell/2}>0. \end{aligned} $$

For each class, form the upper boundary $H_s$ of the convex hull of
$$ \{(b_{s,i},v_{s,i})\}_i. $$
For a normalized class mean checking probability $y$, this boundary is exactly the maximum class value obtainable by mixing the endpoint policies. Indeed, all attainable mean-value pairs form that convex hull.

Write the upper-hull vertices as
$$ (x_{s,0},h_{s,0}),\ldots,(x_{s,J_s},h_{s,J_s}), \qquad x_{s,0}=0,\quad x_{s,J_s}=1. $$
The segment slopes
$$ \alpha_{s,j} =\frac{h_{s,j}-h_{s,j-1}}{x_{s,j}-x_{s,j-1}} $$
are nonincreasing by concavity and satisfy
$$ \alpha_{s,j}\ge e^{-\ell/2}>0. $$
Their budget capacities are the rational numbers
$$ C_{s,j}=\nu_s(x_{s,j}-x_{s,j-1}). $$
Their total is $1$.

Increasing a class mean traverses its hull segments in order. Its value increase is slope times budget allocated to each segment. Temporarily dropping the within-class ordering constraints gives a fractional-knapsack problem.

Sort all segments by nonincreasing slope, placing earlier segments of a class first when slopes tie. Filling this order automatically respects every within-class ordering constraint, since that class’s slopes are nonincreasing. It therefore yields a feasible solution to the original problem.

To verify optimality, suppose the greedy allocation has one partially filled segment of slope $\lambda$. Earlier segments are full and later segments are empty. For any competing allocation $t$ with the same total budget, subtracting objectives gives
$$ \sum_j(\alpha_j-\lambda)(t_j^{\mathrm{greedy}}-t_j)\ge0. $$
Each summand is nonnegative: earlier segments have both factors nonnegative, later segments have both factors nonpositive, and the partially filled segment has first factor zero. At a boundary, the same argument applies using an adjacent marginal slope. Positive slopes also imply that an optimum uses the full budget.

There are
$$ K=\sum_sJ_s\le N-m\le m(\min\{\tau,\ell\}+1) $$
segments. In the sorted order, let their slopes and capacities be $\alpha_j,C_j$, and put
$$ T_0=0,\qquad T_j=\sum_{i=1}^jC_i. $$
Then for $T_{r-1}\le k\le T_r$,
$$ V(k)=V(0)+\sum_{j<r}\alpha_jC_j +\alpha_r(k-T_{r-1}). $$

Thus $V$ is concave and piecewise affine with at most $K$ segments. Every $T_j$ is rational and has polynomial bit length, being a sum of polynomially many products of input masses and rational threshold differences.

This also gives a direct explanation of the atom bound: only the class containing the partially filled segment needs to mix two hull vertices.

Limitation. Computing this description exactly still requires ordering its exponential expressions. The polynomial number of segments does not resolve those comparisons.

5. Exact equality and a terminating exact decision algorithm

5.1 Equality of rational policy values

I use the following form of Lindemann–Weierstrass: if $\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_n$ are distinct algebraic numbers, then $e^{\beta_1},\ldots,e^{\beta_n}$ are linearly independent over the algebraic numbers. A self-contained proof is available in [Popescu’s paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14352).

For any rational atomic policy, group atoms having the same exponent:
$$ F(\rho)=1-\sum_{r\in R}\gamma_r e^{-r}, \qquad \gamma_r=\sum_{\substack{\text{atoms }(s,x)\\A_s(x)=r}} w_{s,x}(1-x). $$
Here $r,\gamma_r$ are nonnegative rationals. The hypotheses of the theorem hold because the grouped arguments $-r$ are distinct rational, hence algebraic, numbers.

Consequently, two rational atomic policies have equal values if and only if all their grouped coefficients $\gamma_r$ agree, interpreting missing coefficients as zero. Computing effort, grouping rational exponents, and checking these coefficients takes polynomial time in the encoded instances and policies.

Furthermore, if any $r>0$ has $\gamma_r>0$, the policy value is transcendental. Otherwise an algebraic value of $F(\rho)$ would give a nonzero algebraic linear relation among $e^0$ and the $e^{-r}$.

If all positive-exponent coefficients vanish, then
$$ F(\rho)=1-\gamma_0 =\sum_{s,x}w_{s,x}x, $$
because the total atom mass is $1$.

5.2 Classification of rational optimum values

If $\kappa=1$, checking everything gives $\mathrm{OPT}=1$.

If $\kappa=0$, nonnegative checking cost forces every class measure to be supported at $0$. Therefore
$$ \mathrm{OPT}=\sum_s\nu_s(1-e^{-A_s(0)}). $$
It equals $0$ if every $A_s(0)=0$, and is transcendental otherwise.

Now suppose $0<\kappa<1$. Say an effective reviewer is inducible below $1$ if some pair satisfies
$$ a_{st}>0,\qquad c_{ts}=0\ \text{or}\ c_{ts}<r_{ts}. $$
If no such pair exists, $A_s(x)=0$ for every $x<1$, while $f_s(1)=1$. Thus $f_s(x)=x$ everywhere and
$$ \mathrm{OPT}=\kappa. $$

If such a pair exists, then $\mathrm{OPT}>\kappa$. Start with
$$ \rho_s=\nu_s\bigl((1-\kappa)\delta_0+\kappa\delta_1\bigr). $$
Its cost is $\kappa$ and its value is
$$ \kappa+(1-\kappa)\sum_s\nu_sf_s(0). $$
This exceeds $\kappa$ if any scheduled effort is free.

Otherwise, some class has a threshold $z\in(0,1)$ with $A_s(z)>0$. Choose $h>0$ sufficiently small that
$$ h(1-z)\le\nu_s(1-\kappa),\qquad hz\le\nu_s\kappa. $$
Replace masses $h(1-z)$ at $0$ and $hz$ at $1$ by mass $h$ at $z$. This preserves cost and increases value by
$$ h(f_s(z)-z) =h(1-z)(1-e^{-A_s(z)})>0. $$

At a rational optimal vertex, a value exceeding $\kappa$ requires a positive-exponent coefficient, since otherwise the value would equal its checking cost. Hence this optimum is transcendental.

Finally, every feasible policy of checking cost $C$ satisfies
$$ F(\rho) \le1-e^{-\ell/2}(1-C) \le1-(1-\kappa)e^{-\ell/2}. $$
This is strictly below $1$ when $\kappa<1$.

The classification is therefore:

| Case | Optimum |
|---|---|
| $\kappa=1$ | $1$ |
| $\kappa=0$, all $A_s(0)=0$ | $0$ |
| $\kappa=0$, some $A_s(0)>0$ | Transcendental |
| $0<\kappa<1$, no effective reviewer inducible below $1$ | $\kappa$ |
| $0<\kappa<1$, some effective reviewer inducible below $1$ | Transcendental, strictly between $\kappa$ and $1$ |

All the rational cases are recognizable by rational input comparisons.

5.3 Decidability and gap-dependent time

Handle those rational cases directly. In every other case, the rational query $q$ differs from $\mathrm{OPT}$.

Run the approximation algorithm at successively finer precisions, obtaining
$$ \widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}} \le\mathrm{OPT} \le\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}+2^{-b}. $$
Return yes when $q\le\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}$ and no when
$q>\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}+2^{-b}$.

Since
$$ \Delta=|\mathrm{OPT}-q|>0, $$
one of these conditions holds once $2^{-b}<\Delta$. Doubling $b$ gives total running time polynomial in
$$ m,\tau,L,\log(\ell+1),1+\log(1/\Delta). $$
This proves exact decidability, and polynomial time under an explicit inverse-exponential gap promise. It does not give an unconditional input-length polynomial bound.

Indeed, take two equally weighted classes, two types of masses $1/2$, and $\ell=2^n$. Schedule only perfect, free-effort reviewers in the first class and only never-effort reviewers in the second. With $\kappa=0$,
$$ \mathrm{OPT}=\frac12(1-e^{-2^{n-1}}). $$
For $q=1/2$,
$$ \Delta=\frac12e^{-2^{n-1}}, $$
although the instance has length $O(n)$. The schedule is feasible by the realization argument below. Thus a uniform bound $\Delta\ge2^{-\operatorname{poly}(L)}$ is false. This particular comparison is nevertheless easy by positivity.

6. Zero budget is equivalent to positive exponential-sum comparison

Define positive exponential-sum comparison as follows: given positive rational $\lambda_s$ with total $1$, nonnegative rational $z_s$, and rational $h\in[0,1]$, decide
$$ \sum_s\lambda_s e^{-z_s}\le h. $$

The zero-budget restriction reduces to this problem immediately:
$$ \mathrm{OPT}=1-\sum_s\nu_s e^{-A_s(0)}, $$
so use $\lambda_s=\nu_s$, $z_s=A_s(0)$, and $h=1-q$.

I now prove the converse reduction, including all input entries and the feasibility promise.

6.1 Representing arbitrary nonnegative rational exponents

For each source exponent, write
$$ z_s=\frac{k_s}{2}+f_s,\qquad k_s=\lfloor2z_s\rfloor,\qquad 0\le f_s<\frac12. $$
If $k_s>0$, create an effective type dedicated to class $s$, with perfect reliability and reviewer count $k_s$.

If $f_s>0$, write $f_s=u_s/v_s$. Then
$$ 2f_s=\frac{2u_sv_s}{v_s^2}, \qquad 2u_sv_s<v_s^2. $$
Expand the integer $2u_sv_s$ in binary. A set bit at position $2j$ contributes
$$ \left(\frac{2^j}{v_s}\right)^2; $$
a set bit at position $2j+1$ contributes twice this square.

For each contribution, create a dedicated type with reliability
$$ p_t^1=\frac{1+2^j/v_s}{2} $$
and reviewer count $1$ or $2$, respectively. A contributing bit satisfies
$$ 2^{2j}\le2u_sv_s<v_s^2, $$
so $0<2^j/v_s<1$ and the reliability is admissible.

For every effective type, put $c_{ts}=r_{ts}=0$ in its dedicated class. It therefore exerts effort at $0$. The resulting exponent contribution is exactly
$$ \frac{k_s}{2}+f_s=z_s. $$

Let $D_s$ be the total effective reviewer count. Its integer part is $k_s$; its remaining part is polynomial in the source encoding length. Thus $D_s$ has polynomial binary length.

Choose a power of two $\ell>\max_sD_s$. Create one filler type with reliability $1$, with count $\ell-D_s$ in class $s$, and which never exerts effort.

Complete the input arrays explicitly as follows:

  • Every unspecified reviewer count is $0$.
  • Every cost-reward pair initially has $c_{ts}=1$, $r_{ts}=0$.
  • Only the dedicated effective pairs are changed to $c_{ts}=r_{ts}=0$.

Thus filler and unscheduled pairs have valid parameters. Identical peer types, if any arise, may be merged by adding their reviewer counts and masses.

Set
$$ \nu_s=\lambda_s,\qquad \mu_t=\frac1\ell\sum_s\nu_s a_{st}. $$
Every retained type has positive mass. Moreover,
$$ \sum_t\mu_t =\frac1\ell\sum_s\nu_s\sum_ta_{st} =1, $$
and the balance equations hold by definition.

The construction has polynomial support size and total binary encoding length. For source exponents in $[0,1/2]$, even the numerical value of $\ell$ is polynomially bounded. For unrestricted exponents, its binary length remains polynomial. If the source rationals are dyadic, all constructed input parameters are dyadic.

6.2 Distinct reviewers, no self-review, and prescribed loads

In fact, any rational schedule satisfying the balance equations admits a finite realization after sufficiently enlarging the population.

Choose a population size making all type and class counts integral. Give every peer $\ell$ outgoing reviewer stubs. Give each assignment in class $s$ exactly $a_{st}$ incoming stubs labelled by type $t$. For each type, balance makes the total incoming and outgoing stub counts equal, so they can be matched. This creates an $\ell$-regular bipartite multigraph.

An $\ell$-regular bipartite multigraph decomposes into $\ell$ perfect matchings. Indeed, for any subset $U$ on one side,
$$ \ell|U|\le\ell|N(U)|, $$
so Hall’s condition holds. Remove a perfect matching and repeat on the remaining regular multigraph.

Colour these matchings by $1,\ldots,\ell$. Replace every peer and its owned assignment by $\ell+1$ clones indexed modulo $\ell+1$. Lift an edge of colour $j$ from peer clone $k$ to assignment clone $k+j$.

Each clone has incoming and outgoing degree $\ell$. Every assignment clone retains its prescribed reviewer-type counts. Parallel base edges have different colours, hence different shifts, so no reviewer clone reviews the same assignment clone twice. A base self-edge has a nonzero shift and therefore never becomes self-review. A base edge between different owners cannot become self-review.

This verifies the feasibility promise without requiring the potentially large realizing population to be output.

6.3 Preservation of the decision

Set $\kappa=0$ and $q=1-h$. Then
$$ \mathrm{OPT} =1-\sum_s\lambda_s e^{-z_s}, $$
and hence
$$ \mathrm{OPT}\ge q \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_s\lambda_s e^{-z_s}\le h. $$

This proves polynomial-time equivalence of the zero-budget restriction and positive exponential-sum comparison. The constructed instances have no interior effort thresholds: all scheduled effective reviewers always exert effort, and fillers never do.

Taking one source term shows that scalar comparison of $e^{-z}$ with a rational is already embedded with $m=1$.

No hardness classification for this arithmetic comparison problem is proved here.

7. A more precise oracle upper bound for exact decision

The hull construction in Section 4 requires only restricted signed exponential comparisons.

For endpoints $i<j$ within a class, the slope is
$$ \frac{v_{s,j}-v_{s,i}}{b_{s,j}-b_{s,i}} = \frac{ (1-b_{s,i})e^{-A_s(b_{s,i})} -(1-b_{s,j})e^{-A_s(b_{s,j})} }{ b_{s,j}-b_{s,i} }. $$
Thus:

  • Comparing adjacent slopes in a hull construction involves at most three exponential terms, since they share an endpoint.
  • Comparing two arbitrary hull-segment slopes involves at most four exponential terms.

All coefficients and arguments are rational with polynomial bit length. A standard stack construction of each upper hull, followed by sorting its segments, uses $O(N\log N)$ such comparisons.

After greedy allocation, the resulting optimal policy has at most $m+1$ atoms. Its final comparison with $q$ is
