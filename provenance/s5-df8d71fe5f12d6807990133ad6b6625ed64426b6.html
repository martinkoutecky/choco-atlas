The continuous mirror question, as graded

Each period \(t\), draw an i.i.d. shock \(\omega_t\) from a finite rational distribution of support size \(\tau\). It specifies arriving worker masses \(a_H^{\omega_t},a_L^{\omega_t}\) and job masses \(a_h^{\omega_t},a_l^{\omega_t}\), with each side’s total mass equal to \(1\). Given rational joint match surpluses \(u_{xy}\), \(x\in\{H,L\}\), \(y\in\{h,l\}\), and per-period waiting cost \(c>0\), the planner chooses feasible match masses \(m_{xy,t}\geq 0\) from the available masses and carries unmatched mass into the next period’s queues \(q_{z,t+1}\). Given these inputs and \(\epsilon>0\), compute the optimal value of \(\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\sum_{x,y}m_{xy,t}u_{xy}-c\sum_{z\in\{H,L,h,l\}}q_{z,t+1}\right)\) within additive error \(\epsilon\), together with a stationary policy attaining that guarantee.

Let $D$ be a positive integer such that every arrival quantity is an integer multiple of $1/D$. One may take the product of their input denominators. Define
$$ K=\left\lceil\frac{U}{c}\right\rceil+3. $$

The following claims hold for every instance in the question, including signed surpluses:

  • An optimal stationary policy exists that matches congruent pairs immediately and then clips the remaining signed imbalance between two thresholds $a\leq 0\leq b$.
  • The thresholds can be chosen in

$$ [-K,K]\cap D^{-1}\mathbb Z. $$
Consequently, an optimal policy has a description of polynomial bit length and produces feasible rational actions in time polynomial in the current state’s bit length and the input length.

  • The exact optimal value is rational. It can be computed by a finite search in time polynomial in $\tau$, $L$, and $DK$.

The last algorithm is generally exponential in $L$, because $DK$ can be exponentially large. Thus these results establish neither of the requested complexity classifications.

1. Conservation and the terminal-inventory lemma

Write
$$ Q_t=q_{H,t}+q_{L,t}=q_{h,t}+q_{l,t}. $$
The equality follows inductively: arrivals add one unit on each side, and each match removes equal mass from both sides. The waiting cost in period $t$ is therefore $2cQ_{t+1}$.

We first justify removing terminal inventory terms, even when surpluses are negative.

Lemma. If $V(\pi)>-\infty$, then
$$ \frac{\mathbb E_\pi Q_{T+1}}{T}\longrightarrow 0. $$

Proof. Set
$$ M=\max\{0,u_{Hh},u_{Hl},u_{Lh},u_{Ll}\}, $$
and let $g_T$ denote the expected average welfare through period $T$. Cumulative matched mass is at most $T$, so
$$ g_T\leq M-\frac{2c}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T\mathbb E Q_{t+1}. $$
If $v=V(\pi)>-\infty$, then $v$ is finite, and $g_T\geq v-1$ for all sufficiently large $T$. Hence, for some finite constant $C$,
$$ \sum_{t=1}^T\mathbb E Q_{t+1}\leq CT $$
for all sufficiently large $T$.

Pathwise, $Q_{t+1}\leq Q_t+1$ and $Q_{T+1}\leq T$. It follows that
$$ Q_{t+1}\geq \bigl(Q_{T+1}-(T-t)\bigr)^+. $$
Summing and comparing with an integral gives
$$ \sum_{t=1}^TQ_{t+1} \geq \sum_{j=0}^{T-1}(Q_{T+1}-j)^+ \geq \int_0^T(Q_{T+1}-x)^+\,dx =\frac{Q_{T+1}^2}{2}. $$
Thus $\mathbb E Q_{T+1}^2\leq 2CT$, and Cauchy–Schwarz yields
$$ \frac{\mathbb E Q_{T+1}}{T} \leq \sqrt{\frac{2C}{T}}\longrightarrow 0. $$
This proof uses neither stationarity nor bounded inventories. $\square$

Define
$$ Z^\omega=a_h^\omega-a_H^\omega,\qquad \mu=\mathbb E Z, $$
and
$$ \alpha_H=u_{Hl},\qquad \alpha_L=u_{Ll},\qquad \beta_h=u_{Hh}-u_{Hl}. $$
Then
$$ u_{xy}=\alpha_x+\beta_y-U\mathbf 1_{\{(x,y)=(L,h)\}}, $$
where $\beta_l=0$. Set
$$ B=\mathbb E\!\left[ \alpha_Ha_H^\omega+\alpha_La_L^\omega+\beta_ha_h^\omega \right], $$
$$ \Phi(q)=\alpha_Hq_H+\alpha_Lq_L+\beta_hq_h, \qquad d_t=q_{H,t}-q_{h,t}. $$

Inventory conservation gives
$$ \sum_{t=1}^T\sum_{x,y}u_{xy}m_{xy,t} = \sum_{t=1}^T \left(\alpha_Ha_H^{\omega_t}+\alpha_La_L^{\omega_t} +\beta_ha_h^{\omega_t}\right) -\Phi(q_{T+1}) -U\sum_{t=1}^Tm_{Lh,t}. $$
Also,
$$ m_{Lh,t}-m_{Hl,t}=Z^{\omega_t}+d_{t+1}-d_t. $$

Introduce the positive constants
$$ \kappa=\frac U2,\qquad \eta=2c, $$
and write
$$ X_t=m_{Lh,t}+m_{Hl,t},\qquad \Psi(q)=\Phi(q)+\kappa(q_H-q_h),\qquad \overline R=B-\kappa\mu. $$
Combining the preceding identities gives the exact expected-welfare identity
$$ g_T= \overline R -\frac1T\mathbb E\sum_{t=1}^T \left(\kappa X_t+\eta Q_{t+1}\right) -\frac{\mathbb E\Psi(q_{T+1})}{T}. $$

Because $|d_t|\leq Q_t$, there is an input-dependent constant $C_\Psi$ such that
$$ |\Psi(q)|\leq C_\Psi Q. $$
The lemma therefore implies, for every finite-value policy,
$$ V(\pi)= \overline R- \limsup_{T\to\infty} \frac1T\mathbb E\sum_{t=1}^T \left(\kappa X_t+\eta Q_{t+1}\right). $$

Thus the relevant average optimization has nonnegative costs: cross-match cost $\kappa X_t$ and inventory cost $\eta Q_{t+1}$.

2. A scalar relaxation and its feasible realization

Suppose the signed inventory before arrivals is $d$, the observed imbalance is $Z$, and the signed inventory after matching is $e$. Put
$$ w=d-Z. $$
The inventory identity implies
$$ X_t\geq |w-e|, \qquad Q_{t+1}\geq |e|. $$
Consequently, every original action has transformed cost at least
$$ \ell(w,e)=\kappa|w-e|+\eta|e|. $$

Consider the auxiliary scalar problem allowing any real action $e$. Its state evolves from $d$ to $e$, after observing an independent copy of $Z$.

There is a particularly useful feasible realization. Define a canonical inventory associated with $d$ by
$$ q_H=q_l=d^+,\qquad q_L=q_h=(-d)^+. $$
After arrivals, greedily matching $H$ with $h$ and $L$ with $l$ leaves exactly
$$ H,l\text{ mass }w^+,\qquad L,h\text{ mass }(-w)^+. $$
If $e$ lies between $0$ and $w$, cross-matching the excess produces the canonical inventory associated with $e$. This action has exactly
$$ X=|w-e|,\qquad Q'=|e|, $$
so it attains the scalar cost $\ell(w,e)$.

The next sections prove that an average-optimal scalar policy always chooses such an $e$.

3. Discounted scalar functions: convexity and the rational grid

Fix a discount factor $\gamma\in(0,1)$. Define recursively
$$ J_{\gamma,0}(d)=0, $$
$$ J_{\gamma,n+1}(d)= \mathbb E\min_{e\in\mathbb R} \left[ \kappa|d-Z-e|+\eta|e|+\gamma J_{\gamma,n}(e) \right]. $$

Every $J_{\gamma,n}$ is convex and $\kappa$-Lipschitz. Indeed, convexity follows by taking the infimum over $e$ of a jointly convex function and then taking expectation. For Lipschitz continuity, changing $d$ to $d'$ changes the objective at any fixed $e$ by at most $\kappa|d-d'|$; taking infima preserves this bound.

We also need the following action restriction.

Segment lemma. If $J$ is $\kappa$-Lipschitz, every minimizer of
$$ \kappa|w-e|+\eta|e|+\gamma J(e) $$
lies between $0$ and $w$.

Proof. Suppose $w\geq0$. If $e<0$, comparison with $e=0$ gives
$$ \kappa|w-e|+\eta|e|+\gamma J(e) \geq \kappa w+\gamma J(0) +\bigl(\kappa+\eta-\gamma\kappa\bigr)|e|, $$
which is strictly larger than the cost at $0$. If $e>w$, comparison with $e=w$ similarly gives a strictly positive excess of at least
$$ \bigl(\kappa+\eta-\gamma\kappa\bigr)(e-w). $$
The case $w\leq0$ follows by reversing signs. $\square$

Thus all minima above are attained on compact segments. The functions increase with $n$, since all costs are nonnegative. Clearing the scalar inventory every period gives the bound
$$ J_{\gamma,n}(d) \leq \kappa\mathbb E|d-Z| +\frac{\gamma\kappa\mathbb E|Z|}{1-\gamma}. $$
Hence the pointwise limit $J_\gamma$ exists and is finite, convex, and $\kappa$-Lipschitz. The common Lipschitz bound implies uniform convergence on compact intervals. Passing to the limit in the compact-segment minima yields
$$ J_\gamma(d)= \mathbb E\min_e \left[ \kappa|d-Z-e|+\eta|e|+\gamma J_\gamma(e) \right]. $$

Let $\Delta=1/D$. Since $Z\in\Delta\mathbb Z$, these functions have an additional property:

Every $J_{\gamma,n}$, and also $J_\gamma$, is affine on each interval $[j\Delta,(j+1)\Delta]$.

Here is the required calculation. Let $F$ be convex and affine on every such interval, and define
$$ G(w)=\min_e\{\kappa|w-e|+F(e)\}. $$
If $F$ has slope $r$ on an open grid interval, then $G$ has slope
$$ \operatorname{clip}(r;-\kappa,\kappa), $$
where
$$ \operatorname{clip}(x;a,b)=\min\{b,\max\{a,x\}\}. $$

To verify this:

  • If $|r|\leq\kappa$, the supporting-line inequality for $F$ shows that $e=w$ minimizes the objective, so $G(w)=F(w)$.
  • If $r>\kappa$, the objective is strictly increasing in $e$ from that interval’s left endpoint onward. Every minimizer therefore lies at or to the left of that endpoint. On the interval, the minimum consequently has the form $\kappa w+\text{constant}$.
  • If $r<-\kappa$, the corresponding argument gives slope $-\kappa$.

Applying this calculation to
$$ F(e)=\eta|e|+\gamma J_{\gamma,n}(e) $$
and then translating by $Z\in\Delta\mathbb Z$ proves the property inductively. Uniform convergence on each grid interval proves it for $J_\gamma$.

4. Uniformly bounded discounted thresholds

Set
$$ N=\left\lceil\frac{4\kappa}{\eta}\right\rceil+1, \qquad K=N+2. $$
Thus $K=\lceil U/c\rceil+3$.

For a grid point $x$, let
$$ S_\gamma(x)= \frac{J_\gamma(x+\Delta)-J_\gamma(x)}{\Delta}. $$
Convexity and Lipschitz continuity imply
$$ -\kappa\leq S_\gamma(x)\leq\kappa. $$

Whenever $x-Z\geq0$, the preceding slope calculation gives the recurrence
$$ S_\gamma(x)= \mathbb E\operatorname{clip} \left(\eta+\gamma S_\gamma(x-Z);-\kappa,\kappa\right). $$
The analogous recurrence on negative grid intervals uses
$$ -\eta+\gamma S_\gamma(x-Z). $$

Assume
$$ \gamma\geq 1-\frac1{2N}. $$
Then $\eta+\gamma\kappa>\kappa$, because $N>4\kappa/\eta$.

Define
$$ r_0=-\kappa,\qquad r_{j+1}=\min\{\kappa,\eta+\gamma r_j\}. $$
We claim
$$ x\geq j\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S_\gamma(x)\geq r_j. $$
The case $j=0$ is the Lipschitz bound. For the inductive step, $Z\leq1$ implies
$$ x-Z\geq j-1\geq0, $$
so the positive-interval recurrence and the induction hypothesis give the assertion.

Because $\eta+\gamma\kappa>\kappa$, the recursion also has the explicit form
$$ r_j= \min\left\{ \kappa,\, \eta\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\gamma^i-\gamma^j\kappa \right\}. $$
For $i<N$, Bernoulli’s inequality gives
$$ \gamma^i\geq 1-\frac{i}{2N}\geq\frac12. $$
Therefore
$$ \eta\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\gamma^i-\gamma^N\kappa \geq \frac{\eta N}{2}-\kappa >\kappa, $$
and $r_N=\kappa$. Hence
$$ S_\gamma(x)=\kappa\qquad\text{for }x\geq N. $$

The negative-interval recurrence gives, by the same induction,
$$ S_\gamma(x)=-\kappa \qquad\text{if }x+\Delta\leq-N. $$
Thus $J_\gamma$ has slopes $\kappa$ and $-\kappa$ on its positive and negative tails.

Now define
$$ F_\gamma(e)=\eta|e|+\gamma J_\gamma(e). $$
Its tail slopes are $\eta+\gamma\kappa>\kappa$ and $-\eta-\gamma\kappa<-\kappa$. Consequently, both
$$ F_\gamma(e)+\kappa e \quad\text{and}\quad F_\gamma(e)-\kappa e $$
are coercive and attain their minima within $[-K,K]$.

Choose grid minimizers
$$ a_\gamma\in\operatorname*{argmin}_e \{F_\gamma(e)+\kappa e\}, $$
$$ b_\gamma\in\operatorname*{argmin}_e \{F_\gamma(e)-\kappa e\}. $$
Grid minimizers exist because these functions are affine on grid intervals. Moreover,
$$ a_\gamma\leq0\leq b_\gamma. $$
For example, on positive intervals, every slope of $F_\gamma+\kappa e$ is at least
$$ \eta+(1-\gamma)\kappa>0, $$
so its minimum cannot occur strictly above zero. The other assertion follows symmetrically.

The optimal scalar action is
$$ e=\operatorname{clip}(w;a_\gamma,b_\gamma). $$

For completeness, the threshold rule follows directly from convex supporting slopes. At $a_\gamma$, $-\kappa$ is a supporting slope of $F_\gamma$; at $b_\gamma$, $\kappa$ is one. At each point between them, convexity supplies a supporting slope in $[-\kappa,\kappa]$. These supporting inequalities show that clipping minimizes
$$ \kappa|w-e|+F_\gamma(e). $$
For instance, if $w<a_\gamma$, then for every $e$,
$$ F_\gamma(e)+\kappa|w-e| \geq F_\gamma(a_\gamma)-\kappa(e-a_\gamma)+\kappa(e-w) = F_\gamma(a_\gamma)+\kappa(a_\gamma-w). $$

We have therefore obtained discounted optimal thresholds in the finite set
$$ [-K,K]\cap\Delta\mathbb Z. $$

5. Passing to an average-cost certificate

Define
$$ f_\gamma(d)=J_\gamma(d)-J_\gamma(0), \qquad \lambda_\gamma=(1-\gamma)J_\gamma(0). $$
Then
$$ f_\gamma(0)=0,\qquad |f_\gamma(d)|\leq\kappa|d|, $$
and clearing every period gives
$$ 0\leq\lambda_\gamma\leq\kappa\mathbb E|Z|. $$

Take a sequence $\gamma\uparrow1$. There are only finitely many possible threshold pairs, so an infinite subsequence has one fixed pair $(a,b)$. Extract a further subsequence on which $\lambda_\gamma$ converges to some $\lambda$.

The values of $f_\gamma$ at each grid point are bounded. A diagonal subsequence therefore converges at all grid points. Since the functions are affine on grid intervals and share a Lipschitz bound, they converge uniformly on compact intervals to a convex, $\kappa$-Lipschitz function $f$, with $f(0)=0$.

Write
$$ P(w)=\operatorname{clip}(w;a,b). $$
The normalized discounted equation is
$$ \lambda_\gamma+f_\gamma(d)= \mathbb E\left[ \ell(d-Z,P(d-Z)) +\gamma f_\gamma(P(d-Z)) \right]. $$
Passing to the limit gives
$$ \lambda+f(d)= \mathbb E\left[ \ell(d-Z,P(d-Z))+f(P(d-Z)) \right]. $$

For each fixed $w$ and candidate $e$, discounted optimality also gives
$$ \ell(w,P(w))+\gamma f_\gamma(P(w)) \leq \ell(w,e)+\gamma f_\gamma(e). $$
Passing to the limit proves that $P(w)$ minimizes $\ell(w,e)+f(e)$. Thus we have the average-cost optimality equation
$$ \boxed{ \lambda+f(d)= \mathbb E\min_{e\in\mathbb R} \{\kappa|d-Z-e|+\eta|e|+f(e)\}. } $$

This equation will certify optimality against all original policies, including randomized and nonstationary ones.

6. Optimality in the original matching model

Define a stationary matching policy on a balanced available state $s$ as follows:
$$ w=s_H-s_h,\qquad e=P(w), $$
$$ m_{Hh}=\min\{s_H,s_h\},\qquad m_{Ll}=\min\{s_L,s_l\}, $$
$$ m_{Hl}=(w-e)^+,\qquad m_{Lh}=(e-w)^+. $$

Since $a\leq0\leq b$, the quantity $e$ lies between $0$ and $w$. The scalar realization in Section 2 therefore proves feasibility. The resulting inventory is canonical, with signed imbalance $e$, and
$$ Q'=|e|\leq K. $$
Starting empty, all subsequent signed states lie in
$$ [-K,K]\cap\Delta\mathbb Z. $$
The rule depends only on available masses.

Under this policy the transformed period cost is exactly $\ell(d_t-Z_t,d_{t+1})$. The average-cost equation therefore telescopes to
$$ \mathbb E\sum_{t=1}^T (\kappa X_t+\eta Q_{t+1}) = T\lambda+f(0)-\mathbb E f(d_{T+1}). $$
The terminal term is bounded by $\kappa K$, so the expected average transformed cost converges to $\lambda$. The inventory potential is also bounded. Hence
$$ V(\pi_{a,b})=\overline R-\lambda. $$
This argument establishes the limit from the specified empty initial state.

Now consider an arbitrary original policy with finite value. Conditional on its history before the current arrivals, the average-cost equation, together with
$$ X_t\geq|d_t-Z_t-d_{t+1}|, \qquad Q_{t+1}\geq|d_{t+1}|, $$
implies
$$ \lambda+f(d_t) \leq \mathbb E\left[ \kappa X_t+\eta Q_{t+1}+f(d_{t+1}) \,\middle|\,\text{history} \right]. $$
This inequality allows arbitrary actions after observing the profile and arbitrary policy randomization.

Summing and taking expectations gives
$$ \frac1T\mathbb E\sum_{t=1}^T (\kappa X_t+\eta Q_{t+1}) \geq \lambda-\frac{\mathbb E f(d_{T+1})}{T}. $$
Because $|f(d)|\leq\kappa|d|\leq\kappa Q$, the terminal-inventory lemma makes the last term vanish. The welfare identity then yields
$$ V(\pi)\leq\overline R-\lambda. $$
Policies with value $-\infty$ satisfy this bound trivially. Consequently,
$$ \boxed{ V^*=\overline R-\lambda=V(\pi_{a,b}). } $$

This proves the claimed optimal stationary threshold structure.

7. Exact finite computation and its bit-complexity limitation

The structural theorem supplies an explicit algorithm.

Enumerate every pair
$$ a,b\in[-K,K]\cap D^{-1}\mathbb Z, \qquad a\leq0\leq b. $$
For a fixed pair, the signed inventory is a finite Markov chain with transition
$$ d\longmapsto \operatorname{clip}(d-Z^\omega;a,b) $$
with probability $p_\omega$. Its expected transformed cost at state $d$ is
$$ r_{a,b}(d)= \sum_\omega p_\omega \left[ \kappa\left|d-Z^\omega- \operatorname{clip}(d-Z^\omega;a,b)\right| +\eta\left|\operatorname{clip}(d-Z^\omega;a,b)\right| \right]. $$
All transition probabilities and costs are rational.

To evaluate its average cost from zero, find the closed communicating classes. For each such class, solve the rational stationary-distribution equations. Solve the transient-state linear equations for the probabilities of eventually entering each class. The average cost is
$$ \lambda(a,b)= \sum_C h_C(0) \sum_{d\in C}\rho_C(d)r_{a,b}(d), $$
where $h_C(0)$ is the probability of reaching class $C$, and $\rho_C$ is its stationary distribution.

The finite-chain facts used here do not require aperiodicity. Transient states have vanishing average occupation, and within each finite irreducible class the Cesàro averages converge to its unique stationary distribution. One way to verify the latter is to take an accumulation point of the averaged distributions: its difference from its image under the transition matrix tends to zero, so it is stationary; irreducibility gives uniqueness. Thus the formula applies from the empty initial state even if the chain is reducible or periodic.

All these linear systems have rational solutions. Therefore $\lambda(a,b)$, and hence the resulting welfare
$$ \overline R-\lambda(a,b), $$
are rational. Selecting the best pair returns the exact optimum, because Section 6 proves that at least one enumerated pair is optimal.

There are at most $2DK+1$ states and at most $(DK+1)^2$ threshold pairs. Graph computations and exact rational linear algebra therefore give running time polynomial in
$$ \tau,\quad L,\quad DK. $$
The exact value may instead be rounded to the requested accuracy $\epsilon$.

Finally, $D$ and $K$ have $O(L)$ bits. Each optimal threshold is an integer multiple of $1/D$ with magnitude at most $K$, so it also has $O(L)$ bits. The stationary policy uses only a constant number of rational arithmetic operations, comparisons, and positive-part operations. Its evaluation time is polynomial in $L$ and the current state’s bit length.

What remains unresolved is the dependence on the number $DK$ of grid states. This number can be exponential in $L$. A short optimal policy description does not by itself provide a polynomial-time method for finding its thresholds or estimating its long-run value.
