The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given applicant types \(T\), house categories \(H\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(\beta_h\), does there exist a feasible mass assignment \(x_{t,B}\) to integral bundles \(B\) of size at most each type's capacity such that no feasible challenger, coupled to the incumbent assignment type by type, has greater mass of applicants who prefer it than mass who prefer the incumbent?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: the list of \(\tau\) applicant types with positive masses \(\mu_t\), each encoded as a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types.
There are \(m\) house categories \(H\), with nonnegative binary-rational per-capita supplies \(\beta_h\). Each type specifies a positive integer capacity \(c_t\), an acceptable subset \(H_t\subseteq H\), and positive integer ranks \(r_t(h)\) on that subset; lower ranks are better and ties are allowed. Houses have no preferences.
The admissible bundles are \(\mathcal B_t=\{B\subseteq H_t:|B|\le c_t\}\), including the empty bundle; a bundle contains at most one unit of each category. These families are implicit in the input.
For \(R\) the largest supplied rank (take \(R=0\) if all acceptable subsets are empty), define \(s_t(B)=(|\{h\in B:r_t(h)=i\}|)_{i=1}^{R}\). Type \(t\) prefers \(D\) to \(B\), written \(D\succ_t B\), exactly when \(s_t(D)\) is lexicographically larger; equal signatures are indifferent.
A feasible assignment consists of real masses \(x_{t,B}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{B\in\mathcal B_t}x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t\sum_{B\ni h}x_{t,B}\le\beta_h\) for every \(h\).
A coupled challenger consists of real masses \(z_{t,B,D}\ge0\), with \(B,D\in\mathcal B_t\), satisfying \(\sum_Dz_{t,B,D}=x_{t,B}\); its marginal \(y_{t,D}=\sum_Bz_{t,B,D}\) must be feasible under the same supplies.
Call \(x\) popular if every coupled challenger satisfies \(\sum_{t,B,D:D\succ_t B}z_{t,B,D}\le\sum_{t,B,D:B\succ_t D}z_{t,B,D}\). Thus each applicant contributes one vote, and indifferent mass contributes none.

Is deciding whether a popular feasible mass assignment exists solvable in polynomial time, or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard? State running times in \(m\), \(\tau\), the total input bit length \(L\), \(C=\max_t c_t\), and \(R\).

Discrete popular \(b\)-matching existence is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even with two ranks, applicant capacities at most two, and house capacities one ([Paluch](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1101.0021)).
For a different continuous comparison, popular random one-to-one assignments under independent lottery comparisons always exist and can be computed in polynomial time, including with equal treatment of equals ([Aziz, Brandt, and Stursberg](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~haziz/assign.pdf)). No result settling the arbitrary-coupling bundle problem above was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Undefined type preferences are specified by acceptable house subsets and integer ranks, with ties allowed; bundles are compared by lexicographic rank-count signatures, as in the discrete definition.
  • Integral bundles are subsets of acceptable categories, with no repeated category, reflecting the discrete model's simple edges; empty and undersized bundles remain admissible.
  • Applicant capacities are positive integers supplied in the input; supplies are nonnegative binary rationals and need not sum to one.
  • Assignment and coupling masses may be arbitrary nonnegative reals; only the input masses and supplies require rational encodings.
  • Coupling is quantified universally over all type-preserving joint mass assignments with the incumbent marginal fixed and a feasible challenger marginal.
  • Bundle families are represented implicitly by acceptability and capacity, rather than explicitly enumerated.
  • When there are no acceptable houses, the rank-signature length is zero and all admissible bundles are indifferent.

Result

The problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even when \(C=2\), \(R=2\), all nonempty types have the same mass, and all house categories have the same positive supply.

The essential step is an integral rounding theorem for popularity under arbitrary coupling. Discrete popularity alone does not establish popularity against fractional challengers, so the reduction also needs an explicit certificate for its yes-instances.

If \(R=0\), the assignment giving everyone the empty bundle is popular. The hardness concerns the general case.

1. A price certificate for popularity

Define the challenger’s vote by
\[ v_t(B,D)= \begin{cases} 1& D\succ_t B,\\ -1& B\succ_t D,\\ 0& \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]
For nonnegative house prices \(p_h\), write \(p(B)=\sum_{h\in B}p_h\). Call \(B\) *price-compatible* for type \(t\) if
\[ p(D)-p(B)\ge v_t(B,D) \qquad\text{for every }D\in\mathcal B_t. \tag{1} \]

Price-certificate lemma. A feasible assignment \(x\) is popular if and only if there are prices \(p_h\ge0\) such that:

  • every bundle with \(x_{t,B}>0\) satisfies (1);
  • every house with \(p_h>0\) has its supply fully used.

Proof. Fix \(x\). The maximum challenger advantage is the finite linear program
\[ \max_z\ \sum_{t,B,D}v_t(B,D)z_{t,B,D}, \]
subject to the row-marginal and challenger-supply constraints in the question. Its dual is
\[ \min_{a,p}\ \sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}a_{t,B}+\sum_h\beta_hp_h, \qquad a_{t,B}+p(D)\ge v_t(B,D),\quad p_h\ge0, \]
where the variables \(a_{t,B}\) are unrestricted.

The identity challenger is feasible and has advantage zero. Therefore popularity is equivalent to the primal optimum being zero. Strong duality applies: the primal is feasible and bounded, and the dual is feasible, for example with \(a_{t,B}=1\) and \(p_h=0\).

Let \(U_h(x)\) denote house usage. At an optimal dual solution of value zero,
\[ 0= \sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}\bigl(a_{t,B}+p(B)\bigr) +\sum_hp_h\bigl(\beta_h-U_h(x)\bigr). \]
Every summand is nonnegative: the first follows from the dual constraint with \(D=B\), and the second from feasibility. Consequently, occupied bundles satisfy \(a_{t,B}=-p(B)\), which gives (1), and positive-price houses are saturated.

Conversely, a certificate satisfying (1) bounds any coupled challenger by
\[ \sum_{t,B,D}v_t(B,D)z_{t,B,D} \le \sum_hp_hU_h(y)-\sum_hp_hU_h(x) \le0. \]
The last inequality uses challenger feasibility and saturation of positive-price houses. This proves the lemma.

Three consequences will be useful.

  • Every occupied nonempty bundle has price at most one: compare it with the empty bundle in (1).
  • If two price-compatible bundles are indifferent, they have equal prices. If one is strictly preferred, its price is exactly one greater. Both assertions follow by applying (1) in both directions.
  • Thus price-compatible bundles have at most two distinct signatures. When two signatures occur, their prices are respectively zero and one.

If every house supply is positive, a certificate additionally satisfies \(p_h\le1\). Indeed, a house priced above one belongs to no occupied bundle; its positive supply would then contradict required saturation.

2. Integral rounding of a popular mass assignment

We prove the rounding statement needed for the reduction. Work in rescaled units where each applicant has mass one and house supplies \(b_h\) are positive integers. An additional type accepting no houses can be omitted from this argument.

Rounding lemma. If a popular mass assignment exists in these units, then there is a feasible assignment choosing one deterministic bundle per applicant that satisfies the same price certificate. In particular, the resulting discrete assignment is popular.

The proof uses the following precise form of Edmonds’ matroid-intersection integrality theorem: for two matroids on the same finite ground set, the intersection of their independent-set polytopes is the convex hull of their common independent sets. See [Vondrák’s notes, Theorem 4](https://theory.stanford.edu/~jvondrak/CS369P/lec10.pdf).

2.1. The relevant bundle families are matroid bases

Fix the certificate prices. They belong to \([0,1]\), by the preceding lemma.

For each applicant, enlarge its occupied bundle family to include every minimum-price bundle with an occupied signature. This preserves price compatibility: both the signature and the price of the baseline bundle determine all inequalities (1).

We show that the enlarged family is a matroid base family, after adding one private dummy element when necessary.

One occupied signature

Let its rank counts be \(k_i\). A minimum-price bundle with this signature chooses the \(k_i\) cheapest houses independently within each rank.

Within a rank, this means including every house strictly cheaper than the cutoff price and choosing a fixed number from the houses tied at that price. This is a uniform-matroid base family together with mandatory elements. Taking the direct sum over ranks gives a matroid base family.

Two occupied signatures

The lower signature has price zero; the higher has price one. Put \(F=\{h:p_h=0\}\). A lower-signature bundle must be lexicographically best among bundles contained in \(F\), since a strictly better free bundle would violate (1).

First suppose \(|F|<c_t\). The lower bundle is exactly \(F\). Adding any acceptable house outside \(F\) improves it, so (1) requires every such house to have price one. A higher-signature bundle therefore consists of \(F\) and one house of the best rank available outside \(F\).

Add a private dummy \(d_t\) to the lower bundle. The padded family consists of \(F\) together with one choice from the best-rank priced houses and \(d_t\). These are matroid bases.

Now suppose \(|F|\ge c_t\). Let \(j_0\) be the worst rank appearing in a best free bundle. Define:

  • \(A\): all free houses of rank strictly better than \(j_0\);
  • \(J\): all free houses of rank \(j_0\);
  • \(q=c_t-|A|\).

The lower bundles are exactly \(A\) together with \(q\) houses from \(J\).

Every acceptable nonfree house of rank better than \(j_0\) has price one. Otherwise, replacing a rank-\(j_0\) house in a lower bundle would yield an improvement costing less than one, contrary to (1).

Any bundle strictly better than the lower signature must contain such a nonfree house. To verify this, suppose it contains none. At ranks better than \(j_0\), its counts cannot exceed those of a best free bundle. If those counts are equal, the capacity bound prevents an increase at rank \(j_0\); equality through that rank already uses all \(c_t\) slots.

A higher bundle has total price one. Hence it contains exactly one such price-one house, and all its other houses are free. Among these bundles, it must have the best signature: a better bundle of price one would violate (1) for the higher bundle.

Let \(P\) be the price-one houses of the best available rank better than \(j_0\). The higher bundles are therefore exactly \(A\), one house from \(P\), and \(q-1\) houses from \(J\).

The combined family consists of \(A\) together with a size-\(q\) subset of \(J\cup P\) containing at most one member of \(P\). It is the base family of a truncation of a partition matroid, together with the mandatory elements \(A\).

This proves the claimed matroid structure.

2.2. Rounding while preserving saturation

Use a separate ground element \((t,h)\) for each applicant-house pair, and include the private dummies just described.

Let \(M_1\) be the direct sum of the applicant matroids. Let \(M_2\) be the partition matroid imposing at most \(b_h\) selected elements associated with house \(h\); private dummies have their own unconstraining singleton blocks.

The expected incidence vector \(u\) of the padded mass assignment lies in the base polytope of \(M_1\) and the independent-set polytope of \(M_2\). By matroid-intersection integrality, \(u\) is a convex combination of common independent sets.

Write \(r\) for the rank of \(M_1\). Since \(u\) has total cardinality \(r\), every set appearing with positive coefficient in that combination has cardinality \(r\). Thus it contains a base of every applicant matroid.

Give \((t,h)\) weight \(p_h\) and every dummy weight zero. Certificate saturation gives
\[ p\cdot u=\sum_hp_hb_h. \]
Every common independent set has weight at most this quantity, by the house-capacity constraints. Its average weight equals that bound, so every set appearing with positive coefficient attains the bound. Each therefore saturates every positive-price house.

Choose one such set and remove its dummies. Its bundles are price-compatible, it is feasible, and it saturates the positive-price houses. The price-certificate lemma proves its popularity, completing the rounding proof.

3. Reduction from exact cover

Use the exact-cover-by-triples construction underlying [Paluch’s hardness proof](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1101.0021), but verify both directions for the present mass model.

The starting problem has a universe \(K\) of size \(3q\) and triples \(T_1,\ldots,T_k\), with every element occurring in two or three triples. This restricted exact-cover problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, as specified in Paluch’s proof. We may assume \(q>0\); counting incidences gives \(k\ge2q\).

Create the following houses:

  • \(v_e\) for each \(e\in K\);
  • \(h_i,h'_i\) for each triple;
  • \(g_1,\ldots,g_{k-q}\).

For \(T_i=\{e_{i1},e_{i2},e_{i3}\}\), create five applicants:

| Applicant | Capacity | Rank-one houses | Rank-two houses |
|---|---:|---|---|
| \(a_{ij}\), \(j=1,2,3\) | \(1\) | \(v_{e_{ij}}\) | \(h_i\) |
| \(a_{i4}\) | \(2\) | \(h'_i\) | \(h_i\) |
| \(a_{i5}\) | \(1\) | all \(g_\ell\) | \(h'_i\) |

Choose a power of two \(N>5k\). Give every listed applicant type mass \(1/N\), every house supply \(1/N\), and add an empty-acceptance type of mass \(1-5k/N\). Thus even an interpretation requiring dyadic rational masses is satisfied.

3.1. An exact cover gives a popular mass assignment

Suppose \(I\) indexes an exact cover.

For every \(i\), assign \(\{h_i,h'_i\}\) to \(a_{i4}\). For \(i\in I\), give all three applicants \(a_{ij}\) their element houses and give \(a_{i5}\) the empty bundle. For \(i\notin I\), leave the three applicants \(a_{ij}\) empty and assign \(a_{i5}\) one distinct \(g\)-house.

Every house is used exactly once in the rescaled units.

Set the prices of all \(v_e\) and \(g_\ell\) to one, and set
\[ (p_{h_i},p_{h'_i})= \begin{cases} (0,1)&i\in I,\\ (1,0)&i\notin I. \end{cases} \]

We check (1) for every occupied bundle.

  • The bundle of \(a_{i4}\) has price one and is strictly better than every alternative. All alternative prices are nonnegative, so the required inequality holds.
  • For \(i\in I\), applicant \(a_{ij}\) receives its rank-one house at price one. Its rank-two alternative and the empty bundle have price zero.
  • For \(i\notin I\), applicant \(a_{ij}\) receives the empty bundle, and both nonempty alternatives have price one.
  • For \(i\in I\), applicant \(a_{i5}\) receives the empty bundle, and every nonempty alternative has price one.
  • For \(i\notin I\), applicant \(a_{i5}\) receives a rank-one \(g\)-house at price one. Other \(g\)-houses are indifferent and have the same price; its rank-two alternative and the empty bundle have price zero.

The empty-acceptance type contributes no votes. All positive-price houses are saturated. The price-certificate lemma therefore proves popularity against every fractional coupled challenger.

3.2. A popular mass assignment forces an exact cover

Suppose a popular mass assignment exists. Multiply masses and supplies by \(N\), and omit the empty-acceptance type. Each remaining applicant has mass one, and each house has supply one. The rounding lemma produces a discrete popular assignment.

We show directly that such an assignment determines an exact cover.

First, every \(a_{i4}\) receives \(h'_i\). If \(h'_i\) is unused, giving it to \(a_{i4}\) yields one improving vote. Otherwise, if it is not assigned to \(a_{i4}\), it is assigned to \(a_{i5}\).

Transfer \(h'_i\) to \(a_{i4}\) and give \(a_{i5}\) a \(g\)-house. If a \(g\)-house is unused, this creates two improving votes and no worsening vote. If all are used, take one from another applicant \(a_{j5}\); there are still two improving votes and only one worsening vote. At least one \(g\)-house exists because \(k-q>0\). All moves respect capacities. Popularity rules out every case except assigning \(h'_i\) to \(a_{i4}\).

Second, every \(a_{i4}\) also receives \(h_i\). If \(h_i\) is unused, adding it improves \(a_{i4}\). Otherwise it is held by some \(a_{ij}\). Transfer \(h_i\) to \(a_{i4}\) and give \(a_{ij}\) its rank-one element house. Taking that element house from its current owner, if necessary, produces two improving votes and at most one worsening vote. Thus \(h_i\) must already belong to \(a_{i4}\).

Consequently, each \(a_{ij}\) receives either its element house or the empty bundle, and each \(a_{i5}\) receives either a \(g\)-house or the empty bundle.

Every element house is used. Otherwise, an adjacent \(a_{ij}\) is empty and could receive it, yielding one improving vote. Every \(g\)-house is used as well: there are fewer \(g\)-houses than applicants \(a_{i5}\), so an unused \(g\)-house could be given to an empty such applicant.

Therefore exactly \(3q\) applicants among the \(a_{ij}\) receive element houses, and exactly \(q\) applicants among the \(a_{i5}\) are empty.

If \(a_{i5}\) is empty, all three applicants \(a_{ij}\) must receive their element houses. Otherwise, give \(h_i\) to an empty \(a_{ij}\) and \(h'_i\) to \(a_{i5}\), removing both from \(a_{i4}\). This yields two improving votes and one worsening vote, contradicting popularity.

The \(q\) indices with empty \(a_{i5}\) thus specify \(q\) triples whose \(3q\) element houses are assigned without repetition. These triples form an exact cover.

This completes the hardness proof. The construction has \(C=2\), \(R=2\), \(\tau=5k+1\), and \(m=3k+2q\), and its bit length is polynomial in the exact-cover input size.

4. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\) and running times

4.1. Polynomial-size certificates exist

Start with any popular assignment, possibly with irrational masses. Let \(S\) be its finite bundle support, and let \(T\) be the positive-price houses in a price certificate.

The price constraints (1) for bundles in \(S\), together with nonnegativity and \(p_h=0\) outside \(T\), form a nonempty rational polyhedron. Its coefficients belong to \(\{-1,0,1\}\), and its right-hand sides belong to \(\{-1,0,1\}\). It has a rational vertex whose coordinate bit lengths are \(O(m\log(m+1))\), by the determinant bound for a basic solution.

Next, restrict assignment variables to \(S\), enforce the type masses and house bounds, and require saturation of houses in \(T\). This nonempty bounded rational polytope has a vertex with at most
\[ K_0=\tau+m \]
positive bundle variables. Indeed, more positive variables would have linearly dependent columns in the matrix of the type and house constraints, allowing a nonzero perturbation in both directions.

The vertex masses have polynomial bit length in \(L,\tau,m\). Together with the rational prices, they give a polynomial-size certificate.

4.2. Implicit bundle families can be checked in polynomial time

For each type, retain only its distinct supplied rank labels, in increasing order. Let \(a_i(k)\) be the sum of the \(k\) cheapest house prices at rank \(i\).

For a claimed occupied bundle with signature counts \(b_i\):

  • the cheapest indifferent bundle costs \(\sum_i a_i(b_i)\);
  • if the bundle is nonempty, the cheapest strictly worse bundle is the empty bundle, costing zero;
  • the cheapest strictly better bundle costs

\[ \min_j\left(\sum_{i<j}a_i(b_i)+a_j(b_j+1)\right), \tag{2} \]
where the minimum includes only indices satisfying \(b_j+1\le |H_{t,j}|\) and \(\sum_{i<j}b_i+b_j+1\le c_t\). An empty minimum means that no better bundle exists.

To justify (2), a better bundle has a first differing rank \(j\), equal counts at earlier ranks, and at least \(b_j+1\) houses at rank \(j\). Nonnegative prices allow us to discard later-rank houses and all excess houses at rank \(j\). Choosing the cheapest required houses attains the resulting lower bound.

These three computations verify every inequality (1). After preprocessing, verification uses
\[ O\!\left(\tau m\log(m+1)+K_0m\right) \]
rational arithmetic operations, plus the feasibility checks. The overall bit running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Capacities may be clipped at \(m\); rank labels are compared directly, so there is no iteration through \(C\) or \(R\).

Thus the general problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\), completing the proof of \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.

4.3. A deterministic upper bound

Put
\[ \bar C=\min(C,m), \qquad B=\sum_{j=0}^{\bar C}\binom mj. \]

Enumerate at most \(\tau+m\) candidate occupied bundles and a subset \(T\subseteq H\). For each choice, test linear feasibility of the assignment equations, saturation on \(T\), prices zero outside \(T\), and inequalities (1). The preceding minimum-price computations give a polynomial-time separation oracle for these inequalities.

This yields a deterministic running-time bound
\[ 2^m(\tau B)^{\tau+m}\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L). \]

In particular, no algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,C,R\) exists unless \(\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}\), since hardness already holds for \(C=2\) and \(R=2\).
