The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given two disjoint candidate sets \(C_1,C_2\) and binary-rational masses \(\mu_c\) over candidate-favorite voter types, with \(\sum_c \mu_c=1\), each party partitions its candidates into lists. A list \(\ell\) has mass \(\mu(\ell)=\sum_{c\in\ell}\mu_c\); using quota \(1/2\), it receives \(\min\{|\ell|,\lfloor 2\mu(\ell)\rfloor\}\) first-round seats, and remaining seats are allocated by the paper’s largest-remainder rule with ties split by lottery. Does there exist a pair of list partitions that is a pure Nash equilibrium and gives party 1 expected payoff \(2\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C_1,C_2\) be disjoint nonempty finite candidate sets, with \(m=|C_1|+|C_2|\), and fix two seats. Each voter type has one favorite candidate and votes for the list containing that candidate.
The society is given by its support: the list of the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, each mass a binary rational, summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Candidates absent from the support have mass \(0\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
A strategy for party \(i\) is a partition of \(C_i\) into nonempty lists; candidates are indivisible. For each list \(\ell\), set \(\mu(\ell)=\sum_{c\in\ell}\mu_c\) and award \(a_\ell=\min\{|\ell|,\lfloor2\mu(\ell)\rfloor\}\) first-round seats, using quota \(1/2\).
Set \(R=2-\sum_\ell a_\ell\). Only lists with \(|\ell|>a_\ell\) remain eligible. Rank these by \(r_\ell=\mu(\ell)-a_\ell/2\), and award one additional seat to each of the highest-ranked \(\min\{R,\text{number of eligible lists}\}\) lists. At a cutoff tie, choose uniformly among subsets of the tied lists of the required size. Any unfillable seats remain vacant.
Each party's payoff is its expected number of seats. The decision problem asks whether some pure Nash equilibrium gives party \(1\) payoff exactly \(2\).

Is this decision problem solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For integer vote counts, the corresponding two-seat equilibrium decision problem is NP-complete; that formulation uses quota \(\lfloor V/2\rfloor\), where \(V\) is the total vote count. [Ding and Lin (2014)](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/download/8888/8747)
No result settling the exact normalized rational-mass formulation above was identified in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Required both candidate sets and every list to be nonempty, as in the discrete strategy model.
  • Retained zero-mass candidates in the candidate sets while listing only positive-mass voter types in the society's support.
  • Made explicit that candidates remain indivisible and each favorite type votes entirely for the list containing its candidate.
  • Specified that exhausted lists are excluded, each eligible list receives at most one second-round seat, and seats remain vacant if eligible lists are insufficient.
  • Specified a uniform lottery among subsets at the cutoff tie, reproducing the discrete expected-seat formula.

Prover output (gpt-6.1-sol / high, Bolzano prover prompt)

Result. The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete, even when party \(2\) has exactly one candidate, every candidate has positive mass, and every mass is a dyadic rational. Consequently, it has no algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\).

The proof below applies directly to the stipulated quota \(1/2\). It uses PARTITION, also used in [Ding and Lin’s integer-model proof, Theorem 2](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/8888/8888-13-12416-1-2-20201228.pdf).

1. A polynomially verifiable equilibrium certificate

Write \(p=\mu(C_1)\) and \(q=\mu(C_2)=1-p\). Denote partitions by \(\Pi_1,\Pi_2\), and let \(U_2=\{C_2\}\) be party \(2\)’s unified partition.

First, every outcome allocates at most two seats. Indeed,

\(\displaystyle \sum_\ell a_\ell\leq \sum_\ell 2\mu(\ell)=2,\)

and the second round allocates at most the remaining number of seats. Thus both payoffs are nonnegative and at most \(2\).

Lemma 1. There exists a pure Nash equilibrium giving party \(1\) payoff \(2\) if and only if there exists a partition \(\Pi_1\) giving party \(1\) payoff \(2\) against \(U_2\).

*Proof.* Fix \(\Pi_1\), and write

\(\displaystyle F=\sum_{\ell\in\Pi_1}a_\ell,\qquad E=\{\ell\in\Pi_1:|\ell|>a_\ell\}.\)

Suppose first that \(q<1/2\). Under every partition of party \(2\), its lists receive no first-round seats, remain eligible, and have remainders at most \(q\). Consequently the number of second-round seats is \(R=2-F\).

Against \(U_2\), party \(2\)’s sole list has remainder exactly \(q\). It receives zero expected seats precisely when

\[\displaystyle \bigl|\{\ell\in E:r_\ell>q\}\bigr|\geq R. \tag{1}\]

To justify necessity, suppose fewer than \(R\) eligible party \(1\) lists have remainder strictly greater than \(q\). If fewer than \(R\) lists in total are eligible, party \(2\)’s list is selected. Otherwise the cutoff remainder is at most \(q\). A cutoff below \(q\) selects party \(2\)’s list certainly; a cutoff equal to \(q\) selects it with positive probability under the stipulated uniform rule. In either case its expected payoff is positive. Sufficiency follows because the \(R\) strictly higher party \(1\) lists fill every remaining seat.

Moreover, condition (1) guarantees party \(1\) both seats against every opposing partition: every opposing remainder is at most \(q\).

Now suppose \((\Pi_1,\Pi_2)\) is an equilibrium with party \(1\)’s payoff \(2\). Party \(2\)’s payoff is therefore zero, so its deviation to \(U_2\) must also give zero. Necessarily \(q<1/2\), since otherwise \(U_2\) receives at least one first-round seat. Condition (1) then holds, and party \(1\) receives both seats against \(U_2\).

Conversely, suppose \(\Pi_1\) gives party \(1\) payoff \(2\) against \(U_2\). Again \(q<1/2\), and condition (1) holds. Party \(2\) therefore receives zero against every deviation. Party \(1\), already receiving the maximum possible payoff, cannot improve. Hence \((\Pi_1,U_2)\) is an equilibrium. \(\square\)

A certificate consists solely of the list label assigned to each candidate in \(C_1\), using \(O(m\log(m+1))\) bits. The verifier computes \(q,F\), eligibility, and the count in (1).

For an explicit bit-complexity bound, put

\(\displaystyle B=L+m\lceil\log_2(m+1)\rceil+1.\)

The product of the input rational denominators has \(O(L)\) bits. Using this common denominator, schoolbook integer arithmetic verifies the certificate in

\(\displaystyle O\bigl((m+\tau)B^2\bigr)\)

bit operations. Candidates absent from the support receive mass zero. This proves membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\), with no enumeration of opposing strategies or possible voter types.

2. The critical subset-sum interval

Lemma 2. Suppose \(2/3<p\leq3/4\). A desired equilibrium exists if and only if some subset \(S\subseteq C_1\) satisfies

\[\displaystyle q<\mu(S)<p-q. \tag{2}\]

*Proof.* By Lemma 1, it suffices to consider play against \(U_2\).

For any party \(1\) partition,

\(\displaystyle F\leq2p\leq3/2.\)

Since \(F\) is an integer, it is either zero or one.

If \(F=1\), the sum of the remainders of all party \(1\) lists, including ineligible ones, is

\(\displaystyle \sum_{\ell\in\Pi_1}r_\ell=p-\frac12\leq1-p=q.\)

Every remainder is nonnegative, because \(a_\ell\leq2\mu(\ell)\). Therefore no eligible party \(1\) list has remainder strictly greater than \(q\). Condition (1) fails, so party \(1\) cannot receive both seats.

Thus a successful partition must have \(F=0\). Condition (1) then requires two distinct party \(1\) lists with masses \(x>q\) and \(y>q\). Since \(x+y\leq p\),

\(\displaystyle q<x\leq p-y<p-q.\)

Taking either list as \(S\) proves necessity of (2).

Conversely, suppose (2) holds. The two lists \(S\) and \(C_1\setminus S\) both have mass greater than \(q\), so both are nonempty. Their masses are also strictly less than

\(\displaystyle p-q=2p-1\leq1/2.\)

Hence neither receives a first-round seat. Both are eligible and strictly outrank party \(2\)’s unified list, so each receives one second-round seat. Lemma 1 yields the desired equilibrium. \(\square\)

3. A reduction using only dyadic masses

We reduce from the standard \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete PARTITION problem: given positive binary integers \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\), decide whether some index subset has sum \(W/2\), where

\(\displaystyle W=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i.\)

Choose \(D\) to be the smallest power of two satisfying \(D\geq16W^2\), and define

\(\displaystyle K=\left\lceil\frac{2D}{3W}\right\rceil,\qquad t=3KW-2D.\)

These quantities satisfy

\[\displaystyle 0<t<3W<K. \tag{3}\]

Indeed, the ceiling gives \(0\leq t<3W\). Equality \(t=0\) would imply \(3KW=2D\), impossible because \(2D\) is a power of two. Finally,

\(\displaystyle K\geq\frac{2D}{3W}\geq\frac{32W}{3}>3W.\)

Construct the election with

\(\displaystyle C_1=\{c_1,\ldots,c_n\},\qquad C_2=\{d\},\)

and masses

\(\displaystyle \mu_{c_i}=\frac{Ka_i}{D},\qquad \mu_d=\frac{D-KW}{D}.\)

Give each candidate one supported voter type favoring that candidate. Thus \(\tau=m=n+1\).

Party \(1\)’s total mass is \(p=KW/D\). By (3) and the definition of the ceiling,

\(\displaystyle \frac23<p<\frac23+\frac WD\leq\frac23+\frac1{16W}\leq\frac{35}{48}<\frac34.\)

Consequently all constructed masses are positive, sum exactly to one, and are dyadic. Lemma 2 applies.

For a subset \(S\subseteq C_1\), let \(s=\sum_{c_i\in S}a_i\). Its mass is \(Ks/D\). The interval condition (2) becomes

\(\displaystyle D-KW<Ks<2KW-D.\)

Subtracting the midpoint and doubling gives the equivalent condition

\(\displaystyle |2Ks-KW|<3KW-2D=t,\)

or

\[\displaystyle K|2s-W|<t. \tag{4}\]

Since \(2s-W\) is an integer and \(0<t<K\), condition (4) holds exactly when \(2s=W\). Therefore

\(\displaystyle \text{the PARTITION instance is yes}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{the constructed election has the requested equilibrium}.\)

This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness. In particular, the construction respects indivisibility of candidates and the exact quota \(1/2\); it does not replace either by an approximation.

4. Encoding size and conclusion

Minimality of \(D\) gives \(16W^2\leq D<32W^2\). Hence \(D,K\), and every mass numerator have \(O(\log(W+1))\) bits. The output length is

\(\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{out}}=O\bigl((n+1)(\log(W+1)+\log(n+1))\bigr).\)

The construction takes \(O((n+1)(\log(W+1))^2)\) bit operations using schoolbook arithmetic, and therefore is polynomial in the PARTITION input length.

Together with the verifier from Section 1, this establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness for the stated support representation. Hardness already holds with \(\tau=m\), so it does not depend on implicitly encoding additional voter types.
