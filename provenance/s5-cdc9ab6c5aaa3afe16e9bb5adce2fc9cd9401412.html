Theorem 2 — class: hardness

The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compatible type pairs, and strict rankings over compatible partner types with being unmatched ranked last, decide whether the atomless population \(U=\bigsqcup_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t]\) admits a dominant popular matching, assuming it admits a stable matching. Matchings are measurable pairings of individuals, and \(\Delta(M,N)\) is the measure of individuals who prefer their assignment in \(M\) to their assignment in \(N\), minus the measure who prefer \(N\). Popularity requires \(\Delta(M,N)\ge 0\) for every feasible \(N\); dominance additionally requires \(\Delta(M,N)>0\) whenever \(N\) matches strictly more population mass than \(M\). Stability means there are no compatible positive-mass subpopulations that mutually prefer one another to their current assignments.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

A society is given by its support: a nonempty list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with positive binary rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types. Compatibility is symmetric and specified by \(m\) unordered type pairs, possibly including equal types; each type strictly ranks its compatible partner types above unmatched status. Individuals are indifferent between partners of the same type. Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).
The population is \(U=\bigsqcup_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t]\), with Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\). A feasible matching \(M\) is a measurable, measure-preserving involution on a measurable subset \(D_M\subseteq U\), pairing distinct individuals of compatible types; individuals outside \(D_M\) are unmatched. All conditions are interpreted up to null sets, and matched population mass is \(s(M)=\lambda(D_M)\).
Write \(a_M(u)\) for the partner type of \(u\), or unmatched status, and define \(\Delta(M,N)=\lambda\{u:a_M(u)\succ_u a_N(u)\}-\lambda\{u:a_N(u)\succ_u a_M(u)\}\). A matching is popular if \(\Delta(M,N)\geq0\) for every feasible \(N\), and dominant if it is popular and \(\Delta(M,N)>0\) whenever \(s(N)>s(M)\).
A matching is stable if there are no compatible types \(t,s\) and disjoint measurable positive-mass sets \(A\subseteq U_t\), \(B\subseteq U_s\) such that every individual in \(A\) strictly prefers partner type \(s\) to its assignment and every individual in \(B\) strictly prefers partner type \(t\). Inputs are promised to admit a stable matching; no stable matching is supplied.

Is deciding whether such a society admits a dominant popular matching solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard under the stable-existence promise?

For finite roommates instances with strict preferences, dominant-matching existence is NP-hard even when a stable matching exists ([Kavitha](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.00141)).
No result settling this exact atomless, type-based dominant-matching problem was located.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made measurable pairings measure-preserving involutions on the matched subset, excluding self-pairing and interpreting conditions up to null sets, to conserve population mass.
  • Allowed compatibility between equal types when explicitly listed; this permits distinct individuals of that type to pair, while preferences depend only on partner type.
  • Made compatibility symmetric and specified indifference between individual partners of the same type.
  • Formalized positive-mass blocking using disjoint measurable subpopulations, including when both have the same type.
  • Treated stable existence as an input promise, without requiring a supplied stable matching.
  • Defined m as the number of compatible unordered type pairs and L as the total input bit length.

Result

The problem is $\mathrm{NP}$-hard under the stable-existence promise. This holds even when:

  • all masses are equal dyadic rationals;
  • no type is compatible with itself;
  • the promised stable matching assigns each type a single partner type or unmatched status.

The key is a gadget that replaces an unmatched vertex in the finite reduction while preserving its zero popularity-certificate value. The atomless rounding argument needed for the reverse direction is proved below.

The problem also belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$, without the stability promise.

1. The finite reduction used

We use the following properties of Kavitha’s reduction from positive $1$-in-$3$ SAT. From a formula $\varphi$, it constructs in polynomial time a loopless strict roommates instance $G_0$ on
$$ X\sqcup Y\sqcup\{z\}. $$

Its relevant properties are:

  • $G_0$ has a stable matching $S_0$ leaving $z$ unmatched.
  • $\varphi$ is satisfiable exactly when $G_0$ has a popular matching covering $X\cup Y$. In the satisfiable case, such a matching $N_0$ has a vertex certificate $\alpha$ satisfying

$$ \alpha_z=0,\qquad \alpha_v\geq c_v,\qquad \alpha_u+\alpha_v\geq w_{uv}, $$
together with $\alpha_u+\alpha_v=0$ on its matched edges.

  • A popular matching leaving $z$ unmatched but failing to cover $X\cup Y$ has a larger tied matching that also leaves $z$ unmatched.

Here $w_{uv}$ is the sum of the endpoints’ votes for edge $\{u,v\}$ against their current assignments, and $c_v$ is the vote for becoming unmatched.

Property 2 follows from Theorem 4 and the witness constructed in Section 4.2. Property 3 is the tied augmentation in Lemma 12; stable existence is established immediately before that lemma. [Kavitha, Sections 4–5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.00141).

We will apply these properties only after checking their stated hypotheses.

2. Atomless certificates and support restriction

This section concerns instances without self-compatible types, which suffice for the reduction.

Write $\bot$ for unmatched status. For a matching $M$, define its positive assignment classes by
$$ C_{t,a}=\{u\in U_t:a_M(u)=a\},\qquad p_{t,a}=\lambda(C_{t,a})>0. $$

Let $H$ be their index set. Measure preservation gives
$$ p_{t,s}=p_{s,t}. $$

Conversely, nonnegative assignment masses satisfying these symmetry equations and
$$ \sum_a p_{t,a}=\mu_t $$
are realizable: partition each type interval into the prescribed classes and pair reciprocal classes using measure-preserving bijections.

The measure-theoretic fact used here is that equal-measure measurable subsets of bounded intervals are isomorphic modulo null sets as atomless finite Lebesgue spaces. Pairing a bijection with its inverse gives the required involution.

2.1 Exact representation of opponents

For statuses $a,b$, let $v_t(b,a)$ be $1$, $0$, or $-1$ according as type $t$ prefers $b$ to $a$, is indifferent, or prefers $a$ to $b$.

For classes $h=(t,a)$ and $k=(s,b)$ with compatible underlying types, put
$$ w_{hk}=v_t(s,a)+v_s(t,b),\qquad c_h=v_t(\bot,a). $$

An opponent is represented by nonnegative pair masses $y_{hk}$ and unmatched masses $z_h$, satisfying
$$ \sum_{k:\{h,k\}\text{ allowed}}y_{hk}+z_h=p_h \qquad(h\in H). $$

Denote this polytope by $Q(p)$. Every opponent produces such a vector. Conversely, partitioning each class according to these variables and pairing reciprocal pieces realizes every vector in $Q(p)$.

Thus, exactly,
$$ V(y,z)=\sum_{\{h,k\}}w_{hk}y_{hk}+\sum_h c_hz_h =\Delta(N,M), $$
and
$$ \sigma(y)=2\sum_{\{h,k\}}y_{hk}=s(N). $$

The polytope is nonempty and compact. Its point representing $M$ has vote value zero.

2.2 Popularity and dominance certificates

Popularity is equivalent to
$$ \max_{Q(p)}V=0. $$

By linear-programming duality and complementary slackness, this holds exactly when there are real numbers $\alpha_h$ satisfying
$$ \alpha_h+\alpha_k\geq w_{hk},\qquad \alpha_h\geq c_h, $$
with
$$ \alpha_{(t,s)}+\alpha_{(s,t)}=0 $$
on every positive matched type pair, and
$$ \alpha_{(t,\bot)}=0 $$
on every positive unmatched class.

Indeed, the dual objective is $\sum_h p_h\alpha_h$. The displayed equalities make that objective zero.

For a popular $M$, dominance is equivalent to
$$ \max\{\sigma(y):(y,z)\in Q(p),\ V(y,z)=0\}=s(M). $$

The dual of this problem has unrestricted variables $\beta_h,\gamma$ and constraints
$$ \beta_h+\beta_k+\gamma w_{hk}\geq2,\qquad \beta_h+\gamma c_h\geq0. $$

Complementary slackness at the point representing $M$ gives
$$ \beta_{(t,s)}+\beta_{(s,t)}=2 $$
on positive matched type pairs, and
$$ \beta_{(t,\bot)}=0 $$
on positive unmatched classes.

Conversely, those equalities make the dual objective
$$ \sum_h p_h\beta_h=s(M), $$
so they exclude a larger zero-vote opponent. Together with popularity, they prove dominance.

All applications of strong duality above have feasible, bounded primal problems.

Support-restriction lemma. If a popular matching exists with positive assignment support $H$, then every feasible matching whose positive assignment support is contained in $H$ is popular. If the original matching is dominant, every such matching is dominant.

Proof. Restrict the certificates to the retained assignment labels. All inequalities remain valid. Reciprocal masses and the retained matched-pair equalities make the restricted popularity objective zero and the restricted dominance objective equal to the new matched mass. Weak duality proves both assertions. The new numerical masses need not equal the old masses. $\square$

3. Rounding under equal masses and a pure stable matching

A matching is pure if every type has only one positive assignment status. With equal type masses and no self-compatible types, pure matchings correspond to ordinary finite matchings on the type graph.

We prove the following bridge.

Rounding theorem. Suppose all type masses equal $q$, the instance has no self-compatible types, and it admits a pure stable matching. If it admits an atomless dominant matching, it admits a pure atomless dominant matching.

3.1 At most two statuses

Lemma. Every popular matching gives each type at most two positive assignment statuses.

Proof. Suppose a type has three positive classes with statuses
$$ a_1\succ_t a_2\succ_t a_3. $$

Choose subsets of equal positive mass $\varepsilon$ from these classes. Let $\pi$ cyclically permute the subsets, mapping the first to the second, the second to the third, and the third to the first, and preserve every individual’s type.

Conjugating $M$ by $\pi$ preserves feasibility. The second and third subsets improve, while the first worsens. All other assignment types remain unchanged. Therefore
$$ \Delta(\pi M\pi^{-1},M)=\varepsilon>0, $$
contradicting popularity. $\square$

3.2 Certificate values at a split type

Suppose type $t$ receives two statuses $a\succ_t b$ in a popular matching. Then every popularity certificate satisfies
$$ \alpha_{(t,b)}-\alpha_{(t,a)}=1. $$

To prove this, first suppose both statuses are matched. The reciprocal classes have certificate values
$$ \alpha_{(a,t)}=-\alpha_{(t,a)},\qquad \alpha_{(b,t)}=-\alpha_{(t,b)}. $$

The allowed class pair $\{(t,b),(a,t)\}$ has vote coefficient $1$, giving
$$ \alpha_{(t,b)}-\alpha_{(t,a)}\geq1. $$

The allowed pair $\{(t,a),(b,t)\}$ has coefficient $-1$, giving the reverse bound.

If $b=\bot$, the first inequality still applies. Also,
$$ \alpha_{(t,\bot)}=0,\qquad \alpha_{(t,a)}\geq-1, $$
which supplies the reverse bound.

3.3 A pure stable matching constrains split statuses

Let $S$ be a pure stable matching.

For every compatible edge, its vote coefficient against $S$ is nonpositive: coefficient $2$ would mean both endpoint types strictly prefer that edge, contradicting stability. Becoming unmatched contributes either $-1$ or $0$. Hence $S$ is popular even against atomless opponents.

If $M$ is popular, antisymmetry of votes therefore gives
$$ \Delta(M,S)=0. $$

It also follows that every type matched in $S$ is fully matched in $M$. Otherwise its positive unmatched mass would make the preceding sum of nonpositive contributions strictly negative.

Now suppose $t$ has two matched statuses $a\succ_t b$ in $M$. We claim that $S(t)$ is one of $a,b$.

If $S(t)=\bot$, the opponent $S$ leaves every class of $t$ unmatched. Complementary slackness against a zero-objective certificate for $M$ gives
$$ \alpha_{(t,a)}=\alpha_{(t,b)}=-1, $$
contradicting their difference of $1$.

Thus $S(t)=r$ is matched. Fix any positive class $k$ of type $r$. Because all individuals of $t$ and $r$ receive each other’s type in $S$, their pairing can be chosen to use positive mass from both class pairs
$$ \{(t,a),k\},\qquad \{(t,b),k\}. $$

Such a realization still has the same assignments as $S$, and hence vote value zero against $M$. Complementary slackness makes both associated dual inequalities tight. Subtracting them gives
$$ 1=\alpha_{(t,b)}-\alpha_{(t,a)} =v_t(r,b)-v_t(r,a). $$

Under strict rankings, this difference equals $1$ exactly when $r=a$ or $r=b$: a status strictly between them gives difference $2$, and a status above both or below both gives difference $0$. The claim follows.

3.4 The positive matched support is bipartite

The graph of positive matched type pairs has maximum degree two by Section 3.1.

Suppose it had an odd-cycle component. Every vertex on that cycle has two matched statuses. Section 3.3 says its partner in $S$ must be one of its two cycle neighbors. Therefore $S$ would restrict to a perfect matching of the odd cycle, which is impossible.

Consequently every component is a path, an even cycle, or an isolated vertex. The positive matched support is bipartite.

3.5 Integral rounding

Scale assignment masses by $q$. On the positive matched support, consider the polytope
$$ x_e\geq0,\qquad u_t\geq0,\qquad \sum_{e\ni t}x_e+u_t=1. $$

An unmatched variable is allowed only if unmatched status was present in the original support. The original matching supplies a feasible point.

The constraint matrix is totally unimodular. Indeed, signing rows on one side of the bipartition turns each edge column into a column with one $1$ and one $-1$; unmatched columns have one nonzero entry. Every square submatrix either has a column with at most one nonzero entry, permitting determinant expansion, or has columns whose sums are zero, making its determinant zero. Induction gives determinants in $\{0,1,-1\}$.

The polytope is nonempty and bounded, so it has a vertex. Total unimodularity and integral right-hand sides make every vertex integral. Its variables are consequently $0$ or $1$.

Such a vertex defines a pure matching with assignment support contained in the original support. The support-restriction lemma preserves popularity and dominance. This proves the rounding theorem. $\square$

4. A gadget forcing a matched vertex’s certificate to zero

Add seven vertices
$$ g,u_1,u_2,u_3,v_1,v_2,v_3 $$
to the existing vertex $z$.

The new compatibility edges are:

  • the triangles on $\{u_1,u_2,u_3\}$ and $\{v_1,v_2,v_3\}$;
  • the three edges $\{u_i,v_i\}$;
  • $\{z,g\}$, $\{z,u_1\}$, and $\{g,v_1\}$.

The preferences are
$$ \begin{aligned} z&:\ \text{original neighbors in their original order}\succ g\succ u_1,\\ g&:\ z\succ v_1,\\ u_1&:\ u_2\succ z\succ v_1\succ u_3,\\ u_2&:\ u_3\succ v_2\succ u_1,\\ u_3&:\ u_1\succ v_3\succ u_2,\\ v_1&:\ v_2\succ g\succ u_1\succ v_3,\\ v_2&:\ v_3\succ u_2\succ v_1,\\ v_3&:\ v_1\succ u_3\succ v_2. \end{aligned} $$

Define
$$ P_*=\{\{z,g\},\{u_1,v_1\},\{u_2,v_2\},\{u_3,v_3\}\}. $$

This matching is stable inside the gadget. Every triangle edge is preferred to the current assignment at exactly one endpoint. On $\{z,u_1\}$, vertex $z$ prefers $g$; on $\{g,v_1\}$, vertex $g$ prefers $z$.

Gadget lemma. Suppose a pure atomless popular matching matches these eight vertices internally. Its restriction to the gadget is $P_*$. Every popularity certificate then has value zero at all eight vertices.

4.1 Bounds used in the proof

For a pure matched vertex $a$,
$$ \alpha_a\geq-1,\qquad \alpha_a+\alpha_{M(a)}=0. $$
Thus
$$ -1\leq\alpha_a\leq1. $$

A blocking edge has vote coefficient $2$, so both endpoints must have certificate value $1$.

4.2 Exhausting the perfect matchings

There are exactly six internal perfect matchings.

If $\{z,g\}$ is selected, either all three cross edges $\{u_i,v_i\}$ are selected, giving $P_*$, or exactly one cross edge is selected and the remaining vertices are paired within their respective triangles.

Consider the latter case. Write the selected triangle edge as $\{u_h,u_\ell\}$, where $\ell$ is the cyclic successor of $h$, and let $k$ be the remaining index. The corresponding $v$-triangle edge is $\{v_h,v_\ell\}$, while $\{u_k,v_k\}$ is selected.

The edge $\{u_\ell,v_\ell\}$ blocks: both endpoints prefer their cross partner to their assigned cyclic predecessor. Therefore
$$ \alpha_{u_\ell}=\alpha_{v_\ell}=1, \qquad \alpha_{u_h}=\alpha_{v_h}=-1. $$

The edges $\{u_k,u_h\}$ and $\{v_k,v_h\}$ have vote coefficient zero. They imply
$$ \alpha_{u_k}\geq1,\qquad \alpha_{v_k}\geq1, $$
contradicting the selected cross edge’s equality
$$ \alpha_{u_k}+\alpha_{v_k}=0. $$

If $\{z,g\}$ is absent, the matching must select $\{z,u_1\}$ and $\{g,v_1\}$. The remaining four vertices have two possible perfect matchings.

First, suppose they use $\{u_2,v_2\}$ and $\{u_3,v_3\}$. Edge $\{z,g\}$ blocks, so
$$ \alpha_z=\alpha_g=1,\qquad \alpha_{u_1}=\alpha_{v_1}=-1. $$
The zero-vote edges $\{u_1,u_2\}$ and $\{v_1,v_2\}$ force
$$ \alpha_{u_2}\geq1,\qquad \alpha_{v_2}\geq1, $$
contradicting their matched-pair equality.

Second, suppose the remaining edges are $\{u_2,u_3\}$ and $\{v_2,v_3\}$. Again $\alpha_{u_1}=\alpha_{v_1}=-1$. Edge $\{u_3,v_3\}$ blocks, giving
$$ \alpha_{u_3}=\alpha_{v_3}=1,\qquad \alpha_{u_2}=\alpha_{v_2}=-1. $$
But $\{u_1,u_2\}$ has vote coefficient zero, contradicting
$$ \alpha_{u_1}+\alpha_{u_2}=-2. $$

Thus only $P_*$ can be popular.

4.3 Its certificate is forced to zero

Under $P_*$,
$$ \alpha_{v_i}=-\alpha_{u_i}. $$

Every triangle edge has vote coefficient zero. Consequently, for every distinct $i,j$,
$$ \alpha_{u_i}+\alpha_{u_j}\geq0,\qquad -\alpha_{u_i}-\alpha_{u_j}\geq0. $$

All three pair sums are zero, implying
$$ \alpha_{u_i}=\alpha_{v_i}=0 \qquad(i=1,2,3). $$

The zero-vote edges $\{z,u_1\}$ and $\{g,v_1\}$ then give $\alpha_z\geq0$ and $\alpha_g\geq0$. Their matched-pair equality gives
$$ \alpha_z+\alpha_g=0. $$
Hence $\alpha_z=\alpha_g=0$, proving the gadget lemma. $\square$

5. The reduction and its correctness

Start with $G_0$ from Section 1 and add the gadget.

Let the resulting number of vertices be $n$. It is even, because $|X|=|Y|$ and seven vertices were added to $G_0$.

Choose the smallest power of two $\tau\geq n$. Add $\tau-n$ padding vertices as disjoint pairs, each vertex having only its paired vertex as a compatible partner.

Turn every vertex into a type of mass
$$ \mu_t=\frac1\tau. $$

These masses are positive dyadic rationals summing to one.

5.1 The stability promise holds

Extend $S_0$ by $P_*$ and all padding edges.

The original edges not incident to $z$ remain stable. For an original neighbor $a$ of $z$, stability of $S_0$ with $z$ unmatched means $a$ prefers its assignment to $z$. Thus changing $z$ from unmatched to $g$ creates no blocking original edge.

The gadget’s internal stability was checked in Section 4, and padding edges are stable.

This finite stable matching lifts to a pure stable matching of the society. Therefore every output instance satisfies the promise.

5.2 Satisfiability implies an atomless dominant matching

Suppose $\varphi$ is satisfiable. Take the matching $N_0$ and certificate from Property 2 of the finite reduction. Extend $N_0$ by $P_*$ and the padding edges.

The resulting matching $P$ is perfect.

Extend the certificate by zero on all new vertices. Its original constraints remain valid:

  • assignments at original vertices other than $z$ are unchanged;
  • $z$ prefers every original neighbor to $g$, exactly as it previously preferred every neighbor to unmatched status.

Every new nonmatching gadget edge has vote coefficient zero. Every new matched edge has certificate sum zero. The unmatched inequalities remain valid because all new certificate values are zero and all vertices are matched.

Thus this is an atomless popularity certificate for $P$. Since
$$ s(P)=1, $$
no feasible opponent has larger matched mass. Hence $P$ is dominant.

5.3 An atomless dominant matching implies satisfiability

Suppose the constructed society has an atomless dominant matching.

Its masses are equal, it has no self-compatible types, and Section 5.1 supplied a pure stable matching. The rounding theorem therefore gives a pure atomless dominant matching $P$.

Every gadget vertex is matched in that pure stable matching. Section 3.3 consequently implies that all eight gadget vertices are matched in $P$.

Vertex $z$ cannot be matched to an original vertex outside the gadget. If it were, the other seven gadget vertices would all have to be matched internally, which is impossible because their number is odd. Thus the eight gadget vertices are matched internally.

The gadget lemma now gives
$$ P|_{\text{gadget}}=P_*, \qquad \alpha_z=0 $$
for a popularity certificate of $P$.

Remove the new vertices and leave $z$ unmatched, obtaining a finite matching $N_0$ in $G_0$. Restrict the certificate to the original vertices.

The original edge vote coefficients are unchanged: assigning $g$ to $z$ and leaving $z$ unmatched both make $z$ strictly prefer every original neighbor. The unmatched constraint at $z$ holds because $\alpha_z=0$. Therefore the restricted certificate proves that $N_0$ is popular in $G_0$.

Suppose $N_0$ fails to cover $X\cup Y$. Property 3 applies: $N_0$ is popular and leaves $z$ unmatched. It supplies a larger tied finite matching $N_1$ also leaving $z$ unmatched.

Extend $N_1$ by the same gadget and padding edges, and lift it to the society. The gadget and padding votes are zero, while original votes are scaled by $1/\tau$. Thus
$$ \Delta(N_1^{\mathrm{ext}},P)=0, \qquad s(N_1^{\mathrm{ext}})>s(P), $$
contradicting dominance.

Therefore $N_0$ covers $X\cup Y$. Property 2 implies that $\varphi$ is satisfiable.

We have proved the required equivalence:
$$ \varphi\text{ is satisfiable} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the constructed society admits a dominant popular matching}. $$

5.4 Size and running time

If the finite instance has $\tau_0$ vertices and $m_0$ edges, then
$$ \tau<2(\tau_0+7),\qquad m=m_0+12+\frac{\tau-(\tau_0+7)}2. $$

Every mass has encoding length $O(\log\tau)$. The added preference lists have constant length, except for appending two entries to $z$’s list. Thus the construction has polynomial total bit length and is computable in polynomial time. Apart from the source reduction, its explicit encoding takes $O(m+\tau+L)$ time.

This establishes $\mathrm{NP}$-hardness under the promised restrictions.

6. Polynomial verification in the general model

For completeness, the certificate argument also gives membership in $\mathrm{NP}$ when self-compatible types are allowed.

Guess a support consisting of positive compatible type pairs and positive unmatched statuses. Discard guesses assigning more than two statuses to any type; Section 3.1 applies equally when self-compatibility is present.

There are at most $2\tau$ assignment classes. A distinct-type compatibility pair generates at most four opponent class pairs, and a self-compatible pair generates at most three. Hence the opponent LP has $O(m+\tau)$ columns and $O(\tau)$ balance equations.

For an internal class pair, use a variable $y_{hh}$ consuming $2y_{hh}$ class mass. Its vote coefficient is
$$ w_{hh}=2v_t(t,a). $$

Accordingly, its certificate inequalities are
$$ 2\alpha_h\geq w_{hh},\qquad 2\beta_h+\gamma w_{hh}\geq2. $$

A present self-partner assignment has certificate equalities
$$ \alpha_{(t,t)}=0,\qquad \beta_{(t,t)}=1. $$

Internal pair masses are realizable by dividing their allocated sets into equal halves and pairing those halves. Thus the same opponent representation and duality proofs remain exact.

A guessed support is checked by three rational LPs:

  • Positive mass feasibility: impose the type balance equations, require every selected assignment mass to be at least $\delta$, and maximize $\delta$ subject to $0\leq\delta\leq1$. The support is realizable exactly when the optimum is positive.
  • Popularity: check feasibility of the $\alpha$ certificate.
  • Dominance: check feasibility of the $\beta,\gamma$ certificate.

Each LP has $O(\tau)$ variables, $O(m+\tau)$ constraints, and encoding length polynomial in $m,\tau,L$. Rational LP algorithms perform these checks, including the exact comparison with zero, in
$$ \operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L) $$
time.

The guessed support uses polynomially many bits. Hence dominant popular matching existence belongs to $\mathrm{NP}$ even without the stability promise.
