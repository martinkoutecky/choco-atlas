The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates, rational masses over partial-order types, and a fixed priority for pairwise ties, determine whether the profile-based possible and necessary Condorcet-winner sets equal the corresponding sets computed from the incomplete weighted majority graph. For the profile-based sets, feasible completions allocate each type’s mass among its compatible linear orders; the incomplete graph records pairwise majorities forced by the type masses.

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a set of \(m\geq 1\) candidates, with a fixed strict total priority order for resolving pairwise ties.
A society \(P\) is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct strict partial orders \(P_i\) on \(C\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(w_i\), with \(\sum_i w_i=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible types; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
A feasible completion assigns nonnegative real masses \(x_{i,r}\) to the linear extensions \(r\) of each \(P_i\), satisfying \(\sum_r x_{i,r}=w_i\).
In a completion, \(a\) defeats \(b\) when the mass ranking \(a\) above \(b\) exceeds \(1/2\), or equals \(1/2\) and the priority favors \(a\). A Condorcet winner defeats every other candidate under this convention.
Define \(\operatorname{PossC}(P)\) and \(\operatorname{NecC}(P)\) using, respectively, existence of a feasible completion and every feasible completion.
The incomplete majority graph \(G(P)\) retains only forced pairwise outcomes: writing \(q_{ab}=\sum_{i:a\,P_i\,b}w_i\), include \(a\to b\) exactly when \(q_{ab}>1/2\), or \(q_{ab}=1/2\) and priority favors \(a\).
A graph completion independently orients every missing pair to obtain a tournament, with no further realizability or margin constraints. Define \(\operatorname{PossC}(G(P))\) and \(\operatorname{NecC}(G(P))\) by existence and universality over these graph completions.

Is deciding whether both \(\operatorname{PossC}(P)=\operatorname{PossC}(G(P))\) and \(\operatorname{NecC}(P)=\operatorname{NecC}(G(P))\) hold solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

For finite profiles with an odd number of unweighted voters, both equalities always hold, including when voting trees permit repeated candidates ([Pini et al., 2011](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2011/papers/C2_G52.pdf)).
No result settling the exact divisible-mass decision problem above was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the positive type masses to sum to one; the source uses a nonempty finite electorate and majority thresholds relative to its size.
  • Allowed nonnegative real completion allocations, while retaining binary-rational input masses.
  • Interpreted the fixed priority as a strict total order and counted a priority-resolved pairwise tie as a victory for defining Condorcet winners.
  • Defined forced graph edges to include outcomes forced by the tie priority at exactly half the mass; graph completions retain only orientations and impose no numerical margin or profile-realizability constraints.
  • Used pairwise victories to express the source's victory in every voting tree, with every candidate represented at least once; this excludes trees omitting candidates.
  • Allowed any nonempty candidate set, since the anchor imposes no minimum beyond nonemptiness.

Result

Both equalities hold for every valid instance. Consequently, the decision problem is polynomial-time solvable: on the promised input domain, the answer is always “yes.”

The proof handles arbitrary positive rational masses and the stated priority rule directly. It extends the profile–graph equality discussed by [Pini et al. (2011)](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2011/papers/C2_G52.pdf).

1. A completion simultaneously maximizes a candidate’s pairwise support

Write \(a\succ_\pi b\) when the fixed priority order favors \(a\) over \(b\). For a feasible completion \(x\), let \(s_{ab}(x)\) be the total mass ranking \(a\) above \(b\).

Lemma. For any candidate \(a\), there is a feasible completion \(x^{(a)}\) satisfying
\(s_{ab}(x^{(a)})=1-q_{ba}\) for every \(b\ne a\). Moreover, every feasible completion satisfies \(s_{ab}(x)\leq 1-q_{ba}\).

Proof. Fix a support order \(P_i\), and partition the other candidates into
\(A_i=\{b\in C:b\,P_i\,a\}\) and \(B_i=C\setminus(A_i\cup\{a\})\).

Construct a total order \(r_i^{(a)}\) by concatenating:

  • a linear extension of the restriction of \(P_i\) to \(A_i\);
  • the candidate \(a\);
  • a linear extension of the restriction of \(P_i\) to \(B_i\).

The two restricted linear extensions exist because finite strict partial orders are acyclic.

To verify that the concatenation extends \(P_i\), it suffices to exclude relations directed from a later block to an earlier block:

  • A relation \(b\,P_i\,a\) with \(b\in B_i\) contradicts the definition of \(B_i\).
  • Relations \(b\,P_i\,d\) with \(b\in B_i\), \(d\in A_i\) would imply \(b\,P_i\,a\) by transitivity, again a contradiction.
  • A relation \(a\,P_i\,d\) with \(d\in A_i\), together with \(d\,P_i\,a\), would imply \(a\,P_i\,a\), contradicting irreflexivity.

Thus \(r_i^{(a)}\) is a linear extension. In this extension, \(a\) is above \(b\) exactly when \(b\,P_i\,a\) does not hold.

Assign all mass \(w_i\) to \(r_i^{(a)}\), separately for each support order. This is a feasible completion, and its support for \(a\) against \(b\) is
\(s_{ab}(x^{(a)})=\sum_{i:\,\neg(b\,P_i\,a)}w_i=1-q_{ba}\).

Conversely, the mass \(q_{ba}\) must rank \(b\) above \(a\) in every completion, so \(s_{ab}(x)\leq 1-q_{ba}\). This proves the lemma.

The essential point is that these maxima are attained simultaneously for all opponents of \(a\).

2. Possible Condorcet winners coincide

The lemma implies the following exact characterization:
\(a\in\operatorname{PossC}(P)\) if and only if, for every \(b\ne a\), either \(q_{ba}<1/2\), or \(q_{ba}=1/2\) and \(a\succ_\pi b\).

For necessity, if this condition fails for some \(b\), then either \(q_{ba}>1/2\), or \(q_{ba}=1/2\) and priority favors \(b\). Since every completion has \(s_{ab}(x)\leq 1-q_{ba}\), candidate \(a\) cannot defeat that \(b\).

For sufficiency, use \(x^{(a)}\) from the lemma. Against every opponent \(b\), its support is \(1-q_{ba}\). The stated condition makes this support either strictly greater than \(1/2\), or equal to \(1/2\) with priority favoring \(a\). Hence \(a\) defeats every opponent in this one completion.

Now consider graph completions. A candidate \(a\) can be a Condorcet winner in a graph completion exactly when \(G(P)\) has no incoming arc to \(a\):

  • An existing incoming arc persists in every graph completion.
  • If there is no incoming arc, orient every missing pair incident with \(a\) outward; all remaining missing pairs may be oriented arbitrarily.

By the definition of \(G(P)\), the absence of \(b\to a\) means precisely that \(q_{ba}<1/2\), or \(q_{ba}=1/2\) and \(a\succ_\pi b\). Thus the society and graph characterizations agree, proving
\(\operatorname{PossC}(P)=\operatorname{PossC}(G(P))\).

3. Necessary Condorcet winners coincide

For any distinct \(a,b\), every feasible completion satisfies \(s_{ab}(x)\geq q_{ab}\), because every extension of a support order containing \(a\,P_i\,b\) ranks \(a\) above \(b\).

This lower bound is attainable. Apply the lemma with distinguished candidate \(b\): in \(x^{(b)}\),
\(s_{ba}(x^{(b)})=1-q_{ab}\).
Every linear order ranks exactly one of \(a,b\) above the other, and the total mass is \(1\), so
\(s_{ab}(x^{(b)})=1-s_{ba}(x^{(b)})=q_{ab}\).

Consequently, \(a\) defeats \(b\) in every feasible completion if and only if
\(q_{ab}>1/2\), or \(q_{ab}=1/2\) and \(a\succ_\pi b\).

Indeed, this condition makes even the minimum support sufficient for victory. If it fails, the completion attaining that minimum makes \(a\) lose to \(b\).

A candidate is a necessary Condorcet winner exactly when it necessarily defeats each opponent. Therefore,
\(a\in\operatorname{NecC}(P)\) if and only if \(a\to b\) belongs to \(G(P)\) for every \(b\ne a\).

The same condition characterizes necessary Condorcet winners of the graph:

  • If every incident arc points outward, \(a\) wins in every graph completion.
  • If an incident arc points inward, \(a\) loses in every graph completion.
  • If an incident pair is missing, orienting that pair toward \(a\) produces a graph completion in which \(a\) loses.

Hence
\(\operatorname{NecC}(P)=\operatorname{NecC}(G(P))\).

4. Complexity and boundary cases

These arguments prove both equalities universally, so the requested decision algorithm returns “yes” on every valid input.

The winner sets themselves can also be computed in polynomial time. Compute all \(q_{ab}\) by exact rational addition, construct \(G(P)\), and select candidates with no incoming arcs for the possible set and candidates with outgoing arcs to every opponent for the necessary set. The number of arithmetic operations is polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and exact rational arithmetic has polynomial bit complexity in \(L\). If the partial orders are represented by generating relations, their transitive closures can first be computed in polynomial time.

For \(m=1\), all opponent conditions are vacuous, and the sole candidate belongs to all four sets.

Although divisible completions are allowed, the possible-winner witness constructed above assigns each support order’s entire mass to a single linear extension. Thus no fractional splitting or realizability assumption about arbitrary graph completions is needed.
