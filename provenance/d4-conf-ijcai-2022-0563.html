The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given \(m=2^r\) candidates, a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, \(m/2\) designated seeds, and a target candidate \(c^\star\), define \(a\succ_\mu b\) when \(a\) has strict majority support over \(b\). Decide whether a valid seeded knockout bracket exists whose winner under \(\succ_\mu\) is \(c^\star\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) contain \(m=2^r\) candidates, with integer \(r\ge 2\). A society is given by its support: a list of \(\tau\) distinct strict complete rankings of \(C\), each with a positive binary rational mass; masses sum to \(1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number of possible rankings, and \(L\) is the total input bit length.
Define \(a\succ_\mu b\) when the mass ranking \(a\) above \(b\) exceeds \(1/2\). Promise that no pair has mass exactly \(1/2\), so every match has a unique winner.
The input also specifies distinct candidates assigned seed numbers \(1,\ldots,m/2\), and a target \(c^\star\in C\), which may be seeded or unseeded.
A bracket assigns each candidate once to the leaves of a balanced binary knockout tree. It is valid if, for every \(k\in\{1,\ldots,r-1\}\), the top \(2^k\) seeds occupy different depth-\(k\) subtrees, with the root at depth \(0\). Validity concerns their initial positions, regardless of subsequent eliminations. Each match advances its winner under \(\succ_\mu\).

Is deciding whether a valid bracket makes \(c^\star\) win solvable in polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), or is it NP-hard?

With pairwise outcomes supplied as a tournament graph, this decision problem with \(m/2\) numbered seeds is NP-complete ([Manurangsi and Suksompong](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.11171)).
No result settling the exact rational-distribution input version was located in the literature search.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Interpreted complete rankings as strict linear orders and the distribution as positive support masses summing to one.
  • Excluded pairwise majority ties to preserve the discrete model's unique outcome for every pair.
  • Specified that the designated seeds have input seed numbers, rather than being an unordered subset, and stated all hierarchical placement constraints.
  • Required a balanced bracket using every candidate exactly once and imposed seed constraints on initial positions regardless of eliminations.
  • Restricted to integer r at least two, preserving the discrete condition that there are at least two seeds.
  • Defined L as the total input bit length.

Result

The decision problem is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. The key is a polynomial-size realization of every tournament by a rational distribution over distinct rankings. The construction below uses dyadic masses, so it also covers the interpretation of “binary rational” as a finite binary fraction.

1. The tournament-graph hardness result applies

We use the stated theorem of Manurangsi and Suksompong: tournament fixing with \(m/2\) numbered seeds is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Their model uses \(m=2^r\) players and requires the top \(2^k\) seeds to be separated until the appropriate round. This is exactly separation into distinct depth-\(k\) subtrees in the initial bracket; their discussion also specifies that later elimination does not change these placement constraints. See [Section 2 and Theorem 5.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.11171).

Thus it suffices to transform an arbitrary tournament into a polynomial-size society inducing precisely its edges, while retaining the candidates, seed assignments, and target.

2. Polynomial-size realization by dyadic masses

Lemma. Every tournament \(T\) on \(m\ge4\) candidates has a polynomial-time constructible society satisfying:

  • Its majority tournament is exactly \(T\).
  • Every mass is positive and dyadic.
  • Its support consists of distinct rankings and has size \(\tau<2m(m-1)\).
  • Its total encoding length is \(O(m^3\log m)\).

Proof. We first construct a multiset of rankings, then merge repeated rankings.

For every directed edge \(a\to b\) of \(T\), choose an ordering \(x_1,\ldots,x_{m-2}\) of the other candidates and add the following two rankings, written from most preferred to least preferred:

\[ \text{} \]

\(a>b>x_1>\cdots>x_{m-2}\),

\(x_{m-2}>\cdots>x_1>a>b\).

For two distinct candidates \(u,v\), define their count margin as the number of rankings placing \(u\) above \(v\), minus the number placing \(v\) above \(u\).

The displayed pair contributes \(2\) to the count margin of \(a\) over \(b\). It contributes zero to every other pair:

  • Two candidates among the \(x_i\) appear in opposite orders in the two rankings.
  • Each of \(a,b\) appears above every \(x_i\) in the first ranking and below every \(x_i\) in the second.

There are \(\binom m2\) edges, so the multiset contains \(N=2\binom m2=m(m-1)\) rankings. For each edge \(u\to v\), its count margin is exactly \(2\): its own gadget contributes \(2\), and all other gadgets contribute zero.

Let \(Q\) be the smallest power of two with \(Q\ge N\). Then \(N\le Q<2N\). Both \(N\) and \(Q\) are even. Add \((Q-N)/2\) copies of a neutral pair consisting of an arbitrary ranking and its reverse. Each neutral pair contributes zero to every count margin.

The resulting multiset therefore has exactly \(Q\) rankings and still has count margin \(2\) on every directed edge of \(T\).

For each distinct ranking \(\pi\) occurring in this multiset, let \(h_\pi\) be its positive multiplicity and assign mass \(\mu_\pi=h_\pi/Q\). These masses are positive, dyadic, and sum to \(1\). Merging identical rankings preserves every pairwise comparison mass.

For an edge \(u\to v\), write \(A_{uv}\) for the number of multiset rankings placing \(u\) above \(v\). Strict completeness gives \(A_{uv}+A_{vu}=Q\), while the construction gives \(A_{uv}-A_{vu}=2\). Consequently,

\(\mu(u\text{ above }v)=A_{uv}/Q=\frac12+\frac1Q>\frac12\).

The reverse comparison has mass \(\frac12-\frac1Q\). Thus the majority tournament is exactly \(T\), and every pair satisfies the no-tie promise.

Finally, there are at most \(Q<2m(m-1)\) distinct rankings. A ranking requires \(O(m\log m)\) bits under candidate labels of \(O(\log m)\) bits. Each numerator \(h_\pi\) and the denominator \(Q\) require \(O(\log m)\) bits. Hence the entire society has encoding length \(O(m^3\log m)\). Generating the rankings and grouping identical ones take polynomial time. This proves the lemma.

3. The reduction preserves the answer

Take a tournament-graph instance \((T,\text{seed assignments},c^\star)\) from Section 1. Replace \(T\) by the society constructed in Section 2, copying the candidate set, every numbered seed assignment, and the target unchanged.

This is a polynomial-time transformation, and its output satisfies every input requirement.

For any leaf assignment:

  • Its validity is identical in the two instances, because validity depends only on initial seed positions.
  • Every possible match has the same winner, because the society’s majority tournament is \(T\).
  • Induction from the leaves to the root therefore gives the same winner at every internal node.

Hence a valid bracket makes \(c^\star\) win in the tournament instance if and only if one does so in the society instance. This proves \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness.

4. Membership in \(\mathrm{NP}\)

A certificate lists the candidates assigned to the \(m\) leaf positions, requiring \(O(m\log m)\) bits.

A verifier checks that every candidate occurs exactly once. For each \(k\in\{1,\ldots,r-1\}\), it identifies the depth-\(k\) subtree containing each of the top \(2^k\) seeds and checks that these subtrees are distinct. These checks take polynomial time.

Pairwise majority outcomes can also be computed exactly in polynomial time. Write each input mass as \(p_i/q_i\), with the integers encoded in binary, and let \(B=\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}q_i\). The bit length of \(B\) is at most the sum of the denominator bit lengths, hence \(O(L)\). For candidates \(a,b\), compute the integer

\(S_{ab}=\sum_{i:\,a\text{ above }b\text{ in ranking }i}p_i(B/q_i)\).

Then \(a\succ_\mu b\) precisely when \(2S_{ab}>B\). All these computations use polynomially many operations on integers of polynomial bit length. The verifier then simulates the \(m-1\) bracket matches and checks that the root winner is \(c^\star\).

Thus the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\). Together with the reduction, this establishes \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness.
