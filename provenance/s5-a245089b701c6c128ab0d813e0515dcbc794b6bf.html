The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given rational \(w\in(0,1)\) and finite-support rational probability laws \(\nu_\omega\) over \(\Delta_4\), one for each true state \(\omega\in\{++, +-, -+, --\}\), find a symmetric, upward-closed region \(U\subseteq\Delta_4\) minimizing \(w\,\nu_{+-}(U)+(1-w)\,\nu_{++}(\Delta_4\setminus U)\). Here \(\Delta_4\) is the simplex of masses on the four complete judgment types, and upward closure is under transfers of mass along \(--\to +-\), \(--\to -+\), \(+-\to ++\), and \(-+\to ++\); the laws respect the corresponding reflection symmetry and stochastic dominance.

Result

The threshold decision problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\). An optimal admissible region can be computed by one directed minimum-cut computation.

For the stated parameters \(m=2\) and \(\tau\le4\), a conservative bit-complexity bound is
\[ \]
\(O\!\left((L+N^{2}\tau+N^{5})L^{2}\right)\).

The argument works even without the stochastic-dominance promises.

1. Exact characterization of the transfer order

For \(p=(x,y,z,t)\) and \(q=(X,Y,Z,T)\) in \(\Delta_4\), we claim that

\(p\preceq q\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X\ge x,\quad X+Y\ge x+y,\quad X+Z\ge x+z,\quad T\le t.\)

Necessity. Every allowed transfer weakly increases each of \(x\), \(x+y\), and \(x+z\), and weakly decreases \(t\). Consequently, these four inequalities hold after any finite sequence of transfers.

Sufficiency. Suppose the inequalities hold. Define

\(B=t-T,\qquad A=X-x,\qquad d_y=Y-y,\qquad d_z=Z-z.\)

Equality of total masses gives

\(A=B-d_y-d_z.\)

The assumed inequalities imply

\(B\ge0,\qquad d_y\le B,\qquad d_z\le B,\qquad d_y+d_z\le B.\)

Set

\(a=\max\{0,d_y\},\qquad b=B-a,\qquad c=a-d_y,\qquad d=B-a-d_z.\)

These four quantities are nonnegative. Indeed, \(a\le B\) follows from \(d_y\le B\). Also, \(a\le B-d_z\): if \(d_y\le0\), this follows from \(d_z\le B\); otherwise it follows from \(d_y+d_z\le B\).

Perform, in order, the following transfers:

  • Mass \(a\) along \(--\to+-\).
  • Mass \(b\) along \(--\to-+\).
  • Mass \(c\) along \(+-\to++\).
  • Mass \(d\) along \(-+\to++\).

The first two transfers are feasible because \(a+b=B\le t\). The last two are feasible because

\(y+a-c=Y\ge0,\qquad z+b-d=Z\ge0.\)

The resulting coordinates are \(Y,Z,T\) in the last three positions, and

\(x+c+d=x+B-d_y-d_z=X\)

in the first position. Thus the resulting society is exactly \(q\).

This proves the characterization. In particular, every principal upper set

\(\uparrow p=\{q\in\Delta_4:p\preceq q\}\)

is closed: it is the intersection of \(\Delta_4\) with four closed linear half-spaces. The characterization also shows that \(s\) preserves the order, since it exchanges the two middle inequalities.

2. Exact reduction to the finite support

Let

\(S_0=\bigcup_{\omega}\operatorname{supp}(\nu_\omega),\qquad S=S_0\cup s(S_0),\qquad K=|S|\le2N.\)

Repeated listings of the same society are merged, adding their probabilities separately for each law. Thus \(S\) is finite, contains every atom of every law, and satisfies \(s(S)=S\).

Call \(A\subseteq S\) feasible if it satisfies both conditions:

  • \(s(A)=A\);
  • whenever \(p\in A\), \(q\in S\), and \(p\preceq q\), we have \(q\in A\).

Every admissible region \(U\) induces a feasible set \(A=U\cap S\).

Conversely, every feasible \(A\) extends to the admissible region

\(U_A=\bigcup_{p\in A}\uparrow p.\)

This region is closed, hence Borel, because the union is finite. It is upper by transitivity of \(\preceq\). It is symmetric because \(s\) preserves the order and \(s(A)=A\).

Moreover,

\(U_A\cap S=A.\)

For one inclusion, every \(p\in A\) belongs to \(\uparrow p\). For the other, if \(q\in U_A\cap S\), then some \(p\in A\) satisfies \(p\preceq q\), so feasibility forces \(q\in A\).

Therefore optimization over admissible Borel regions is exactly equivalent to optimization over feasible subsets of \(S\). This also proves that every feasible finite decision pattern has a valid extension to the entire simplex.

3. Minimum-cut formulation

For each \(p\in S\), define the rational coefficient

\(c_p=w\,\nu_{+-}(\{p\})-(1-w)\,\nu_{++}(\{p\}).\)

Since both laws are supported on \(S\), the loss associated with a feasible set \(A\) is

\(R_w(U_A)=(1-w)+\sum_{p\in A}c_p.\)

We now minimize this expression subject to feasibility.

Construct a directed network with vertices \(S\), a source \(\sigma\), and a sink \(\rho\). Add the following arcs:

  • If \(c_p<0\), add \(\sigma\to p\) with capacity \(-c_p\).
  • If \(c_p>0\), add \(p\to\rho\) with capacity \(c_p\).
  • For distinct \(p,q\in S\) with \(p\preceq q\), add \(p\to q\) with capacity \(2\).
  • For each \(p\ne s(p)\), add \(p\to s(p)\) with capacity \(2\).

The last construction includes both directions of every nontrivial transpose pair. Duplicate constraint arcs may be merged into a single capacity-\(2\) arc.

For any source–sink cut, let \(A\) denote the society vertices on its source side. An order arc crosses the cut exactly when \(p\in A\), \(p\preceq q\), and \(q\notin A\). A symmetry arc crosses exactly when its corresponding equality of decisions is violated. Thus no constraint arc crosses precisely when \(A\) is feasible.

Define

\(D=\sum_{p\in S}\max\{-c_p,0\}.\)

We have

\(D\le(1-w)\sum_{p\in S}\nu_{++}(\{p\})=1-w<1.\)

The cut whose source side contains only \(\sigma\) has capacity \(D\). Any cut crossing a constraint arc has capacity at least \(2\). Hence every minimum cut satisfies all order and symmetry constraints.

For a cut satisfying these constraints, its capacity is

\(\operatorname{cap}(A)=\sum_{p\notin A,\ c_p<0}(-c_p)+\sum_{p\in A,\ c_p>0}c_p =D+\sum_{p\in A}c_p.\)

Consequently, if \(\lambda\) is the minimum-cut capacity, then the exact optimal loss is

\(R_w^*=(1-w)-D+\lambda.\)

The threshold answer is therefore “yes” exactly when

\((1-w)-D+\lambda\le b.\)

A minimum cut also supplies an optimal feasible \(A\), and Section 2 supplies the optimal admissible region \(U_A\). Its description uses at most \(2N\) principal upper sets, each defined by four inequalities.

4. Running time and rational encoding

The network has \(K+2=O(N)\) vertices and \(O(N^2)\) arcs.

For \(m=2\), each society has four possible coordinates. Its sparse input contains at most \(\tau\le4\) positive coordinates. Decoding the societies takes \(O(N\tau)\) coordinate operations. Pairwise identification of equal societies and testing all order relations can conservatively be bounded by \(O(N^2\tau)\) exact arithmetic operations.

The Edmonds–Karp algorithm computes maximum flow, and hence minimum cut, in \(O(VE^2)\) arithmetic operations. With the network sizes above, this is \(O(N^5)\). This bound is independent of capacity magnitudes; see the [Boost Graph documentation](https://www.boost.org/doc/libs/1_48_0/libs/graph/doc/edmonds_karp_max_flow.html).

To account explicitly for bit complexity, let \(H\) be the product of all denominators occurring in the input, including those of \(w\) and \(b\). Because their total encoding length is at most \(L\), the integer \(H\) has \(O(L)\) bits.

Every input rational can be represented over denominator \(H\). Each coefficient \(c_p\), and hence every network capacity, can be represented over denominator \(H^2\). Scaling all capacities by \(H^2\) therefore gives integer capacities with \(O(L)\) bits. Residual capacities and flow values also have \(O(L)\) bits: they are bounded by sums of polynomially many such capacities, and \(\log N=O(L)\).

Using elementary integer arithmetic, denominator construction and normalization take \(O(L^3)\) bit operations. Charging \(O(L^2)\) per subsequent exact arithmetic operation gives the conservative overall bound

\(O\!\left(L^3+(N^2\tau+N^5)L^2\right) =O\!\left((L+N^2\tau+N^5)L^2\right).\)

Thus, for the stated case \(m=2\), \(\tau\le4\), the algorithm runs in polynomial time in the explicit input length. It never enumerates societies beyond the listed support and its transposes.

5. Why independent orbit selection is insufficient here

The stochastic-dominance promises do not, by themselves, make all individually beneficial transpose-orbits form an upper set.

For a concrete example, take the symmetric chain

\(p_i=(a_i,0,0,1-a_i),\qquad (a_0,a_1,a_2,a_3)=(0,\tfrac14,\tfrac12,1).\)

Let \(\mu\) and \(\eta\) assign probabilities

\(\mu=(\tfrac48,\tfrac18,\tfrac28,\tfrac18),\qquad \eta=(\tfrac28,\tfrac28,\tfrac18,\tfrac38)\)

to these four societies, respectively. Set

\(\nu_{--}=\nu_{+-}=\nu_{-+}=\mu,\qquad \nu_{++}=\eta,\qquad w=\tfrac12.\)

Every support point is fixed by \(s\), so all symmetry promises hold. Since the support is a chain, its intersection with any upper set is a suffix. At the three nontrivial suffixes, the respective probabilities under \(\mu\) and \(\eta\) are

\((\tfrac48,\tfrac68),\qquad(\tfrac38,\tfrac48),\qquad(\tfrac18,\tfrac38).\)

These inequalities prove \(\mu\preceq_{\mathrm{st}}\eta\), so all stochastic-dominance promises hold as well.

However, the loss coefficients are

\((c_{p_0},c_{p_1},c_{p_2},c_{p_3}) =(\tfrac{2}{16},-\tfrac{1}{16},\tfrac{1}{16},-\tfrac{2}{16}).\)

Independent selection of exactly the beneficial orbits selects \(p_1\) and \(p_3\), excluding \(p_2\). This violates monotonicity because \(p_1\preceq p_2\).

The minimum-cut construction handles this coupling exactly, and completes the polynomial-time solution for arbitrary finitely supported laws in the question.
