The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given candidates \(C\) and a finite support \(T\) of strict rankings with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compute each candidate’s per-capita Dodgson score \(D_\mu(c)\), defined as the infimum mass-weighted adjacent-swap cost needed to make \(c\) strictly beat every rival, and return the candidates minimizing \(D_\mu(c)\).

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(C\) be a nonempty set of \(m\) candidates. A society is given by its support: a list \(T\) of \(\tau\) distinct strict rankings of \(C\), each with positive mass \(\mu_t\) encoded as a binary rational, with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Here \(\tau\) is the support size, not the number \(m!\) of possible rankings; let \(L\) be the total input bit length.
Mass of each type may be divided into finitely many portions of arbitrary nonnegative real sizes. Each portion may undergo its own finite sequence of adjacent swaps of candidates; applying \(k\) swaps to mass \(x\) costs \(kx\). All mass is preserved, and resulting rankings need not belong to \(T\).
For \(c\in C\), define \(D_\mu(c)\) as the infimum total cost of transformations after which, for every \(d\ne c\), the mass ranking \(c\) above \(d\) is strictly greater than \(1/2\). The requested output consists of every exact score and all candidates attaining the smallest score, with no tie-breaking.

Can all scores \(D_\mu(c)\) and their complete set of minimizers be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or is this computation \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?

Discrete GreedyScore and GreedyWinner run in polynomial time and guarantee correctness whenever they declare their answers definite; they do not guarantee exact answers on every input.
[McCabe-Dansted](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1501) defines Dodgson Clone by allowing fractional votes and adjacent swaps, using weak pairwise majorities, and establishes polynomial-time score computation. This is the closest known continuous result; its stated majority condition differs from the strict feasibility condition above.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Made divisibility explicit: each type's mass may be split into finitely many portions of arbitrary nonnegative real sizes and transformed independently.
  • Allowed arbitrary sequential adjacent swaps, including swaps between candidates other than the candidate being scored, following the discrete move definition.
  • Required preservation of all mass and allowed resulting rankings outside the original support.
  • Carried over the nonempty candidate domain, including the permitted single-candidate case.
  • Specified exact score output and retention of every tied minimizer; defined L as total input bit length.

Result

All exact scores and their complete set of minimizers can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The proof has two ingredients: arbitrary swap sequences can be replaced by upward moves of the target candidate, and the strict-majority infimum equals a weak-majority linear-programming optimum.

For \(m=1\), the sole candidate has score \(0\) and is the sole minimizer. Henceforth assume \(m\geq 2\).

1. A dominance lemma for arbitrary swap sequences

Fix a candidate \(c\) and an initial ranking \(t\). Write its candidates above \(c\), in order, as

\(a_1\succ a_2\succ\cdots\succ a_r\succ c.\)

For \(0\leq k\leq r\), let \(t^{(k)}\) be the ranking obtained by moving \(c\) upward exactly \(k\) positions, preserving the relative order of all other candidates. This requires exactly \(k\) adjacent swaps.

Lemma. Suppose a sequence of \(q\) adjacent swaps transforms \(t\) into a ranking \(s\). There is a \(k\leq q\) such that every candidate ranked below \(c\) in \(s\) is also ranked below \(c\) in \(t^{(k)}\).

Proof. Let \(G\) be the set of candidates initially above \(c\) that are below \(c\) in \(s\).

If \(G\) is empty, choose \(k=0\). Every candidate below \(c\) in \(s\) was already below \(c\) in \(t\).

Otherwise, choose the smallest index \(i\) with \(a_i\in G\), and set \(k=r-i+1\). Moving \(c\) upward \(k\) positions passes all candidates in \(G\). It also leaves every candidate initially below \(c\) below \(c\). Thus \(t^{(k)}\) has the required dominance property.

It remains to prove \(q\geq k\). The pair \(\{a_i,c\}\) reverses its relative order between \(t\) and \(s\). For each \(j\) with \(i<j\leq r\), initially

\(a_i\succ a_j\succ c.\)

In \(s\), we have \(c\succ a_i\). There are two possibilities:

  • If \(a_j\succ c\) in \(s\), then \(a_j\succ a_i\), so \(\{a_i,a_j\}\) reverses.
  • If \(c\succ a_j\) in \(s\), then \(\{a_j,c\}\) reverses.

These yield \(k\) distinct reversed pairs, including \(\{a_i,c\}\). An adjacent swap changes the relative order of exactly the two candidates swapped. Consequently, every reversed pair must itself be swapped at least once, and \(q\geq k\). This proves the lemma.

Applying the lemma separately to every portion replaces any allowed transformation by upward moves of \(c\), without increasing cost or decreasing any pairwise support for \(c\). Since the original transformation has finitely many portions, so does its replacement.

2. A linear program using only the listed support

Fix \(c\). For each \(t\in T\), let \(r_t\) be the number of candidates above \(c\) in \(t\). Introduce a real variable \(x_{t,k}\) for \(0\leq k\leq r_t\), representing the mass of type \(t\) on which \(c\) moves upward exactly \(k\) positions.

For each \(d\neq c\), define the initial pairwise support

\(s_{cd}=\sum_{t\in T:\,c\succ_t d}\mu_t.\)

Define \(e_{t,k,d}=1\) if \(d\) is initially above \(c\) in \(t\) and moving \(c\) upward \(k\) positions passes \(d\); otherwise define \(e_{t,k,d}=0\).

Consider the linear program

\(\displaystyle V(c)=\min_x\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{k=0}^{r_t}k\,x_{t,k}\)

subject to

\(\displaystyle \sum_{k=0}^{r_t}x_{t,k}=\mu_t\qquad(t\in T),\)

\(\displaystyle s_{cd}+\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{k=0}^{r_t}e_{t,k,d}x_{t,k}\geq\frac12\qquad(d\neq c),\)

and \(x_{t,k}\geq 0\).

The pairwise-support expression is exact: upward moves preserve all initial support for \(c\) against \(d\), and add precisely the mass on which \(c\) passes \(d\).

This program has at most \(m\tau\) variables and \(\tau+m-1\) constraints apart from nonnegativity. It does not enumerate the \(m!\) possible rankings.

Proposition. \(V(c)\) is exactly the minimum cost of an allowed transformation giving \(c\) weak pairwise majorities against all rivals.

Proof. Any feasible LP point is realized by splitting type \(t\) into portions of sizes \(x_{t,k}\) and performing the corresponding upward moves. There are finitely many such portions, and their total cost is the objective value.

Conversely, apply the dominance lemma to every portion of any weak-majority transformation. Aggregate portions with the same initial type and upward-move length. The resulting variables satisfy the LP constraints and have objective value no greater than the original cost.

Finally, the feasible set is nonempty: move \(c\) to the top of every ranking. It is compact because it is closed and every variable satisfies \(0\leq x_{t,k}\leq\mu_t\). Therefore the LP minimum is attained. Together, these observations prove the proposition.

3. Strict majorities have the same infimum

We now prove the required equality

\(D_\mu(c)=V(c).\)

Every strictly feasible transformation is weakly feasible. The preceding proposition therefore gives \(D_\mu(c)\geq V(c)\).

For the reverse inequality, let \(x^*\) be an optimal LP solution. Let \(z\) represent moving \(c\) to the top of every initial ranking: \(z_{t,r_t}=\mu_t\), with all other coordinates zero. Its cost is

\(\displaystyle Q(c)=\sum_{t\in T}r_t\mu_t\leq m-1,\)

and its pairwise support against every rival is \(1\).

For any \(0<\lambda<1\), define

\(x^\lambda=(1-\lambda)x^*+\lambda z.\)

This is an allowed finite-portion transformation: its coordinates are nonnegative and still sum to \(\mu_t\) within each type. Pairwise support is affine in these coordinates. Thus, for every \(d\neq c\), its support is at least

\(\displaystyle (1-\lambda)\frac12+\lambda=\frac12+\frac{\lambda}{2}>\frac12.\)

Its cost is

\(\displaystyle (1-\lambda)V(c)+\lambda Q(c).\)

As \(\lambda\) tends to zero through positive values, these strictly feasible costs tend to \(V(c)\). Hence \(D_\mu(c)\leq V(c)\), proving equality.

This argument uses the infimum essentially. For example, with two candidates and mass \(1/2\) on each of the two rankings, both scores are \(0\). Making either candidate a strict winner requires positive cost, but swapping it upward on mass \(\varepsilon>0\) suffices at cost \(\varepsilon\).

4. Exact computation and polynomial bit complexity

We use the standard rational linear-programming theorem: an explicitly given LP with binary rational coefficients can be solved in polynomial time in its encoding length, producing an exact rational optimum whenever a finite optimum exists. This is a theorem about bit complexity, including lower-dimensional feasible sets; see [MIT’s lecture on the ellipsoid method](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-251j-introduction-to-mathematical-programming-fall-2009/e13f5e6650f7be1e21101a2e7bd0d5b8_MIT6_251JF09_lec18.pdf).

Here its hypotheses hold:

  • The LP is explicitly given and has polynomially many variables and constraints.
  • Its matrix entries are \(0\) or \(1\), and its objective coefficients are integers between \(0\) and \(m-1\).
  • Its right-hand sides are binary rationals obtained from the input masses.
  • Its feasible set is nonempty and compact, so a finite optimum exists.

To verify the encoding and output sizes explicitly, write each mass as \(\mu_t=a_t/b_t\), where \(b_t\) is a positive integer, and set

\(\displaystyle H=2\prod_{t\in T}b_t.\)

The binary length of \(H\) is \(O(L)\). Each \(H\mu_t\), \(Hs_{cd}\), and \(H/2\) is an integer, so all LP right-hand sides have polynomial encoding length and are computable by exact rational arithmetic.

There is also a direct bound on the exact score’s bit length. Let \(N\leq m\tau\) be the number of variables and scale them by \(y_{t,k}=Hx_{t,k}\). The scaled feasible polytope has integer right-hand sides of absolute value at most \(H\), and constraint coefficients in \(\{-1,0,1\}\).

A linear objective on a nonempty compact polytope has an optimal vertex. At a vertex, choose \(N\) linearly independent active constraints and write them as \(By=b\). By Hadamard’s determinant bound,

\(\displaystyle |\det B|\leq N^{N/2}.\)

Replacing one column by \(b\), whose Euclidean norm is at most \(H\sqrt N\), gives a determinant of absolute value at most \(HN^{N/2}\). Cramer’s rule therefore expresses every coordinate with polynomially many bits. The objective value \(\sum_{t,k}k\,y_{t,k}/H\) consequently also has bit length polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

Thus each exact score \(D_\mu(c)=V(c)\) is computable in polynomial time. Solving one LP for every candidate introduces only a factor of \(m\).

Finally, compare the rational scores exactly by cross multiplication and output

\(\displaystyle \{c\in C:D_\mu(c)=\min_{a\in C}D_\mu(a)\}.\)

These comparisons take polynomial time and preserve every tie.

5. Relation to the cited continuous result

The LP above is the support-sized fractional Dodgson formulation underlying Dodgson Clone. [McCabe-Dansted’s paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1008.1501) describes the weak-majority fractional formulation and its polynomial-time computation.

The proof here supplies the additional strict-infimum argument and verifies polynomial complexity in the explicitly listed support size and binary mass encoding. The requested computation is therefore polynomial-time solvable on every input.
