The continuous mirror question, as graded

Given a task set \(J\), a precedence DAG \(G\), a rational threshold \(q\), and a distribution \(\{(\pi_r,\mu_r)\}_{r=1}^{\tau}\) over preferred task orders, does there exist a permutation \(S\) satisfying \(G\) such that \(\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\mu_r\sum_{j\in J}\max\{0,C_j(S)-C_j(\pi_r)\}\le q\)?

Packaged proof task (what the prover saw — nothing else)

Let \(J\) contain \(m\geq1\) unit-duration tasks. A schedule is a permutation executed on one machine from time \(0\), without idle time; \(C_j(S)\) is the position of task \(j\) in \(S\).
A society is given by its support: \(\tau\geq1\) distinct preferred permutations \(\pi_r\), each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_r\), with \(\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\mu_r=1\). Here \(\tau\) counts supported types, not all possible permutations.
The input also contains a precedence DAG \(G=(J,E)\) and a binary-rational threshold \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\). Preferred permutations need not satisfy \(G\); a feasible output \(S\) must satisfy \(C_a(S)<C_b(S)\) for every \((a,b)\in E\).
Decide whether some feasible \(S\) satisfies \(\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\mu_r\sum_{j\in J}\max\{0,C_j(S)-C_j(\pi_r)\}\leq q\). Let \(L\) be the total input bit length; state running times in \(m,\tau,L\).

Is this decision problem solvable in polynomial time, or is it NP-hard?

For an explicitly listed finite electorate with unweighted total tardiness, optimization is strongly NP-hard even when the imposed precedence graph is a disjoint union of chains.
For that discrete model, if every preferred permutation satisfies the precedence constraints, optimization takes \(O(vm^2+m^3)\) time, where \(v\) is the number of voters.

Modeling choices made when packaging the task

  • Normalized the distribution to total mass one, with strictly positive binary-rational masses and distinct supported permutations.
  • Specified completion times as positions in permutations of unit-duration tasks, executed from time zero without idle time.
  • Specified that preferred permutations may violate the imposed precedence DAG, while the output must respect every edge.
  • Used a nonempty task set and nonempty support, allowed any rational threshold, and defined L as the total input bit length.

Classification

The decision problem is NP-complete, even when the precedence graph is a disjoint union of chains. Encoding the electorate by its distinct supported types preserves the hardness.

Here, binary-encoded rationals mean fractions whose numerators and denominators are encoded in binary. The hardness conclusion also holds if masses and thresholds are required to be dyadic; this is proved below.

1. Exact verification is polynomial

Write the objective as

\( F(S)=\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\mu_r T_r(S),\qquad T_r(S)=\sum_{j\in J}\max\{0,C_j(S)-C_j(\pi_r)\}. \)

A certificate is a permutation \(S\). Its positions allow us to check all precedence edges and compute all \(T_r(S)\). Each \(T_r(S)\) is an integer satisfying

\( 0\leq T_r(S)\leq m(m-1). \)

For exact arithmetic, write \(\mu_r=a_r/b_r\) and \(q=c/d\), with positive denominators, and take

\( D=d\prod_{r=1}^{\tau}b_r,\qquad W_r=a_rD/b_r,\qquad Q=cD/d. \)

The threshold condition is exactly

\( \sum_{r=1}^{\tau}W_rT_r(S)\leq Q. \)

Because the total encoding length is \(L\), the product \(D\) has \(O(L)\) bits. All integers used in this verification have \(O(L+\log(m+1))\) bits.

Set \(B=L+\lceil\log_2(m+1)\rceil\). Using elementary integer arithmetic, a conservative verification bound is

\( O\bigl((\tau m+m^2)B^2\bigr) \)

bit operations, including checking that the certificate is a permutation. This is polynomial in the input length, so the problem belongs to \(\mathrm{NP}\).

2. Polynomial reduction from the listed-electorate problem

We use the threshold form of the stated discrete hardness result:

Given explicitly listed preferred permutations \(\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_v\), a precedence graph consisting of disjoint chains, and an integer \(K\), deciding whether a feasible schedule satisfies

\( T(S):=\sum_{i=1}^{v}\sum_{j\in J}\max\{0,C_j(S)-C_j(\rho_i)\}\leq K \)

is NP-hard. The proof of Proposition 12 explicitly establishes a polynomial reduction between threshold decision problems. Its hypotheses match our source instances: unit tasks, permutation preferences, and imposed chain precedences that preferences need not satisfy. [Durand and Pascual, Proposition 12](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.19197v1#S4.SS2)

Group identical permutations. Let \(\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_\tau\) be the distinct permutations, and let \(n_r\) be the multiplicity of \(\pi_r\). Thus

\( n_r\geq1,\qquad \sum_{r=1}^{\tau}n_r=v,\qquad \tau\leq v. \)

Keep the tasks and precedence graph unchanged, and output

\( \mu_r=n_r/v,\qquad q=K/v. \)

These masses are positive and sum to one. For every schedule \(S\), grouping equal summands gives

\( F(S)=\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\frac{n_r}{v}T_r(S)=\frac{T(S)}{v}. \)

Consequently,

\( F(S)\leq q\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T(S)\leq K. \)

Feasibility is unchanged, so the two decision instances have the same answer.

The construction has polynomial size. It lists at most \(v\) permutations, and each mass requires \(O(\log(v+1))\) bits. Sorting the explicitly listed permutations and counting equal entries also takes polynomial time in the source input length. Crucially, \(\tau\) is the number of permutations actually occurring in the input; the construction never lists all \(m!\) permutations.

This is a polynomial many-one reduction. Together with Section 1, it proves NP-completeness.

If “binary-rational” means dyadic

The same conclusion holds when every rational must have a power-of-two denominator.

Consider a source instance with \(m\geq2\), and put \(H=m(m-1)\). Choose a power of two \(P\) satisfying

\( P>8v\tau H. \)

Round each \(n_r/v\) down to a multiple of \(1/P\), then add the entire rounding deficit to the first mass. Denote the resulting masses by \(\widehat\mu_r\). They are positive because \(P>v\), and they sum exactly to one. Moreover,

\( \sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\left|\widehat\mu_r-\frac{n_r}{v}\right|<\frac{2\tau}{P}. \)

Indeed, each downward rounding error is less than \(1/P\); after assigning the deficit to the first mass, its final error equals the sum of the other rounding errors.

For every schedule, therefore,

\( \left|\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\widehat\mu_rT_r(S)-\frac{T(S)}v\right|<\frac{2\tau H}{P}<\frac1{4v}. \)

Choose the dyadic threshold

\( \widehat q=\frac1P\left\lceil\frac{P(K+1/2)}v\right\rceil. \)

Since \(P>4v\),

\( \frac{K+1/2}{v}\leq\widehat q<\frac{K+3/4}{v}. \)

If \(T(S)\leq K\), its rounded weighted objective is less than \((K+1/4)/v\), hence below \(\widehat q\). If \(T(S)>K\), integrality gives \(T(S)\geq K+1\), so its rounded objective exceeds \((K+3/4)/v\), hence exceeds \(\widehat q\).

Thus this construction preserves the decision answer. The binary length of \(P\) is \(O(\log v+\log\tau+\log(m+1))\), so the construction remains polynomial.

3. The feasible-preferences case remains polynomial

For completeness, suppose every supported permutation satisfies \(G\). There is then an exact algorithm taking

\( O(\tau m^2+m^3) \)

arithmetic operations, or conservatively

\( O\bigl((\tau m^2+m^3)B^2\bigr) \)

bit operations. It uses the support directly, without expanding rational masses into voters.

To prove this, scale the masses to integer weights \(W_r=D\mu_r\), and define the assignment cost of placing task \(j\) at position \(k\) by

\( c_{jk}=\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}W_r\max\{0,k-C_j(\pi_r)\}. \)

These costs take \(O(\tau m^2)\) arithmetic operations to construct. An assignment of tasks to positions has total cost \(DF(S)\).

Choose a topological ordering of \(G\), and let \(h_j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\) be task \(j\)'s rank in it. Set \(M=m^3+1\), and solve the assignment problem with integer costs

\( c'_{jk}=Mc_{jk}-h_jk. \)

The secondary sum \(\sum_jh_jC_j(S)\) lies between \(0\) and \(m^3\). Since primary costs are integers, every minimizer of the modified costs also minimizes the primary assignment cost.

We now show that such a minimizer satisfies every precedence edge. Suppose an edge \((a,b)\) is violated, with \(b\) at position \(x\) and \(a\) at position \(y>x\). Every supported preference has due positions \(d_a<d_b\). For fixed \(x<y\), the function

\( g(d)=\max\{0,y-d\}-\max\{0,x-d\} \)

is nonincreasing in \(d\): it equals \(y-x\) for \(d\leq x\), equals \(y-d\) for \(x<d<y\), and equals zero for \(d\geq y\).

Thus \(g(d_a)\geq g(d_b)\). Swapping \(a\) and \(b\) changes their primary cost for this preference by \(g(d_b)-g(d_a)\leq0\). Summing with positive weights preserves this inequality.

Meanwhile, the swap increases the secondary sum by

\( (h_b-h_a)(y-x)>0, \)

because a topological ordering has \(h_a<h_b\). The modified assignment cost therefore strictly decreases, contradicting optimality. Hence the returned assignment is feasible and minimizes the required objective.

The assignment problem takes \(O(m^3)\) arithmetic operations. All scaled and modified costs have \(O(B)\) bits, giving the stated bit bound.

This polynomial case requires every supported preference to respect the DAG. The unrestricted task does not impose that hypothesis and is NP-complete.
